Afterdienst German
False worship. The fourth part of Religion within the Boundaries of Mere Reason (1793), the most violent, deals with service and false service under the dominion of the good principle, or with religion and priestcraft. Kant lays down there a principle he declares self-evident: whatever, apart from a good way of life, the human being supposes he can still do to become well-pleasing to God is mere religious delusion and false service of God. Prayers, pilgrimages, fasts, rites and sacraments, taken as means of winning God's favour, belong to fetishism, and the Church that lives off them is priestcraft. The essay measures the reach of this text by a Lutheran raised in pietism, which turns almost all actual religious practice into fetishism, and finds in it the name of religion without spirituality: observance, worship that commits the one who performs it to no transformation. It also notes that Kant, by reducing religion to the recognition of our duties as divine commands, sacralized morality, for which Nietzsche will reproach him in The Antichrist as an underhand theology. Allegory
The reading that seeks beneath the letter of a sacred text another meaning, philosophical or moral. Philo of Alexandria, in the 1st century, reads Genesis with the Timaeus: when the two seem to diverge, it is because the letter of Moses hides a meaning that allegory must uncover, and the Logos is its key. The essay speaks of a regime of allegory, earlier than all the regimes of critique: Philo does not criticize religion, he translates it into philosophy, which protects it from the rationalist objection but installs in it a double meaning, reserved for those who know how to read. The method passes to the Christians through Origen, who systematizes it, and reappears in Averroes and Maimonides, for whom the revealed text has an apparent meaning and an inner meaning. Schopenhauer gives its disillusioned version: religions are the allegorical garment of truth, the metaphysics of the masses, and it would be cruel to take away the people's allegory. The Grand Inquisitor is the political culmination of this reading. See also Logos, Taʾwīl, Double truth, Demythologization. Read in the essay
Amor fati Latin, love of fate
In Latin, love of fate. In Nietzsche (The Gay Science § 276, Ecce Homo), the consent that wills what is, wanting nothing else, neither forward nor backward nor in all eternity: not to bear necessity, but to love it. The essay distinguishes it from obedience to fate and from the justification of the world: to love necessity without justifying it is exactly what Ivan Karamazov refuses when he returns his ticket, and what Spinoza accomplishes through the intellectual love of God. It is an exercise that transforms the one who consents, not a submission to an absolute, which makes it a component of the spirituality of exercise. It has its limit: Nietzsche, an exception by attitude and not by position, remains unstable between § 344, which admits where his fire comes from, and Zarathustra's prophetic temptations. The essay's conclusion tries to hold together Spinoza's acceptance and Marx's protest, by consenting to the necessity of the world without consenting to the injustice of men. See also nihilism. Animism
In Edward Tylor (Primitive Culture, 1871), belief in spiritual beings, which he takes to be the minimal definition of religion. Animism is a primitive philosophy: the man of the earliest ages, reasoning about dreams, visions and death, concludes that there exists a soul separable from the body, then peoples the world with spirits; survivals of this stage persist in the higher religions, and anthropology presents itself as a reformer's science. Frazer extends the scheme in The Golden Bough through the succession of magic (the bastard sister of science), religion and science. The essay sees in it the regime of error in its evolutionist form: Tylor and Frazer explain the birth of belief, not its duration. Durkheim objects that a universal and everyday error would be an inexplicable delusion; Wittgenstein, in his remarks on Frazer, that the rite expresses and does not reason, and that Frazer is more savage than his savages (attested response); Malinowski, that magic begins where knowledge stops. Girard takes up all of Frazer's material while reversing its interpretation. What remains established: the universality of belief in invisible beings. Read in the essay
Anschauung und Gefühl German
Intuition and feeling. In On Religion: Speeches to Its Cultured Despisers (1799), Schleiermacher defines the essence of religion against the two reductions of his century: it is neither thinking nor acting, but intuition and feeling, sense and taste for the infinite, “Sinn und Geschmack fürs Unendliche”. Kant had reduced it to morality, rationalist metaphysics to the proofs of God; Schleiermacher places it in the immediate experience of the universe, prior to all dogma and every Church, to the point that a religion without God can be better than a religion with a badly conceived God. The essay sees in it the birth of the regime of experience and the first attempt to leave instituted religion without leaving lived religion. It also marks its structural weakness, which will hold for James, Otto and Bergson: experience proves that one feels, it does not prove what one feels. Hegel will answer that if religion were a feeling of dependence, the dog would be the best Christian; Feuerbach will answer in 1851 that the dependence felt is dependence on nature, and Barth will say in 1927 that Feuerbach is the truth of Schleiermacher. See also Feeling of absolute dependence, Eight regimes of critique, Numinous. Read in the essay
Apocrypha Greek ἀπόκρυφος (apokryphos), hidden
Literally hidden. The word first designates books reserved for initiates, then books that are not read in the assembly and whose origin is judged doubtful. Its meaning varies between confessions: Protestants call apocrypha the Greek books of the Old Testament, Tobit, Judith, Wisdom, Sirach, Maccabees, which Catholics call deuterocanonical and receive; all call apocryphal the gospels, acts and apocalypses excluded from the New Testament, such as the Gospel of Thomas or the Protevangelium of James. A pseudepigraphon is a writing placed under the name of an ancient figure, Enoch or an apostle, a common practice in Antiquity. The apocrypha tell us little about Jesus, but much about the diversity of Christians in the second century and about the origin of popular traditions, from the manger to the parents of Mary. Archetypes and individuation
The two central notions of Jung's psychology after his break with Freud. Archetypes are empty forms of the collective unconscious, filled by the particular representations of each culture, and found with striking constancy in the myths, dreams, visions and delusions of the most distant peoples and ages: the mother, the shadow, the wise old man, and above all the Self, image of psychic totality, which Jung recognizes in Christ, the Buddha, the mandala and the philosopher's stone. Individuation is the process by which the individual enters into relation with this totality; Jung sees in it the psychological equivalent of what religions called salvation, and defines religion, in Psychology and Religion (1938), as the careful observation of the numinosum. The essay follows Buber's objection: psychologism, since God is now only an image of the soul, and gnosis, since the encounter with an external Thou is replaced by knowledge of an inner content. It concludes that Jung makes religion necessary without making it true, and that a religion of the unconscious offers no criterion for refusing the sacrifice an awakened archetype might demand, like Wotan in 1936. See also Numinous, Gnosis, Need and disposition. Read in the essay
Art of writing
Leo Strauss's thesis in Persecution and the Art of Writing (1952): the philosophers who wrote under threat, Maimonides, Averroes, Spinoza and many others, composed their books on two levels, an exoteric teaching in keeping with the opinion of the city and an esoteric teaching reserved for attentive readers, which can be deciphered in contradictions, silences and repetitions. Strauss finds its source in Maimonides, whose seventh cause of contradiction, in the introduction to the Guide for the Perplexed, describes a writing for two publics. The essay judges this theory fragile in its general use, but takes seriously the suspicion it casts on Spinoza: the apparent piety of the Ethics, the piety that made him a God-intoxicated man in the eyes of the Romantics, would in part be an art of writing. It replies that the intellectual love of God is demonstrated there, not suggested, while acknowledging that the Spinozist exception rests on an indemonstrable decision. The theory extends the Averroist division between the elite and the multitude that the essay follows from Averroes to Renan and to Strauss. See also Double truth, Taʾwīl, Intellectual love of God. Ascetic ideal
The subject of the third essay of On the Genealogy of Morality (1887): what do ascetic ideals mean? In the priest, asceticism is the means by which suffering life turns against itself in order to last; it gives suffering a meaning, guilt, and this meaning, however terrible, is better than meaninglessness, for man would rather will nothingness than not will. § 24 to 28 extend the diagnosis to science and atheism: the unconditional will to truth is the most recent and noblest form of the ascetic ideal, and it is Christian morality itself, truthfulness raised into a duty, that triumphed over the Christian God. The essay reads in it both an anticipation of the theories of secularization, Weber and Gauchet rediscovering this self-abolition of the religious by the religious, and the objection Nietzsche addresses in advance to Simone Weil, whose refusal of consolation resembles an asceticism; the essay judges it partly right, while noting that Weil refuses to give misfortune a meaning. See also Will to truth, Genealogy, Disenchantment of the world. Read in the essay
Asylum of ignorance
Asylum ignorantiae. In the appendix to the first part of the Ethics, Spinoza explains the birth of superstition by the finalist prejudice: human beings, who are ignorant of causes and desire what is useful, believe that everything is made for them by gods who govern them, and when an event contradicts this belief, a storm, an earthquake, a disease, they go back from cause to cause until they take refuge in the will of God, that is, the asylum of ignorance. The word thus designates the point where explanation stops to make way for a mystery. The essay makes it the emblem of the regime of error in its most rigorous version: religion as ignorance of causes, a theme d'Holbach will take up in saying that ignorance begets the gods, and that Boyer will rediscover, in another form, by showing how the mind lends intentions where there are only causes. The essay also notes the limit of this explanation: it says why one is mistaken, not why one needs to be mistaken, which Feuerbach, Marx and Freud will seek. See also Deus seu Natura, Eight regimes of critique, Illusion. Read in the essay
Ataraxia Greek
Ἀταραξία, the absence of disturbance. In Epicurus and Lucretius, knowledge of nature is not an end in itself: it is an exercise that delivers from the two fears that spoil life, fear of the gods and fear of death, and procures tranquillity of soul. Explaining lightning, earthquakes and disease by atoms dispels terror; knowing that death is nothing to us, since where it is we no longer are, dispels the other. The essay insists on this spiritual dimension of the regime of error, which the moderns will forget: Epicurus is not an atheist, the gods exist and live in a blessedness that any care would disturb, and the impious man is the one who lends them the opinions of the crowd. This is why the essay says of Onfray, who claims Epicurus and Lucretius, that he inherits from them the regime of error and the hatred of priests, not their piety or their exercise. Ataraxia is the ancestor of the wisdoms of immanence that Part Twelve examines. See also Spiritual exercises, Spirituality of exercise, Eight regimes of critique. Read in the essay
Atheists of the marketplace
The unbelievers who surround the madman of § 125 of The Gay Science (1882), the one who runs into the marketplace with a lantern crying that he is looking for God. They mock him, and it is to them, not to believers, that he announces that we have killed him, you and I. The essay makes them a type: the atheists of the marketplace take the absence of God for a settled matter, one more opinion, whereas the madman knows that it is an event whose consequences have not yet been measured, and which calls for festivals of atonement and sacred games. The essay places among them the new atheists of the early twenty-first century, Dawkins, Harris, Dennett, Hitchens, Sartre in his own way, and those who believe they find in meditation a wisdom without history and without presuppositions: all of them carry shadows they do not see. The figure says, in Nietzschean terms, what the law of refoundations says in Stirnerian terms. See also Death of God, Law of refoundations, Nihilism. Read in the essay
Atheology
Georges Bataille's project, which gathers Inner Experience (1943), Guilty (1944) and On Nietzsche (1945) under the title Somme athéologique: the title parodies the Summa Theologiae of Thomas Aquinas and announces an undertaking of the same scope, but of opposite meaning. Atheology is an inner experience of the sacred without God, a journey to the end of the possible of man, a naked experience that leads thought to non-knowledge, through anguish, laughter, ecstasy and expenditure. Sartre sees in him a new mystic of whom only the corpse can still be touched (attested response, 1943). Michel Onfray takes up the word in 2005 in his Traité d'athéologie, in an entirely different sense, that of a militant critique of the three monotheisms, which the essay calls a reversal. It regards Bataille as a key witness, not as an exception: his sacred of transgression refounds a sacred and confirms Durkheim. See also the sacred. Read in the essay
Attested response and constructed confrontation
The essay's rule of method, announced in the prologue and recalled at each encounter between two authors. An attested response is a debate that took place: one of the two read the other and answered him, in a text that can be quoted, as Origen against Celsus, Pascal against Montaigne, Feuerbach against Stirner, Freud against Rolland, Blumenberg against Löwith. A constructed confrontation is a face-to-face that the essay itself organizes between authors who did not read each other or did not answer each other: Plato and Rousseau, Maimonides and Feuerbach, Bayle and Ivan Karamazov, Wittgenstein and Leibniz. The essay signals the status of each exchange so as not to pass off a reconstruction as a historical fact. The sections entitled “constructed debate” (morality without God, why do people believe, why something rather than nothing) are constructed confrontations for several voices. The site's Responses page lists the seventy-two exchanges, fifty-eight attested and fourteen constructed, with their section. See also Eight regimes of critique. Read in the essay
Aufhebung German, from aufheben
The dialectical movement in Hegel, for which French has no word: aufheben means at once to abolish, to preserve and to raise up. Applied to religion, Aufhebung means that philosophy surpasses religious representation by preserving it in the concept: religion is not refuted, it is comprehended. For Hegel, religion and philosophy have the same content, absolute Spirit; religion thinks it in the form of representation (images, narratives, the Father and the Son), philosophy in the form of the concept. The death of God, in Glauben und Wissen (1802), is a speculative Good Friday followed by a resurrection; Nietzsche will keep the death without the resurrection. The essay judges the argument right and the conclusion wrong: philosophy becomes the higher religion of the wise, and the Averroist division between the elite and the people returns. Kierkegaard answers him that subjectivity is truth (attested response); Barth takes up the word to say that revelation abolishes and fulfils religion. See also death of God. Read in the essay
Axial period
Achsenzeit. In The Origin and Goal of History (1949), Karl Jaspers observes that between roughly 800 and 200 BCE, and above all around 500, comparable spiritual events took place independently of one another in China, India, Iran, Palestine and Greece: Confucius and Lao-tzu, the Upanishads and the Buddha, Zarathustra, the prophets of Israel, the Greek philosophers. Everywhere man becomes conscious of himself and of his limits, raises the question of the Whole and calls received myths into doubt. Jaspers makes it the axis of universal history, in place of the Christian axis of the Incarnation, which holds only for Christians. The essay finds in it what gives Jaspers his place in an inquiry that seeks to move beyond Christianity alone: the critique of religion and the spirituality of exercise have multiple sources, and the history the essay tells, centred by avowal on Christian and post-Christian Europe, is only one of the paths. The notion was taken up by sociologists, from Shmuel Eisenstadt to Robert Bellah, and debated for its chronology. See also Philosophical faith, Encompassing, Relay fable. Read in the essay
Believing lineage
The notion by which Danièle Hervieu-Léger defines religion in La Religion pour mémoire (1993): an ideological, practical and symbolic apparatus through which the consciousness, individual and collective, of belonging to a believing lineage is constituted, maintained, developed and controlled. To believe religiously is to believe in the name of a chain, drawing one's authority from a tradition that goes back to founding witnesses and that one takes on the task of passing on. Modernity is amnesiac: it breaks the chains of memory, and what fades away with the practising believer is religion as a shared self-evidence, Berger's canopy. Two figures take its place: the pilgrim, who composes his own itinerary, and the convert, who chooses his lineage. The essay notes that the convert touches on the criterion, that there are replacement lineages, the Nation, the Revolution, Onfray's Civilization, and that a spirituality without a lineage escapes refoundation but is not passed on: the modern pilgrim has no lineage; he has a library. See also Sacred canopy, Invisible religion, Secularization. Read in the essay
Best of all possible worlds
The thesis of the Theodicy (1710): God, being perfect, chose among all possible worlds the one that contains the most perfection, and the evil it contains, the metaphysical evil of finitude, the physical evil of suffering, the moral evil of sin, is the condition of a greater good in the whole. Leibniz thus answers Bayle, who held the problem of evil to be insoluble by reason, in an attested response. Voltaire turns the formula round after the Lisbon earthquake (1755): Candide (1759) has Pangloss repeat that all is for the best in the best of all possible worlds, and ends on the garden to be cultivated. The essay judges that theodicy accepts the sacrifice of the individual to harmony and asks each person to sacrifice their complaint; it sees in it the distant origin of the revolt of Ivan Karamazov, who refuses harmony out of love for humanity and returns his ticket, in a constructed confrontation. Leibniz's question about the existence of the world nevertheless survives his answer. See also Theodicy, Principle of sufficient reason, Everything is permitted. Read in the essay
Biblical canon Greek κανών (kanôn), rule
The list of books a community recognizes as Scripture. The Greek word designates the mason's rule, then the norm and the catalogue. The Jewish canon, fixed gradually, counts twenty-four books; the Christian canon of the Old Testament varies between confessions, Catholics and Orthodox receiving Greek books that Protestants set aside. The twenty-seven books of the New Testament imposed themselves through liturgical use over roughly two centuries, before being recorded in lists, among them the festal letter of Athanasius in 367, then defined by the Council of Trent in 1546. The criteria invoked, apostolic origin, conformity to the faith, wide reception, antiquity, serve above all to justify an established usage. For a text, being outside the canon means not being read in the assembly, not being without historical value. See also Apocrypha. Read in the essay
Bildungsphilister German
The cultivated philistine. Nietzsche coins the word in the first of his Untimely Meditations, David Strauss, the Confessor and the Writer (1873), against the author of The Old Faith and the New (1872). Strauss, whose Life of Jesus of 1835 had ruined the imposture thesis by showing that the gospel narratives were myths formed without intent to deceive, proposes forty years later a new faith, a piety towards the universe nourished by science, by Beethoven and Haydn, and by common morality. Nietzsche reproaches him not with being too critical but with not being critical enough: he has done away with the Christian God and kept Christian morality intact, without seeing that the one does not stand without the other, and he is content with this half-measure with the good conscience of the man of letters. The essay ranks this attested response among the first applications of the law of refoundations, and links the cultivated philistine with the atheists of the marketplace of The Gay Science: in both cases the absence of God is taken as a settled matter whose consequences have not been measured. See also Atheists of the marketplace, Law of refoundations, Death of God. Read in the essay
Care of the self
Ἐπιμέλεια ἑαυτοῦ, epimeleia heautou. In The Hermeneutics of the Subject, his 1982 Collège de France course, Foucault shows that in Antiquity the Delphic precept know thyself was subordinate to a more fundamental precept, the care of the self: one could know the truth only by taking care of oneself, that is, by transforming oneself. This is the sense of the definition of spirituality the essay borrows from him, the search, the practice, the experience through which the subject carries out on itself the transformations necessary to have access to the truth. The Cartesian moment marks the end of this subordination. Hadot, whom Foucault had read and drawn upon, expressed after Foucault's death both his esteem and his disagreement: the culture of the self, the care of the self in which the subject constitutes itself as a work, seemed to him too aesthetic, a new form of dandyism, whereas the ancient exercises aimed at going beyond oneself towards the Whole. The essay notes that Foucault does not say what the true is nor whether it exceeds the subject, and that is why it takes the definition from him and the description from Hadot. See also Spirituality, Cartesian moment, Spiritual exercises. Read in the essay
Cartesian moment
Foucault's expression in The Hermeneutics of the Subject (lecture of 6 January 1982), which the essay paraphrases, not having found the wording on a reliable page. From Descartes onwards, access to truth comes through knowledge alone: all that is needed is a subject who reasons well and follows a method, and no transformation of his being is required any longer; truth becomes a matter of propositions, and spirituality separates from philosophy. The essay uses this framework to explain why the modern critique of religion has almost always targeted religion as belief, as a set of true or false propositions: it is because, since the Cartesian moment, truth has been a matter of propositions. Spinoza is an exception, the Ethics being a book of geometrical demonstrations whose end is a transformation of life; so is Nietzsche, who constantly asks what truth does to the one who seeks it. The essay itself qualifies the notion, in the Descartes section: the Cartesian moment is not a clean break but a conversion of spirituality into metaphysics. See also Care of the self, Spirituality, Will to truth. Read in the essay
Casing as hard as steel
Stahlhartes Gehäuse, the image that closes The Protestant Ethic and the Spirit of Capitalism (1904-1905, revised in 1920). The Puritan wanted to work in a calling; we are compelled to. Concern for external goods was to lie on the shoulders of the saint like a light cloak, but fate turned the cloak into a casing as hard as steel, and asceticism, leaving the monks' cells for working life, built the cosmos of the modern economy, which now holds without its religious mainspring. For the last men of this development Weber envisages specialists without spirit and sensualists without heart, who imagine they have reached a level of humanity never before attained. The translation as iron cage comes from Talcott Parsons; the essay says casing. It reads this page as the Weberian version of the self-abolition of the religious by the religious: Protestant asceticism produced a world that no longer needs it, as in Nietzsche Christian truthfulness killed the Christian God. See also Disenchantment of the world, Polytheism of values, Sacrifice of the intellect. Read in the essay
Causa sui Latin
Cause of itself. The name metaphysics gives to God when it thinks of him as the ultimate ground of beings: Descartes uses it to say that God draws his existence from his own essence, Spinoza makes it the first definition of the Ethics, before relating it to substance. Heidegger, in the lecture on the onto-theological constitution of metaphysics (Identity and Difference, 1957), sees in it the proper name of the God of the philosophers and shows that such a god has nothing divine about it: to this god man can neither pray nor offer sacrifice; before the causa sui he can neither fall to his knees in awe nor play music and dance. Godless thinking, which must abandon the God of the philosophers, is perhaps closer to the divine god. Marion takes up the analysis to define the conceptual idol, a site, metaphysics, a function, theology in onto-theo-logy, a definition, the causa sui, then turns Heidegger against himself. Becker uses the expression in another sense: the causa sui project is man's effort to be his own cause and to deny death. See also Onto-theology, God of the philosophers, Idol and icon. Christian atheist
The expression by which Michel Onfray, according to a reported formula the essay has not been able to verify, is said to have described André Comte-Sponville, and which he turns into a category, Christian atheism, in the Traité d'athéologie (2005): the atheist who keeps Christian morality, compassion, humility, love of one's neighbour, abandoning only God, is still a pious atheist in Stirner's sense. The essay notes that Spanish criticism has applied the same expression to Onfray, atheology as the discourse of Christian atheism, and that the irony became complete in 2021, when Onfray defended the Latin Mass as liturgical heritage and declared that his world is the one the God of the Christians made possible. Onfray has in turn become a Christian atheist, but differently: Comte-Sponville keeps from Christianity an inner fidelity, a spirituality without a Church; Onfray keeps from it an outward civilization, a Church without spirituality. According to the criterion, the first refounds nothing and the second refounds a collective absolute, Civilization, a contemporary case of Stirner's law and of the figure of the Grand Inquisitor. Ciphers of transcendence
Chiffern. For Karl Jaspers, transcendence is not a supreme being that could be known or denied; it is the Encompassing of all encompassings, and it gives itself only in ciphers: nature, the work of art, the religious symbol, metaphysics itself are ciphers of transcendence, which designate it while withdrawing. What is proper to the cipher is that it refuses knowledge at the very moment it points: it must remain a cipher, and that is why Jaspers reproaches Bultmann, in an attested response of 1954, with translating myths into existential concepts, since myth cannot be translated without loss, and reproaches revelation with turning the cipher into a possessed object. Philosophical faith is the attitude that stands before the ciphers without fixing them into dogmas. The essay ranks Jaspers among the prudent believing exceptions, formulating a reservation about the inflation of ciphers, which ends by saying everything and therefore nothing, and replying to Camus that Jaspers's leap, if it is a philosophical suicide in logic, ends in no obedience. See also Encompassing, Philosophical faith, Limit situations. Read in the essay
Civil religion
In Rousseau (The Social Contract IV, 8, 1762), a purely civil profession of faith, whose articles the sovereign fixes not as dogmas of religion but as sentiments of sociability: the existence of a powerful and beneficent divinity, the life to come, the happiness of the just and the punishment of the wicked, the sanctity of the social contract and the laws, and a single negative dogma, the exclusion of intolerance. Whoever does not believe it may be banished, not as impious but as unsociable; whoever, having acknowledged it, behaves as if not believing it must be punished with death. Rousseau distinguishes it from the religion of man (the Gospel, too detached from the city) and from the religion of the priest (Catholicism, which divides sovereignty). The essay knows of no text that illustrates its criterion more exactly: Rousseau leaves the Church through the heart, with the Profession of Faith of the Savoyard Vicar, and re-enters religion through the city. Robespierre and the cult of the Supreme Being (1794) did not betray this chapter. Strict refoundation; Plato (Laws X) is its original, by constructed confrontation. Read in the essay
Collective effervescence
In Durkheim, the state of exaltation produced by the gathering of a group, in the ceremonies of the Australian Aborigines as in the nights of the French Revolution, from which the sacred is born: the assembled individuals feel a force that surpasses them and refer it to an external being, totem, god, Fatherland or Reason, which is only society itself hypostatized. It is through effervescence that a living cult can be born, and that is why Durkheim reproaches Comte with having wanted to revive a religion artificially: from life itself, and not from a dead past, a living cult can arise; a day will come when our societies will again know hours of creative effervescence, and there are no immortal gospels. The essay sees in it one of the two strongest objections to its thesis, and the most disturbing, since the political religions of the twentieth century are that very effervescence. The essay sets Weber against it: the gods have already returned, but in the plural and without restoring unity; and Malinowski, for whom the effervescence of crowds is often entirely profane. See also The sacred, Collective representations, Secular religions. Read in the essay
Collective representations
The notion on which Durkheim founds the sociology of religion. The Elementary Forms of Religious Life (1912) opens with the thesis that religion is an eminently social thing: religious representations are collective representations that express collective realities, and rites are ways of acting that arise within assembled groups. The very categories of thought, time, space, cause, totality, would have a religious, and therefore social, origin. It follows that there are no false religions: all answer, in their own way, to the given conditions of human existence, and the god is only the figurative expression of society. The essay sees in it Feuerbach transposed from the human species to the group, but with the opposite conclusion, since projection, far from being an error to be dispelled, is the condition of all common life. Bergson readily admits the existence of collective representations deposited in institutions, language and customs, but refuses to make society the sole source of the sacred: it is only one source of it, and the lowest. Covenant Hebrew בְּרִית (berit)
In the Bible, the pact by which God binds himself to a people or to humanity and expects fidelity from it. The narratives give several: with Noah and every living creature, sealed by the bow in the cloud; with Abraham, marked by circumcision; at Sinai with Israel, of which the Decalogue and the Law are the terms; with David, to whom a royal line is promised. Jeremiah announces a new covenant, written on hearts; Christians take up the formula at the Last Supper and call their own Scriptures the New Testament, that is, the new covenant, which makes the Jewish Bible an Old Testament. The essay holds with Sanders that the Judaism of Paul's time was already a religion of the covenant received by grace, not a religion of works, and that Paul does not leave it for another. Death of God German: Gott ist tot
In Nietzsche (The Gay Science, § 108, § 125 and § 343, 1882), not a thesis about the non-existence of God but an event in culture: belief in the Christian God has become unbelievable, and no one has yet measured everything that falls with it, beginning with morality. In § 125, the madman cries it out to the atheists of the marketplace, who laugh: we have killed him, you and I, and what festivals of atonement, what sacred games shall we have to invent? The Buddha's shadow remains for centuries in the cave (§ 108). The formula comes from Hegel, for whom the death of God in Glauben und Wissen (1802) is followed by a speculative resurrection; Nietzsche keeps the death without the resurrection. Heidegger makes it, in The Word of Nietzsche: “God Is Dead” (1943), the completion of metaphysics: Nietzsche completes, he does not leave. The essay retains that what triumphed over the Christian God is, according to On the Genealogy of Morality (III, 27), Christian morality itself, which announces Gauchet, and that the death of God is a task rather than an achievement. See also nihilism, Aufhebung. Read in the essay
Decreation
The metaphysical heart of Simone Weil's thought, in the fragments Gustave Thibon gathered under the title Gravity and Grace (1947). To decreate is to make something created pass into the uncreated; to destroy is to make it pass into nothingness, a guilty substitute for decreation. Deism
Belief in a creator God, architect of the universe and guarantor of morality, established by reason alone, without revelation, dogmas or Churches. The word appears in the sixteenth century and designates in the eighteenth the natural religion of the English and French Enlightenment. It is the position of Voltaire, who fights l'Infâme, superstition, fanaticism and the Church, but keeps a Supreme Being as the foundation of morality and a social restraint: if he did not exist, he would have to be invented, and let the sage proclaim him and kings fear him. Rudolf Bultmann's programme (lecture Neues Testament und Mythologie, 1941): to interpret existentially the message of the New Testament, the kerygma, by freeing it from the mythical world picture that envelops it, the three storeys of the universe, miracles, demons, the end of time, which has become unbelievable for modern man. Double truth
A doctrine attributed to the Latin Averroists of the thirteenth century: a proposition could be true in philosophy and false in theology, or the reverse. Averroes never taught it; the Decisive Treatise (Faṣl al-maqāl) holds on the contrary that truth cannot be contrary to truth, that there is only one truth and two modes of access to it, the apparent meaning for the people and interpretation (taʾwīl) for the men of demonstration. The expression comes from the condemnation pronounced in Paris in 1277 by Bishop Étienne Tempier, who in his prologue reproaches masters of the faculty of arts with saying things true according to philosophy and false according to faith, and from its polemical readers. The essay takes the double truth for a legend, but retains from Averroes a real solution, the sharing of truth between an elite and a multitude, which it judges the most stable of all and finds again in Spinoza, Comte, Renan and Leo Strauss: Averroes does not leave religion and founds nothing, he divides. Eight regimes of critique
The essay's interpretive grid, set out in the prologue. Critiques of religion do not form a continuous tradition: they belong to eight distinct regimes, which do not aim at the same thing. Religion appears in turn as an error to be corrected (Epicurus, Lucretius, Spinoza, Hume, d'Holbach, Feuerbach), a symptom of a world that needs it (Marx), a value whose genealogy is traced (Nietzsche), a component of metaphysics, onto-theology (Heidegger), an illusion commanded by desire (Freud), a social function (Durkheim, Weber), an inheritance whose secularization is disputed (Schmitt, Löwith, Blumenberg, Gauchet, Taylor, Habermas), or an experience that survives dogmas (Schleiermacher, James, Otto, Bergson, Bataille). Ricoeur groups the second, third and fifth under the name of the school of suspicion. The eighth regime obliges the essay to widen religion to include spirituality. These regimes do not add up, they accuse one another: each reproaches the previous one with having remained religious, which the essay calls the law of refoundations. Read in the essay

Ethics of belief English: ethics of belief
The question whether believing is subject to duties. William Clifford (The Ethics of Belief, 1877) maintains, with the example of the shipowner who silences his doubts about his ship, that “it is wrong always, everywhere, and for anyone, to believe anything upon insufficient evidence”. William James (The Will to Believe, 1896) replies that one has the right to believe, at one's own risk, when the option is living, forced and momentous, and reason cannot decide: dupery for dupery, nothing proves that dupery through hope is worse than dupery through fear, and the duty to shun error is not the only one, there is also that of seeking truth. Russell, Mackie and Plantinga extend the debate. The essay combines the two: one has the right to believe where reason leaves the question open, and this right ceases where belief engages the lives of others without their having chosen it, which is the translation of the sacrifice criterion into the order of knowledge, and holds against the Inquisitor as against crusades waged in Clifford's name. Existential security
Existential security, the variable that Pippa Norris and Ronald Inglehart place at the centre of Sacred and Secular (2004): the religiosity of a population depends on the degree to which its members feel protected against illness, poverty, violence and chance. Fetishism

First mover and thinking of thinking
The two determinations of Aristotle's god in Book Λ of the Metaphysics. This god is pure act, and its activity can only be the highest: it thinks itself, and its thinking is thinking of thinking, νόησις νοήσεως (Λ 9, 1074b34). Heteronomy
Literally, receiving one's law from another. Immanence and transcendence
The pair that structures Part Twelve and the quarrel of the two wisdoms. Immanent frame and exclusive humanism
The notions by which Charles Taylor, in A Secular Age (2007), describes what modernity has built, and which the essay paraphrases. The immanent frame is the natural, social and moral order that can be understood entirely without transcendence and that holds up by itself. Invisible religion

Law of refoundations
The name the essay gives to a regularity observed over two centuries: each critique of religion is accused by the next of having remained religious. Stirner reproaches Feuerbach with it, Marx Stirner, Nietzsche all the atheists of his time, Heidegger Nietzsche, Camus Marx, and Onfray, according to a reported formula, Comte-Sponville. The accusation is always the same: you think you have left religion, but you are still in it. It was Stirner who first formulated it, in 1844, in the sentence the essay takes as its guiding thread, “Unsere Atheisten sind fromme Leute”, our atheists are pious people; for this reason the essay also calls it Stirner's law, and notes that Stirner did not escape it himself, since he puts the Ego in the place of God. The full text speaks of a regularity that ends up resembling a law; the assessment in the conclusion draws up its list and shows why it is no accident: the place left empty by God answers a need for meaning, connection and protection that critique does not abolish. Law of the three stages
The doctrine of the Course of Positive Philosophy (1830-1842): the human mind passes through the theological state, in which it explains phenomena by supernatural wills, fetishes, gods or a single God; through the metaphysical state, in which it explains them by abstract entities, Nature, forces, essences; and finally through the positive state, in which it gives up seeking causes in order to establish laws between phenomena. Each science passes through these states at its own pace, and sociology, the last to be born, completes the cycle. The essay stresses what follows, less often read: after the subjective turn linked to Clotilde de Vaux, Comte founds a complete religion of Humanity, with dogma, worship and priesthood, and this foundation is not a late madness but the logical consequence of the three states, since one must regulate and rally, and one destroys only what one replaces. Löwith recognizes in it a history of salvation in three ages, heir to Joachim of Fiore's division of history into the ages of the Father, the Son and the Spirit. Living, forced, momentous option
The three conditions that William James lays down in “The Will to Believe” (1896) for it to be legitimate to believe without sufficient evidence. An option is living when both hypotheses are really possible for the one who chooses; forced when there is no third way and abstaining amounts to choosing; momentous when the stake is unique and irreversible. When an option is of this kind and reason cannot decide, our passional nature not only may but must decide, at our own risk, and James objects to Clifford, for whom believing without evidence is always a fault, that the fear of error is itself a passion: dupery for dupery, what proof is there that dupery through hope is so much worse than dupery through fear? The essay stresses the inner tolerance of this position, which defends faith without asking it of the unbeliever, and combines it with Clifford: one has the right to believe where reason leaves the question open, and this right ends where the belief engages the lives of others without their having chosen it. Man-God and horizontal transcendence
Luc Ferry's notions in L'Homme-Dieu ou le Sens de la vie (1996). Modernity has known a double movement, the humanization of the divine, by which Christianity became ethics and feeling, and the divinization of the human, by which man became the object of sacred values: one no longer dies for God, but for freedom, for human rights, and it does happen. Ferry calls this structure a transcendence within immanence, or horizontal transcendence: the sacred is no longer above, in heaven, but in front, in other human beings and in the horizon of meaning they open. Mimetic desire
In Girard (Deceit, Desire, and the Novel, 1961), desire that imitates the desire of another: one does not desire an object directly, one desires it because a model desires it. Don Quixote wants to be a knight because Amadis was one; Emma Bovary loves through the novels she has read. Minimally counterintuitive concepts
The central notion of Religion Explained (2001), by Pascal Boyer. The human mind has intuitive inference systems about persons, animals, objects and artefacts; a religious concept violates one expectation of one of these categories while respecting all the others: a spirit that has no body but sees, hears and remembers, a statue that listens to prayers, a dead ancestor who gets angry. These minimally counterintuitive concepts are better remembered than ordinary concepts, because they surprise, and better than concepts that are too bizarre, because they remain usable: one can reason about a jealous god. Religion is thus a by-product of faculties that have other functions, neither an adaptation nor a need: science is as unnatural as religion is natural. The essay takes from it that Boyer adds no regime to critique but explains why gods are easy to think, and that he refutes the idea of an empty place of exactly the shape of God; it objects that he explains beliefs, not exercises. Mysterium tremendum Latin
The mystery that makes one tremble. Natural religion
In the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, the religion that reason could establish by itself, without revelation: the existence of a God, author of the world, known through the order of nature, and the duties that follow from it. Need and disposition
The distinction that opens Part Nine, devoted to the causes of belief. To speak of a religious need, as Durkheim does, and as Feuerbach's formulas on desire and Freud's on helplessness incline one to do, is to suppose a constant lack that demands to be filled, and that will be filled by a substitute if religion disappears; to speak of a disposition is to designate something in the human mind that makes gods easy to think and belief probable, without making it a necessity. The essay chooses the second word and asks anthropologists, psychologists, sociologists and the cognitive sciences what the religious disposition owes to fear, to desire, to society, to death and to the very structure of the mind. The constructed debate that closes the part concludes that the disposition is universal in its springs and variable in its intensity: there is a need for meaning, connection and protection, not a need for God, and this is why exits from religion are costly exceptions, which presuppose wisdom and security. Nihilism
In Nietzsche, the devaluation of the highest values: when the true world collapses, nothing seems worth anything any more, and the question “what for?” remains unanswered. The word comes from Turgenev (Fathers and Sons, 1862) and the Russian debate; Dostoevsky stages it in Demons and in the formula, never proverbial in his work, that without God everything would be permitted. A word of Kant's, which Heidegger takes up to name the constitution of Western metaphysics since Plato and Aristotle. Metaphysics asks a double question, that of beings as beings (ontology) and that of the supreme being which grounds all the rest (theology); the two merge in a God who is cause of himself, causa sui. Heidegger concludes, in Identität und Differenz (1957), that this god of the philosophers has nothing divine about it: before the causa sui, man can neither pray nor sacrifice, neither fall on his knees nor make music and dance, and godless thinking is perhaps closer to the divine god. The essay makes it the fourth regime of critique: the death of God as the common completion of metaphysics and Christianity, foreseen by Pascal, who sets the God of Abraham against that of the philosophers, and turned around by Marion, for whom the god of onto-theology is a conceptual idol. Pantheism and acosmism
Two names given to Spinoza's doctrine by his readers. Pantheism identifies God and the world: everything is in God, and God is nothing outside nature; this is how the pantheism controversy, opened by Jacobi in 1785, and the Romantics read the Ethics, some to denounce it as disguised atheism, others to find in it a religion of the universe, like Schleiermacher hailing Spinoza as holy and outcast. Pascal's wager
Fragment 233 of the Pensées in the Brunschvicg edition, the most famous and, according to the essay, the most misread. Pascal addresses a libertine who can neither believe nor decide: since reason cannot settle the matter, one must wager, and the stake is such, an eternity of happiness against a finite life, that wagering for God is the only reasonable choice. Principle of sufficient reason
The great principle, not commonly used, that Leibniz formulates in § 7 of the Principles of Nature and Grace (1714): nothing happens without a sufficient reason, that is, without its being possible for someone who knew things well enough to give a reason why it is so and not otherwise. The Monadology (§ 32) sets it beside the principle of contradiction. Applied to the totality of things, it gives rise to the question the essay places at the centre of its last constructed debate: why is there something rather than nothing, since nothing is simpler and easier than something? Since no contingent thing accounts for the whole series, the ultimate reason of things must lie in a necessary substance, God. The essay follows the fate of this demand: Hume dissolves it, Kant sees in it the abyss of reason, Schelling drives it to despair, Heidegger turns it against Leibniz, Wittgenstein keeps the wonder without the answer. It notes that the demand for reason leads to piety by a back door, and that it is when wonder becomes debt that spirituality becomes religion again. Projection
Feuerbach's thesis (Das Wesen des Christentums, 1841): man places outside himself, in a God, the qualities of his own species (Gattung), reason, will, love, and then kneels before them. The secret of theology is anthropology, and the consciousness of God is the self-consciousness of man. The current of analytic philosophy of religion whose principal figure is Alvin Plantinga. Against Clifford's evidentialism, which demands evidence for every belief, Plantinga observes that this principle does not satisfy its own conditions, being neither self-evident nor demonstrated, and maintains that belief in God can be properly basic, like belief in the existence of other minds: rational without being inferred. Religio, relegere, religare Latin
The two ancient etymologies of the word religion, which the essay turns into two conceptions of the thing. For Cicero (De natura deorum II, 72), religio comes from relegere, to read again, to go over scrupulously: religion is ritual and civic exactness, the correct observance of what concerns the worship of the gods, with neither dogma nor inner faith. The essay reads in them the opposition that runs through the sciences of religion: Durkheim, for whom religion is a collective fact with a Church, stands on Cicero's side; William James, for whom it is the experience of individuals in their solitude, on the side of the solitary mystic. Revolt
The concept of The Rebel (1951). Revolt is the movement by which a man says no to a condition he judges unjust and, by this no, affirms a value he holds to be common to all men; its central formula is a cogito, “Je me révolte, donc nous sommes”, I rebel, therefore we are. Sacred canopy
The Sacred Canopy (1967), the book in which Peter Berger applies the theory of the social construction of reality to religion. Every society is an enterprise of world-building, and this world, which he calls by a Greek word, nomos, is an order of meanings that shields human beings from chaos; the individual torn from it, by bereavement, exile or catastrophe, becomes anomic, worldless. Religion is the sacred canopy stretched over this nomos, which grounds it by relating it to a cosmic order, and its first function is not happiness but meaning, as is shown by theodicy, which makes suffering bearable by explaining it. The canopy holds through plausibility structures: a religion is believed only if one's surroundings take it as self-evident, and modern pluralism, which brings everyone face to face with other beliefs, undermines this self-evidence; a chosen belief does not have the same authority as a world. Sacrifice criterion
The criterion the essay proposes for deciding whether an exit from religion has refounded another. In its final formulation (conclusion), one refounds a religion, in the sense that matters, not when one occupies a place that theology has left empty, but when one installs in that place an absolute in whose name the sacrifice of the individual can be demanded, and criticism of which becomes sacrilege; freely consented self-offering does not fall under it. The criterion does not depend on the presence or absence of God: believers have left religion in the sense in which Barth, Bonhoeffer and Weil denounce it, and atheists have remained in it. Scapegoat mechanism
In René Girard (Violence and the Sacred, 1972), the origin of the sacred. A community ravaged by mimetic crisis, in which each imitates the other's violence and differences are effaced, suddenly recovers its unity by discharging its violence onto a single victim, designated by some arbitrary trait; the returning peace is attributed to the victim itself, which becomes sacred, at once guilty and beneficent, accursed and divine. Myth is the account of the lynching from the point of view of the lynchers; rite is its controlled repetition on a substitute victim, sacrifice; prohibitions prevent the return of the crisis. The mechanism requires misrecognition: lynchers who knew their victim to be innocent would not obtain peace. For Girard, the Bible and the Gospels, which tell the Passion from the point of view of the innocent victim, unveil the mechanism and render it inoperative. The essay keeps the anthropological level, which gives the sacrifice criterion its foundation (to refound is to designate a victim, and the Grand Inquisitor is the priest of the mechanism), and leaves aside the apologetic level: the exit from sacrifice belongs to anyone who refuses the crowd, believer or not. Secular religions
Raymond Aron's expression (L'avenir des religions séculières, 1944, paraphrased by the essay), close to Eric Voegelin's political religions (Die politischen Religionen, 1938), for the doctrines that take the place of vanished faith in people's souls and situate salvation here below, in the future of humanity: communism and Nazism first and foremost, the deified Volk, History as judge. Voegelin gives them a genealogy, gnosis, the immanentization of the eschaton since Joachim of Fiore; Aron keeps to the function. Kelsen objects to Voegelin that his is a theory that proves everything (attested response), and Blumenberg sees in it a second victory over gnosis. Secularization
The passage of institutions, property or concepts from the religious to the profane domain. The word comes from canon law (the religious who returns to the world) and from the treaties of Westphalia (Church property transferred to princes), before coming to designate, in the nineteenth century, the retreat of religion in modern societies. The debate that Part Eleven puts on trial concerns its nature: a transposition of Christian contents, for Schmitt (the concepts of the State) and Löwith (eschatology become philosophy of history); a category of historical injustice, for Blumenberg, who sets against it the reoccupation of empty places by new answers; an exit from religion produced by Christianity itself, for Gauchet; the passage of belief to one option among others, for Taylor; a translation of religious contents, for Habermas. Weber had described its mainspring under the name of disenchantment. What the essay retains from the quarrel is that all of them speak of the institution and none of what the individual lives, and that Norris and Inglehart provide the empirical arbitration: it is not critique that secularizes, it is existential security. Secularized eschatology
Eschatology is the doctrine of the last things, from the Greek eschata: the end of time, judgement, salvation. Karl Löwith's thesis (Meaning in History, 1949) is that the modern philosophies of history, from Voltaire and Condorcet to Hegel, Marx and Comte, are Christian eschatology transposed: history oriented towards a goal, progress, revolution, the positive state, inherits the Jewish and Christian expectation of salvation, and historical materialism is a messianism in the language of political economy; Comte takes up the three ages of Joachim of Fiore. The Greeks, who thought of time as a cycle, had no philosophy of history. Löwith concludes in favour of a Stoic withdrawal, for which Habermas reproaches him in 1963. Blumenberg answers him that identity of function does not prove identity of content (attested response). The essay holds Löwith to be an ambiguous ally: the best historical support for its thesis, who refounds nothing but proposes no way of living; it judges that its criterion owes more to Stirner than to Löwith. Self-assertion

In The Legitimacy of the Modern Age (1966), Hans Blumenberg maintains that modernity owes its legitimacy to no inheritance, Christian or other, but to a self-assertion, and he dates it precisely: it is man's response to the theological absolutism of the late Middle Ages, that of nominalism, which had made God an arbitrary and omnipotent will before which the world no longer offered any guarantee of order. Faced with a hidden and unpredictable God, man had to take charge of himself, to know and master nature in order to find his way in it. Modernity is therefore not a Christianity that has lost its faith, but a response to an internal crisis of Christian theology, and the secularization theorem, which makes progress a transposed eschatology, is a category of historical injustice. The essay holds Blumenberg to be an unexpected ally, whose intellectual hygiene obliges it to restrict its thesis to the sacrifice criterion. Leo Strauss, with no attested dialogue, is wholly opposed to this reading: modernity is for him a break with ancient wisdom, not a legitimate self-assertion. Self-offering
The clause that, in the final formulation of the sacrifice criterion, settles the borderline cases. What refounds a religion is not that a man agrees to die for what he loves, but that an authority demands this devotion of others and treats its critique as sacrilege. Seminal Logos
Λόγος σπερματικός, reason sown. Justin, in the middle of the 2nd century, maintains in his Apologies that the Logos that became incarnate in Christ was always present, in the state of seed, in all human beings: each philosopher spoke well to the extent that he had a share in the seminal Logos, and whatever has been well said among all men belongs to the Christians (2 Apology, 13). Augustine will repeat the gesture when he debates with the Platonists without excluding that other sages thought the same of God, which the essay calls the politeness of a victor. Sigh without a heaven

Sôphronistêrion Greek

Spiritual exercises

Spirituality
In the sense the essay adopts, that of Foucault and Hadot: the search, the practice and the experience through which the subject carries out on itself the transformations necessary to gain access to the truth (The Hermeneutics of the Subject, lecture of 6 January 1982). Spirituality of exercise
The form taken, according to the conclusion, by an exit from religion that does not refound another: not a doctrine but a practice by which the subject transforms himself in order to gain access to the true, in the sense Foucault gives to spirituality and that Hadot describes among the Ancients. The essay finds its most coherent ontology in Spinoza, its most exact historical description in Hadot, its strictest limit in Wittgenstein, and neighbouring forms in Nietzsche, Camus or Murdoch. Three traits distinguish it from a religion in the strong sense: it knows where it comes from, it installs no absolute that demands the sacrifice of others, it is a way of living rather than a system. Yet it keeps, knowingly, an orientation towards a Whole or a Good that it does not possess. The essay recalls that it also has a Christian genealogy, through Augustine's inwardness, and that it presupposes an existential security that only institutions guarantee. Static religion and dynamic religion
In Bergson (The Two Sources of Morality and Religion, 1932), the two religions humanity has produced. Static religion is born of the fabulating function, a defensive reaction of nature against what is depressing in intelligence, the representation of death and of the unforeseen; it is coextensive with our species, and Bergson describes it as a tissue of aberrations that protects society. Society is indeed a source, but the lowest one (attested response to Durkheim). Dynamic religion is that of the mystics, who cross in a leap the distance between the closed soul and the open soul, and whose impetus joins the creative impetus: God is love and the object of love, and he creates creators. Instituted religion is its cooled crystallization. Bergson thus answers, by constructed confrontation, Freud and Nietzsche: the overman exists, he is the mystic. The book ends on mysticism calling for mechanics and on the universe as a machine for making gods. The essay judges that Bergson escapes the deification of man by a narrow margin. Strict refoundation and soft refoundation
The two degrees of the sacrifice criterion. Refoundation in the strict sense installs an absolute in whose name the sacrifice of the individual can be demanded and whose critique becomes a sacrilege: Plato's law, Rousseau's civil religion, Comte's Great Being, the secular religions of the twentieth century, the History of the revolutionaries that Camus denounces, Schmitt's political theology. Soft refoundation contents itself with the need to consecrate: it declares human realities sacred, love, the relation of I and Thou, freedom, without demanding of anyone that he die for them. The essay finds its textbook case in Feuerbach, whom Engels reproached in 1886 with wanting to complete religion rather than abolish it, and its most liberal form in Luc Ferry. The distinction matters because the soft prepares the strict: it is through the need to consecrate that consecration becomes a demand among the successors, and the essay sees in Onfray the case of an author who has taken the step, his Civilization demanding that one defend a liturgy in which one does not believe. Survivals
Survivals. Taʾwīl Arabic
The interpretation that leads a word back to what it truly signifies. In The Decisive Treatise (Faṣl al-maqāl), Averroes lays down that truth cannot be contrary to truth: if a demonstration arrives at a knowledge that the apparent meaning (ẓāhir) of the revealed text contradicts, the text must be interpreted and brought back to its inner meaning (bāṭin) by taʾwīl. The absurd
In Camus's The Myth of Sisyphus (1942), the confrontation between the human call for meaning and the unreasonable silence of the world. The absurd lies in their encounter, and the question it raises is that of suicide. Camus rejects two ways out: suicide, which abolishes one of the terms, and the leap, which abolishes the other by giving the world a meaning it does not have; this is the reproach he addresses to Kierkegaard and Jaspers, guilty of philosophical suicide, the absurd becoming god. The sacred
In Durkheim (The Elementary Forms of Religious Life, 1912), what is set apart and forbidden, as opposed to the profane; religion is a system of beliefs and practices relative to sacred things, which unites into one Church all those who adhere to them. The sacred is the form in which a society represents its own force to itself: the god is only the figurative expression of society, and the totem the hypostasized clan. The last part of The Rebel (1951), and the name Camus gives to a thought of limits. Wonder without debt
The affect that the essay retains, at the end of the constructed debate on Leibniz's question, as the only religious affect that demands nothing. To wonder that there is something rather than nothing is the beginning of philosophy in Plato and Aristotle and, in Wittgenstein, the mystical itself: not how the world is, but that it is.
