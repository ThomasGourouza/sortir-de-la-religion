Jean-Luc Marion (b. 1946), a graduate of the École normale supérieure, a pupil of Derrida, Althusser and Beaufret, a historian of Descartes, professor at the Sorbonne and in Chicago, a member of the Académie française since 2008, a Catholic engaged in the journal *Communio*, has pursued at once a body of work as a historian of metaphysics, a phenomenology of givenness and a theology that seeks to think God outside being. He extends Heidegger and Levinas in French thought about God.

#### The historian of Descartes

*Sur l'ontologie grise de Descartes* (1975), *Sur la théologie blanche de Descartes* (1981) and *Sur le prisme métaphysique de Descartes* (1986) reread Descartes in the light of the Heideggerian question of onto-theology: the creation of the eternal truths lets Descartes escape the univocity of being, but the causa sui, the metaphysical name of God, brings him back to it, and Cartesian metaphysics presents a double figure, the ego as the ground of thought and God as the ground of being. These works renewed Cartesian studies and gave Marion his thesis on the constitution of modern metaphysics.

#### God Without Being

*The Idol and Distance* (1977) and *God Without Being* (1982) distinguish two ways of aiming at the divine. The idol is an invisible mirror: the gaze stops and settles on it, and the god it sees there is made to the measure of that gaze; the icon, on the contrary, lets the invisible pass through the visible and reverses the gaze, so that it is the visible that looks at me. The concepts of God, supreme being, causa sui, moral God, are conceptual idols, and the death of God announced by Nietzsche is the death of these idols; Heidegger, by subjecting God to the question of being and making the divine god the one before whom one can dance, sets up a second idolatry. God is not said first in terms of being, but of love, agape, and of distance, and Marion writes the word God crossed out. The book provokes a debate with the Thomists, and in 1995 Marion acknowledges that the Thomist esse is not that of metaphysics.

#### The phenomenology of givenness

*Reduction and Givenness* (1989), *Being Given* (1997) and *In Excess* (2001) construct a phenomenology that goes further than Husserl's reduction to the object and Heidegger's reduction to being: the reduction to givenness, so much reduction, so much givenness. Every phenomenon gives itself, and some give themselves in excess of what intuition can receive and the concept contain: the saturated phenomena, the event, the idol in the sense of the painting, the flesh, the icon of the face, and, combining them all, revelation, thought as a phenomenological possibility and not as a fact of faith. The subject is no longer constituting but gifted, the one who receives himself from what he receives. Janicaud denounces a theological turn; Marion replies that description does not presuppose faith. *The Erotic Phenomenon* (2003) continues with a meditation on love as the first reduction, and works on Augustine and on faith complete the whole.

#### What the essay assumes

The essay assumes familiarity with the idol and the icon, the conceptual idol with its definition by site, function and causa sui, the reversal of Heidegger and of the Zurich seminar, and the partial retraction before the Thomists; it does not draw on the phenomenology of givenness. It closes the Marion section with an assessment of twentieth-century Christianity, at once a victory and a capitulation, purified faith having renounced knowing, consoling, founding and instituting, so that what remains of it, according to the essay, is only a spirituality that atheists also claim.
