Iris Murdoch (1919-1999), born in Dublin, educated at Oxford, a civil servant at the Treasury during the war and then at the United Nations working with refugees, later at Cambridge, where she met Wittgenstein, a philosophy tutor at Oxford from 1948 to 1963, and the author of twenty-six novels, among them the Booker Prize winner *The Sea, the Sea*, pursued side by side a body of fiction and a moral philosophy which, against the analytic philosophy and the existentialism of her time, puts the Good, attention and vision back at the centre.

#### Against the will-centred subject

Her first book, *Sartre, Romantic Rationalist* (1953), is the first English study of Sartre and already a critique: existentialism makes man a naked will choosing its values in a void. Oxford moral philosophy, Hare, Hampshire, shares this picture in another form, reducing morality to choices and public acts. Murdoch objects that moral life is played out mostly before the choice, in the way we see the world and others, and that this vision is clouded by the fat, relentless ego, which projects its fantasies and its consolations. Moral progress is an effort of attention, a word she borrows from Simone Weil, a just and loving gaze directed upon an individual reality; the example of the mother-in-law who learns to see her daughter-in-law differently, without any act changing, illustrates it. Art, and the great novel in particular, Tolstoy, is the school of this disinterested attention, and the example of the kestrel, glimpsed through the window, in which the self forgets itself, is that of unselfing.

#### The sovereignty of the Good

*The Sovereignty of Good* (1970), three essays, argues that the Good, taken from Plato and the image of the sun in the *Republic*, is the centre of moral life, transcendent, unique, unrepresentable, necessarily real, and that it has taken the place God used to occupy: an object of attention that transforms the one who looks at it, of which it can be said that it judges the world without blessing it, and towards which a prayer without an addressee remains possible. *The Fire and the Sun* (1977) discusses Plato's hostility to artists. *Metaphysics as a Guide to Morals* (1992), the Gifford Lectures, a summa of more than five hundred pages, takes up the whole through Plato, Kant, Schopenhauer, Wittgenstein, Heidegger, Derrida and Buddhism, defending a religion without a personal God, centred on the Good, attention and the death of the self.

#### The novels

From *Under the Net* (1954), a picaresque comedy, to *Jackson's Dilemma* (1995), Murdoch's novels, *The Unicorn*, *The Bell*, *The Black Prince*, *The Sea, the Sea*, stage enchanters, false prophets, contingent loves and characters caught in their fantasies, and give flesh to her philosophy: the real resists the self, and love in them is the difficult discovery that something other than oneself is real. Suffering from Alzheimer's disease, she died in 1999; her husband John Bayley told the story of this end.

#### What the essay assumes

The essay assumes familiarity with the critique of Sartre and of analytic philosophy, the fat ego, attention borrowed from Weil, an attested debt, unselfing, the kestrel, the Good in the place of God, the prayer without an addressee, which it knows from a reference-work entry, and the constructed objection to Comte-Sponville. It classes Murdoch among the exceptions, the position of transcendence without God, an absolute that demands nothing but attention, and notes her weakness, a morality of vision before which Marx would ask what one does.
