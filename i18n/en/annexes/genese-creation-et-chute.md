The first three chapters of Genesis open the Bible and tell of the creation of the world, the forming of the first couple and its disobedience. Few pages have been commented on so much, from Augustine's original sin to human dignity in Habermas.

#### The first narrative: the seven days

Gn 1, 1 to 2, 4a tells how God creates in six days, by his word alone, by separating: light from darkness, the waters above from the waters below, the land from the sea; then he fills these spaces with stars, fish and birds, and land animals. On the sixth day he creates the human being in his image, male and female, and entrusts to it dominion over living things (Gn 1, 27). Each stage is declared good; on the seventh day God rests and hallows the sabbath.

#### The second narrative: the garden

Gn 2, 4b to 25 starts again differently. The Lord God fashions the man, adam, from the dust of the ground, adama, and breathes into him the breath of life; he plants a garden in Eden, with the tree of life and the tree of the knowledge of good and evil, whose fruit he forbids; he forms the animals, which the man names, then the woman, taken from the side of the sleeping man. Tone, vocabulary and the order of creation differ: since the eighteenth century criticism has seen here two sources, Priestly for the first narrative, Yahwist for the second, combined by a redactor.

#### The disobedience and the expulsion

Gn 3 tells how the serpent persuades the woman that the forbidden fruit makes one like God; she eats of it and gives some to the man; their eyes are opened and they discover that they are naked. God questions them, each puts the blame on the other, and the sentences fall: the serpent will crawl, the woman will give birth in pain, the man will toil on a cursed soil and return to dust. God drives them out of the garden, whose entrance cherubim guard. The word sin does not appear in the chapter, and the woman receives the name Eve only at the end.

#### Composition and readings

Most exegetes date the Priestly narrative to the exile or the return, in the sixth or fifth century BCE, and the garden narrative to an older tradition, with no agreement on its date. Judaism reads Gn 3 as the story of a fault and an exile, not of a hereditary corruption: it knows no original sin. Paul, in Rm 5, contrasts Adam, through whom sin and death entered the world, with Christ, through whom grace comes. Augustine, against Pelagius, draws from it the doctrine of original sin transmitted by generation, an expression not found in Genesis. Creation out of nothing is not stated by Gn 1 either, which opens on a watery chaos; it appears in 2 M 7, 28, then among the Fathers, against the eternal matter of the Greeks. Philo, before them, reads the narrative together with Plato's *Timaeus*. The Reformation keeps Augustine; the moderns, from Kant to Ricoeur, read Adam as a symbol of the human condition rather than as an ancestor.

#### What the essay assumes

The essay draws on these chapters from several sides. In the section on Habermas, Gn 1, 27, man created in the image of God, is the example of a religious content that secularization must translate, into dignity and into the prohibition on making a human being the product of another's design. In Philo, Genesis and the *Timaeus* say the same thing, man made in the image of God is made after the Logos, and the Garden of Eden becomes, allegorically, virtue. The constructed debate on existence rather than nothing notes that the Greeks had no idea of absolute nothingness and that creation ex nihilo was needed for the question to take its modern form. Finally, the section on Augustine recalls that *The City of God* argues with the Platonists about the creator God, a legacy that Part Eleven follows into the quarrel over secularization.
