The letters of Paul, written around 50 to 60, are the oldest surviving Christian writings. Addressed to particular communities, they have become the foundation of Christian theology and the object of its greatest quarrels.

#### The corpus

Thirteen letters bear Paul's name. Critical scholarship holds seven to be authentic: the First Letter to the Thessalonians, the oldest, c. 50; Galatians; the two letters to the Corinthians; Philippians; the note to Philemon; Romans, c. 56 to 58. Three are disputed and called deutero-Pauline: Colossians, Ephesians, 2 Thessalonians, perhaps written by disciples. The three pastoral epistles, 1 and 2 Timothy and Titus, addressed to leaders and concerned with Church organization, are held to be late, from the beginning of the second century. The Letter to the Hebrews is no longer attributed to Paul. The letters, dictated to a secretary, are read to the assembly and exchanged between Churches.

#### Romans

The longest and most carefully constructed letter, addressed to a community that Paul did not found. All, Jews and Greeks, are under sin; the justice of God is manifested through faith in Christ, independently of the works of the Law (Rm 3 and 4); Adam and Christ correspond to each other (Rm 5); the divided self does not do the good it wants (Rm 7). Chapters 9 to 11 deal with Israel: Paul affirms that the gifts and the calling of God are irrevocable and that all Israel will be saved. Then comes the exhortation: to offer one's body as a living sacrifice, a reasonable or spiritual worship depending on the translation (Rm 12, 1), then submission to the authorities, which come from God (Rm 13).

#### Corinthians, Galatians, Philippians

The First Letter to the Corinthians deals with the divisions between partisans of Paul, of Apollos and of Cephas, and sets the folly of the cross against the wisdom the Greeks seek and the signs the Jews demand (1 Co 1); then with marriage, with the Lord's supper, of which it transmits the oldest account (1 Co 11), with the body of many members (1 Co 12), with love, which never passes away (1 Co 13), and with the resurrection of the dead, with the received creed and the list of appearances (1 Co 15). Galatians is a letter of anger against those who impose circumcision: Paul's calling, the meeting with Cephas and James, the Law as a pedagogue, neither Jew nor Greek. Philippians, written in prison, is a letter of joy; its chapter 2 quotes a hymn, probably earlier than Paul, in which Christ, being of divine condition, empties himself, takes the condition of a slave even unto death on the cross, and is then exalted: this is kenosis, from the Greek kenôsis, emptying.

#### The great readings

Augustine reads Rm 7 and 9 in the Pelagian controversy and draws from them grace and predestination. Luther finds in Rm 1, 17 justification by faith alone; the Reformation is born of a reading of Romans. Barth, in 1919 and then 1922, breaks with liberal theology through his *Römerbrief*. Nietzsche, in *The Antichrist*, makes Paul the inventor of Christianity, who replaced the practice of Jesus with the cross and the resurrection. Since Sanders (1977), a body of research often called the new perspective places Paul back within the Judaism of his time: justification concerns the admission of the Gentiles, not a religion of performance; philosophers (Taubes, Badiou, Agamben) have reread Paul as a thinker of the universal.

#### What the essay assumes

The essay adopts the consensus on the seven letters, the dating between 50 and 60, the creed of 1 Co 15 and the meeting of Ga 1, 19 as testimonies about Jesus, the folly of the cross of 1 Co 1, 22-23 as the formula of the impossible meeting of Athens and Jerusalem, Sanders's reading against the Lutheran image, and Rm 12, 1 as the text in which bloody sacrifice becomes self-offering. It reads Barth through the *Römerbrief* and the infinite qualitative difference, Nietzsche through paragraph 42 of *The Antichrist*, and Weil through decreation, a God who renounces being everything, which is compared with the hymn of Philippians.
