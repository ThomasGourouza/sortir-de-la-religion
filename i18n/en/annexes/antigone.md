Antigone, daughter of Oedipus and Jocasta, is the heroine of the tragedy that Sophocles had performed around 442 BCE. She buries her brother against the king's prohibition and dies of this act. Since Hegel, the play has become the reference text for the conflict between the law of the State and a higher law, and each age has read its own question in it.

#### The myth and the play

After the exile of Oedipus, his two sons, Eteocles and Polynices, were to reign over Thebes in turn. Eteocles refuses to give up the throne; Polynices returns with a foreign army, and the two brothers kill each other before the gates of the city. Creon, their uncle, now king, has Eteocles buried with honour and orders that the body of Polynices, a traitor to his country, remain without burial, left to the dogs and the birds, on pain of death for whoever buries it. Antigone, despite the pleas of her sister Ismene, scatters a handful of dust over the body. Arrested, she answers Creon that she did not believe his decrees could prevail over the unwritten and unchanging laws of the gods, which are neither of today nor of yesterday. Creon has her shut up alive in a tomb. The seer Tiresias warns him that the gods are offended; when he gives way, it is too late: Antigone has hanged herself, his son Haemon, who loved her, kills himself over her body, and his wife Eurydice kills herself in turn.

#### What is at stake

Burying the dead was in Greece a religious and family duty; to deprive a dead person of burial was to prevent him from reaching the underworld. The conflict therefore sets against each other the city and the family, man and woman, written law and divine law, but also two forms of excess: the rigidity of the king, who confuses public order with his own will, and the obstinacy of a young woman who chooses death.

#### The readings

Hegel, in the *Phenomenology of Spirit*, sees in the play the collision of two equally legitimate powers, the human law of the State and the divine law of the family, each of which is both right and wrong; tragedy is the reconciliation born of their mutual destruction. Kierkegaard, Hölderlin, who translated it, and then Heidegger commented on it. Simone Weil, in 1936, wrote for the workers of a factory at Rosières a presentation of it that makes Antigone the figure of love that refuses to take sides in hatred. Jean Anouilh, in 1944, under the Occupation, gave Creon the arguments of a realist ruler, and the play was read sometimes as a work of resistance, sometimes as an accommodating one. Judith Butler has examined what it says about kinship and mourning.

#### What the essay assumes

The essay assumes familiarity with Antigone's answer to Creon, which it quotes from Sophocles in the box in the Weil section, and with the idea of a self-offering that refounds no religion: Antigone dies for a duty she imposes on no one, and the essay places her on the side of the refusal of the scapegoat.
