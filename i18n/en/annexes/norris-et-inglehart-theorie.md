Pippa Norris (b. 1953), a British political scientist at Harvard, and Ronald Inglehart (1934-2021), an American political scientist at the University of Michigan, founder and long-time director of the World Values Survey, proposed in *Sacred and Secular* (2004) a theory of secularization based on existential security and supported by the largest opinion surveys ever conducted. The essay makes it the empirical arbiter of its ninth part.

#### The World Values Surveys

As early as 1977, in *The Silent Revolution*, Inglehart had formulated a theory of value change: generations raised in prosperity and peace move from materialist values, economic and physical security, to postmaterialist values, self-expression, quality of life, autonomy. To test it, he launched from 1981, as an extension of the European Values Study, the World Values Surveys, repeated in waves in a growing number of countries, nearly a hundred today, on political, moral, family and religious attitudes. The resulting cultural map of the world sets two axes against each other, traditional values against secular-rational values, survival values against self-expression values, and shows that countries shift with development while keeping the imprint of their religious heritage, Protestant, Catholic, Orthodox, Islamic, Confucian.

#### Sacred and Secular

The 2004 book starts from a paradox: secularization is advancing in almost all advanced industrial societies, and yet the world has more believers than ever. The theory of existential security explains it: religiosity answers the need for certainty and protection, and it recedes where illness, poverty and violence are held at bay by development and the welfare state, generation after generation; it persists where existence remains precarious. Since poor and religious countries have higher fertility, the share of believers in humanity increases: this is the demographic paradox. Scandinavia, the most secure, is the most secularized; the United States, rich but unequal and offering little protection, remains religious, which according to the authors refutes the religious market theory of Stark and Finke, for whom competition among churches explains American vitality. *Religion's Sudden Decline* (2021), Inglehart's last book, observes that between 2007 and 2020 religiosity declined in most of the countries studied, the United States included, and attributes this to security, to the fall in fertility, which makes religious norms on sexuality unnecessary, and to individualization.

#### Criticisms

The objections bear on measurement, standardized questions translated into dozens of languages, on causality, security possibly being as much effect as cause, on the exceptions, and on the evolutionist inheritance of modernization theory. The sociologists of the religious market and the proponents of the return of religion, Berger, Casanova, contest its scope.

#### What the essay assumes

The essay assumes familiarity with the world surveys, existential security, the demographic paradox, the comparison between Scandinavia and the United States, and the 2021 book. It makes them the arbiter of the debate on the causes of belief, proving Lucretius, Hume, Freud and Becker right and the Enlightenment wrong, since it is not critique that secularizes but health insurance, and concludes from them that exits from religion presuppose a security that only institutions guarantee.
