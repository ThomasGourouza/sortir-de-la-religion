Origen (c. 185-c. 253), born in Alexandria into a Christian family, the son of a martyr, heads the catechetical school of his city while still young, studies philosophy under Ammonius Saccas, the teacher of Plotinus, then settles in Caesarea in Palestine around 232 after a conflict with his bishop. Tortured during the persecution of Decius, he dies from the effects of his wounds. He is one of the first great theologians and the first scholarly exegete of Christianity.

#### The exegete

His immense body of work is largely lost, because of posthumous condemnations. The *Hexapla* set out in six columns the Hebrew text of the Bible, its transliteration and four Greek translations, in order to establish the text of the Septuagint: it is the first work of biblical textual criticism. His commentaries and homilies cover almost the whole of Scripture. His method distinguishes three senses, corresponding to the body, the soul and the spirit: the literal or historical sense, the moral sense, and the spiritual or mystical sense, which only allegory reaches and which concerns Christ and the Church; certain passages, impossible or unworthy if taken literally, have no literal sense and oblige the reader to look higher. He inherits this method from Philo and Clement and bequeaths it to all ancient and medieval exegesis, which will develop it into four senses.

#### On First Principles

The *Peri Archon*, *On First Principles*, written around 230 and preserved mainly in Rufinus's softened Latin translation, is the first theological summa. It sets out the Trinity, with a Son subordinate to the Father, eternally begotten; the first creation of free and equal intelligences, whose fall, through the cooling of their love, explains the diversity of angels, humans and demons, and the creation of the material world as a place of education; free will, defended against the Gnostics and the astrologers; and the end of all things, when God will be all in all: the apocatastasis, a universal restoration in which all creatures, perhaps even the devil, will return to God, the punishments of the afterlife being medicinal and not eternal. Origen puts forward these theses as hypotheses for inquiry where the rule of faith is silent. *Against Celsus*, around 248, refutes the *True Doctrine* point by point and defends the rationality of faith, the moral superiority of Christians and their civic loyalty, while acknowledging that most of them believe without being able to demonstrate.

#### Origenism and its condemnation

Origen's disciples and continuators, Gregory Thaumaturgus, Pamphilus, Eusebius, then the Cappadocians, who compile an anthology of his texts, the *Philocalia*, make him the chief authority of the Greek East. But the pre-existence of souls, the apocatastasis and subordinationism provoke, from the end of the fourth century, an Origenist controversy, Epiphanius and Jerome against Rufinus, which leads in 543, under Justinian, to the condemnation of propositions attributed to Origen, confirmed by the Council of Constantinople of 553. His influence survives in Evagrius, Maximus the Confessor and Eriugena, and his rehabilitation by twentieth-century theologians, de Lubac, Daniélou, Balthasar, was one of the driving forces of the return to the Fathers.

#### What the essay assumes

The essay assumes familiarity with *Against Celsus* as the oldest attested response, with Origen's position on the faith of the simple and the demonstration of the learned, with the doctrine of the two cities that he sketches before Augustine, and with his Platonism. It retains that Origen opens up spiritual equality and closes off freedom of thought, and that he does not leave religion.
