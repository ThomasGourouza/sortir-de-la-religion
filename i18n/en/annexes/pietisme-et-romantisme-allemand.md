Two German movements, a century apart, shifted religion from doctrine towards feeling, and so prepared both Schleiermacher's regime of experience and Feuerbach's critique: Pietism, a Protestant revival of the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, and Romanticism, the literary and philosophical revolution of the years 1795-1830. Schleiermacher and Nietzsche were sons of pastors; Kant, Hegel and Hölderlin were trained in Pietist schools or seminaries, the Collegium Fridericianum in Königsberg, the Stift in Tübingen.

#### Pietism

Against a Lutheran orthodoxy frozen in dogmatic controversy after the Thirty Years' War, Philipp Jakob Spener proposed in 1675, in the *Pia Desideria*, a reform of the Reformation: Bible reading in small groups, the collegia pietatis, an effective priesthood of the laity, piety of the heart and holy conduct rather than doctrine. August Hermann Francke made Halle, with its orphanage, its schools and its missions, the centre of the movement; Count Zinzendorf founded in 1722 the community of the Moravian Brethren at Herrnhut, a piety of love for the Saviour and of lived conversion, in which Schleiermacher would be brought up and which Wesley would frequent, the Methodist revival bearing its mark. Pietism insists on the new birth, the personal experience of grace, self-examination, the spiritual diary and autobiography, and sanctification. Kant, raised in Königsberg in a severe Pietism, kept its morality and rejected its practices; Lessing, Herder, Hamann and Jacobi, who set a leap of faith against the reason of Spinoza and Kant, were marked by it; Hegel saw in it a religion of feeling without concept, and Nietzsche is, through his family, its rebellious heir.

#### Romanticism

Early Romanticism, that of the circle of Jena and Berlin around the journal *Athenaeum* (1798-1800), Friedrich and August Wilhelm Schlegel, Novalis, Tieck, Schleiermacher, is a revolt against the Enlightenment and against classicism in the name of the infinite, of feeling, of poetry and of individuality; it rediscovers Spinoza, the Middle Ages, mythologies and the East, and dreams of a new mythology and a universal poetry. Schleiermacher's *Speeches on Religion* are its religious manifesto; Novalis's *Christendom or Europe* (1799) idealizes medieval Catholic unity; the *Hymns to the Night* blend love, death and Christ. Later Romanticism, in Heidelberg and then in Berlin, Brentano, Arnim, Görres, Eichendorff, turns towards the people, the nation and Catholicism; several convert, Friedrich Schlegel in 1808, and political Romanticism, which Carl Schmitt would analyse in 1919, serves the Restoration. Schelling gives the movement its philosophy of nature and of art, then its philosophy of mythology and of revelation; Hölderlin, standing apart, its poetry of the absent gods.

#### Legacies

Romanticism founded modern hermeneutics, renewed philology, launched the comparative history of religions, the music and painting of the infinite, and a religiosity without dogma that recurs in Carlyle, Emerson, Renan and as far as the New Age; Feuerbach and Marx came out of it, Kierkegaard fought it within himself, and Weber inherited its pessimism in the face of disenchantment.

#### What the essay assumes

The essay takes as known Pietism as a religion of the heart and as the milieu in which Kant, whom the essay calls a Lutheran of Pietist upbringing, and later Schleiermacher and Nietzsche were formed, the Romantic circle in Berlin where the *Speeches* were born, the rediscovery of Spinoza, whom Novalis called a God-intoxicated man, and the conversion of Friedrich Schlegel. It places in this climate the birth of the regime of experience and the weakness that the essay attributes to it: experience proves that one feels, not what one feels.
