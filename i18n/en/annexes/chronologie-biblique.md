The Bible tells a story that runs from the creation of the world to the first Churches, but it was written in a different order and much later than the events it reports. Distinguishing these two times is the first step of historical reading.

#### Two clocks

The table sets side by side what the books narrate, with the dates historians accept when they can be verified, and the moment when the texts took shape according to most exegetes. Dates in the story earlier than the tenth century BCE correspond to no external evidence: they are the dates the story assumes, not established facts.

| What the story narrates | When, according to the story or to history | When the text takes shape |
|---|---|---|
| Creation, Flood, Babel (Gn 1 to 11) | outside history | Persian period, 6th and 5th centuries BCE, on older traditions |
| Abraham, Isaac, Jacob, Joseph | the story places them around the 2nd millennium; no external trace | cycles gathered between the 8th and the 5th century |
| Exodus from Egypt, Sinai, wilderness | the story, c. 13th century; no archaeological trace | founding composition between the 8th and the 5th century |
| Settlement in Canaan, Judges | 12th and 11th centuries, gradual settlement according to archaeology | Deuteronomistic history, late 7th century, reworked during the exile |
| David and Solomon | c. 1000 and 950; house of David mentioned c. 840 at Tel Dan | same Deuteronomistic history |
| Schism, kingdoms of Israel and Judah | c. 930; fall of Samaria c. 722 | first prophetic collections, Amos, Hosea, Isaiah |
| Capture of Jerusalem, exile in Babylon | 587 to 538 | Jeremiah, Ezekiel, Second Isaiah, first major redactions of the Torah |
| Return, Second Temple, Ezra and Nehemiah | 538, Temple dedicated c. 515, 5th century | Torah essentially fixed, Chronicles, Job, Qoheleth |
| Persecution of Antiochus, Maccabees | 167 to 164 | book of Daniel, c. 165 |
| Jesus of Nazareth | crucified c. 30, under Pontius Pilate | oral traditions, then gospels |
| Paul and the first Churches | conversion c. 33, death c. 64 to 67 | authentic letters, 50 to 60; Acts, c. 80 to 90 |
| Life of Jesus narrated | | Mark c. 70; Matthew and Luke c. 80 to 90; John c. 90 to 100 |
| End of time, heavenly Jerusalem | still to come for the story | Revelation, c. 95; 2 Peter, c. 120 to 150 |

#### What the gap means

Three consequences follow. First, the stories oldest in subject are often the most recent in composition: the creation in the first chapter of Genesis is written after the exile, by priests who had seen the Temple fall. Second, each book also speaks of its own time: Exodus is reread by a deported people awaiting a new return, Daniel speaks of Antiochus under the features of Nebuchadnezzar, the gospels speak of communities separated from the synagogue. Finally, the order of the books in the Bible is not the order of their composition: Paul's letters, placed after the gospels and Acts, are the oldest Christian texts.

Schools sometimes differ by a century on these dates; the gap itself is a matter of consensus, and it says nothing about the religious value of the texts, only about where they speak from.

#### What the essay assumes

The essay works with this gap without presenting it. It reports that Porphyry dated the composition of Daniel to the years 167 to 164 BCE, as modern exegesis does; it makes Spinoza's *Theological-Political Treatise* the book that asks for Scripture to be read according to the history of each book; it takes Paul's seven letters to be earlier than all the gospels and places Mark c. 70, at the threshold of the series. Part Two, which recounts the birth of Christianity, assumes the reader knows that the texts of the New Testament are spread over nearly a century.
