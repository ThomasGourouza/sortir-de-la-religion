Scholasticism is the philosophy and theology taught in the schools and then the universities of the Latin West, from the eleventh to the fourteenth century, by a method built on the reading of authorities, disputed questions and summae. It settled the relation between faith and reason for centuries, and then undid it itself; Arab and Jewish authors played a part in it that the essay recalls in connection with Averroes and Maimonides.

#### Anselm, the translations, the universities

Anselm of Canterbury (1033-1109) gives the programme its motto, faith seeking understanding, and the so-called ontological argument of the *Proslogion*. In the twelfth century, translations made in Toledo and Sicily bring almost the whole of Aristotle into Latin, with his Arab commentators, Avicenna and Averroes, and Maimonides' *Guide for the Perplexed* (c. 1220-1240). The universities of Bologna, Paris and Oxford arise around 1200; in Paris, the faculty of arts teaches philosophy, the faculty of theology sacred doctrine, and the tension between the two shapes the history of the century. Aristotle's physics and metaphysics are banned there in 1210 and in 1215, then tolerated, then made compulsory around 1255.

#### Thomas Aquinas: the two orders

Albert the Great and his pupil Thomas Aquinas (1225-1274), Dominicans, set out to integrate Aristotle into theology. The *Summa Theologiae* distinguishes what natural reason can demonstrate, the preambles of faith, the existence of God by the five ways (motion, efficient cause, the contingent and the necessary, the degrees of perfection, finality), from what revelation alone teaches, the Trinity and the Incarnation, which reason can defend against objections without proving them. One truth, two orders of knowledge, ranked in a hierarchy: this is the solution the essay calls the scholastic regulation of the demand. Thomas calls Maimonides Rabbi Moyses and Averroes the Commentator, while writing against him.

#### Latin Averroism and the condemnations

In the faculty of arts, masters such as Siger of Brabant and Boethius of Dacia teach Aristotle as Averroes reads him: the eternity of the world, the unity of the intellect for all men, the happiness of the philosopher in contemplation. They claim to speak as philosophers, according to the principles of reason, without denying the faith. Thomas attacks them in 1270 in *On the Unity of the Intellect*; the bishop of Paris, Étienne Tempier, condemns thirteen propositions in 1270, then two hundred and nineteen on 7 March 1277, some of which target Thomas himself, who had died three years earlier. The prologue to the condemnation reproaches the masters with maintaining that things are true according to philosophy and not according to faith, as if there were two contrary truths: this is the origin of the legend of the double truth, which Averroes did not teach. Pierre Duhem argued that 1277, by asserting God's omnipotence against Aristotelian necessity, had freed the imagination of physicists.

#### Duns Scotus, Ockham and nominalism

The fourteenth century draws the consequences. John Duns Scotus (d. 1308) places the divine will above the intellect; William of Ockham (c. 1287-1347), a Franciscan prosecuted at Avignon, maintains that universals are only names or concepts, that only individuals exist, and that God, by his absolute power, could have made everything quite otherwise: theology is no longer demonstrated, it is believed, and reason falls back on logic and experience. This via moderna shaped Luther, and Blumenberg sees in it the theological absolutism against which modernity asserts itself.

#### What the essay assumes

The essay mentions the condemnation of 1277, the five ways, the abyss of will that Duns Scotus and Ockham would recognize in Descartes, and nominalism in Blumenberg; it takes as known that scholasticism had regulated the rational demand received from the Greeks, and that the classical age set it free.

#### Key dates

- 1078: Anselm's *Proslogion*; c. 1200: universities of Paris and Oxford.
- 1210, 1215: bans on Aristotle in Paris; c. 1255: Aristotle on the curriculum.
- 1270 and 1277: Tempier's condemnations; 1274: death of Thomas Aquinas.
- 1308: death of Duns Scotus; 1347: death of Ockham.
