Sacrifice runs through the Bible from the first offering of Cain and Abel to the Lamb of the Apocalypse. Following this thread shows how a religion of the Temple becomes a religion without blood sacrifices, and how the word sacrifice changes its meaning along the way.

#### The stories of origins

The first murder follows the first offering: God accepts Abel's, not Cain's, and the story does not say why (Gn 4). Noah, having left the ark, offers burnt offerings, and God promises never again to curse the ground (Gn 8). On Mount Moriah, God asks Abraham for his son, then stays his hand and provides a ram (Gn 22). Exegetes often read this story as the memory of a rejection of child sacrifice, which the prophets and Deuteronomy expressly condemn as a practice of the nations and at times of Israel, around the place called Tophet.

#### The system of Leviticus

Leviticus organizes a complete cult, reserved to the priests and, after the reform of Josiah, to the Temple of Jerusalem alone. The burnt offering burns the whole victim for God; the communion sacrifice divides its meat between the altar, the priests and the offerer; the sin offerings and guilt offerings purify the sanctuary and make good transgressions, often involuntary ones; grain offerings complete the whole. Blood, the seat of life, belongs to God and is not eaten. On the Day of Atonement, Yom Kippur, the high priest enters the Holy of Holies, sprinkles the mercy seat and lays the sins of the people on a goat that is driven out into the desert, to Azazel (Lv 16): this is the origin of the expression scapegoat.

#### The prophetic critique

The prophets do not always demand an end to sacrifices, but they subordinate them to justice. Amos reports a God who hates the feasts and offerings of a people that oppresses the poor; Hosea, a God who desires faithfulness rather than sacrifice, words Jesus takes up twice in Matthew; Micah, a God who asks for neither rams nor the firstborn, but for justice, kindness and humility; a psalm declares that the acceptable sacrifice is a broken spirit. Second Isaiah, finally, describes a servant whose life, offered as a sacrifice for guilt, justifies the many.

#### After the Temple

The destruction of the Temple in 70 puts an end to sacrifices. Rabbinic Judaism replaces them with study, with prayer at the hours of the former sacrifices, and with works of mercy. Christians read the death of Jesus in the vocabulary of the Temple: the Last Supper takes up the blood of the covenant, John presents Jesus as the lamb of God who takes away the sin of the world and has him die at the hour when the Passover lambs are slaughtered; the Epistle to the Hebrews makes this death the single sacrifice that renders all the others useless. Paul, in Rm 12, 1, calls believers to offer their bodies as a living sacrifice, a worship in keeping with reason.

#### Readings

Maimonides explains the sacrifices as a pedagogical concession to a people accustomed to the cults of its time; Hubert and Mauss, in 1899, see in them a mechanism of communication between the sacred and the profane; Girard, from 1972 onwards, a ritual repetition of the founding murder, whose victim's innocence the Bible and the Passion would unveil. Historians stress above all the diversity of practices and the slowness of the transformations.

#### What the essay assumes

The essay makes Rm 12, 1 a turning point: Paul spiritualizes sacrifice instead of abolishing it, an immense advance over the burnt offerings and the birth certificate of a religion that will be able to demand that the individual offer himself entirely. It sets out, in Maimonides, the sacrifices transferred to the name of God rather than abolished, in Girard the scapegoat and the Passion, and concludes, in the assessment of the refoundations, that no exit can avoid refounding unless it refuses the scapegoat. Its criterion, the absolute in whose name the sacrifice of the individual can be demanded, presupposes this biblical history of sacrifice.
