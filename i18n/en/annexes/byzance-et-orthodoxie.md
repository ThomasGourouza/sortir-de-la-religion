The Christianity that Dostoevsky reads is not that of Rome. It comes from Constantinople, the second Rome, and it took on in Russia a form of its own, made of liturgy, icons, monasticism and a relation to the State different from that of the West. Without this history, the monks of *The Brothers Karamazov* and the author's polemic against Catholicism remain obscure.

#### Constantinople and the symphony

Founded by Constantine in 330, Constantinople remains the capital of a Christian Roman Empire until 1453. There the emperor convenes the councils and protects the Church, and the patriarch crowns the emperor: this relationship, called symphony, knows nothing of the separation of the two swords that the Gregorian reform imposes in the West. Greek theology, that of the Cappadocian Fathers, of John of Damascus, of Maximus the Confessor, is contemplative and apophatic; it conceives salvation as the deification of man (*theosis*) and holds rational speculation in suspicion. The iconoclast crisis (726-843) ends in the triumph of images, which Orthodoxy still celebrates. In the 14th century, Gregory Palamas defends, against Latin-influenced theologians, hesychasm, the prayer of the heart of the monks of Athos, and the distinction between the unknowable essence of God and his uncreated energies.

#### The schism and the Slavic mission

The two halves of Christendom drift apart through language, rite, the celibacy of priests, the addition of the *filioque* to the creed and, above all, the primacy that Rome claims. The mutual excommunications of 1054 are only an episode; the sack of Constantinople by the crusaders (1204) makes the break definitive, and the union of Florence (1439) is rejected by the people. In the meantime, the brothers Cyril and Methodius have created an alphabet for the Slavs (863), and Bulgaria, Serbia and then Kievan Rus', with the baptism of Vladimir (988), have received Greek Christianity in a Slavic language.

#### Moscow, the third Rome

After 1453, Russia regards itself as the heir of Byzantium: a monk of Pskov writes c. 1520 that two Romes have fallen, that the third stands and that there will be no fourth. The patriarchate of Moscow is created in 1589. The liturgical reforms of Patriarch Nikon (1653-1667), which bring the Russian books into line with the Greek ones, provoke the Raskol: the Old Believers, led by the archpriest Avvakum, burned in 1682, refuse the new rites and form a dissenting body persecuted until the twentieth century. Peter the Great, in 1721, abolishes the patriarchate and entrusts the Church to a Holy Synod presided over by a lay official, the chief procurator: the Church becomes a department of the State, until 1917.

#### The startsy and Dostoevsky

Nineteenth-century Russia experiences a spiritual revival coming from the monasteries. Paisius Velichkovsky translated the *Philokalia* (1793), a collection of the hesychast Fathers; Seraphim of Sarov (1754-1833) embodies popular sanctity; the monastery of Optina, near Kozelsk, shelters a line of startsy (singular starets), elder monks who receive visitors, hear confessions and give counsel. Gogol, Tolstoy, Soloviev and Dostoevsky go there. Dostoevsky spends two days there in June 1878, after the death of his son Alyosha, and meets the starets Ambrose; the starets Zosima of *The Brothers Karamazov* owes him his features. The opposition between the Slavophiles, for whom Orthodoxy and the peasant commune carry a truth that the West has lost, and the Westernizers nourishes his Russian idea; his distrust of Roman Catholicism, which he accuses of having traded Christ for power, explains why the Grand Inquisitor is a cardinal of Seville.

#### What the essay assumes

The essay names Zosima, Markel and the starets, and reads the Grand Inquisitor as a parable of religious power; Dostoevsky's anti-Catholicism is its context. It takes as known the difference between a State Church without scholasticism, whose theology is negative and monastic, and the Roman Church to which almost all its authors address themselves.
