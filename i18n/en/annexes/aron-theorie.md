Raymond Aron (1905-1983), a normalien and fellow student of Sartre, trained in Germany, where he discovered Weber and phenomenology and watched the rise of Nazism, editor in London of *La France libre* during the war, journalist at *Le Figaro* and then at *L'Express*, professor at the Sorbonne and at the Collège de France, was for forty years the liberal sociologist and commentator of French political life, against the commitments of his contemporaries.

#### The critical philosophy of history

The *Introduction to the Philosophy of History* (1938), a thesis defended before a hostile jury, asks what historical knowledge can achieve: against positivism, which believes in brute facts, and against the philosophies of history that claim to know its meaning, Aron, a reader of Dilthey, Rickert and Weber, shows that history is understood from a present and from questions, that it admits a plurality of legitimate interpretations, and that its total meaning is not given: man is in history without mastering it, and choice, political and moral, remains open. This position, called probabilist, underlies his whole work: there are no laws of history, and prudence is the virtue of action.

#### Secular religions

In two articles in *La France libre* in 1944, *L'avenir des religions séculières*, Aron gives this name to the doctrines that occupy, among his contemporaries, the place left empty by faith and that locate the salvation of humanity in a social order to be built here below: communism and National Socialism, which have their dogmas, their prophets, their liturgies and their inquisitions, and whose disappearance does not follow from the end of the war, since they answer a need. *The Opium of the Intellectuals* (1955) applies the analysis to the French intellectuals of the left, who forgive the proletariat and History with a capital H everything: the myths of the left, of revolution and of the proletariat, the idolatry of history, the cleric who turns worshipper of a secular Church; the end of the ideological age is announced. The title turns Marx around, the opium now being the Marxism of the clerics. The book earned him Sartre's hostility, and the saying that it was better to be wrong with Sartre than right with Aron.

#### The sociologist and the commentator

*Main Currents in Sociological Thought* (1967) presents Montesquieu, Comte, Marx, Tocqueville, Durkheim, Pareto and Weber, and has trained generations of students; the lectures on industrial society, *Eighteen Lectures on Industrial Society*, *La Lutte de classes*, *Democracy and Totalitarianism*, compare regimes on the basis of their common infrastructure; *Peace and War* (1962) founds the theory of international relations; *Clausewitz: Philosopher of War* (1976) and the *Memoirs* (1983) complete the work. Aron was the reader of Weber, the interlocutor of Voegelin, the friend of Kojève and of Jaspers, and the constant critic of Sartre, with whom he was reconciled in 1979.

#### What the essay assumes

The essay takes as known the 1944 definition of secular religions, which it paraphrases, *The Opium of the Intellectuals*, the critical philosophy of history and the opposition to Sartre. It distinguishes secular religion in Aron's functional sense, which it finds indispensable for separating modest reoccupations from sacrificial absolutes, from Voegelin's gnostic genealogy, and holds Kelsen and Blumenberg wrong against the function that Aron describes.
