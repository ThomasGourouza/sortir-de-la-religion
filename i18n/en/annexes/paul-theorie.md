Paul (c. 5-c. 64), a Jew from Tarsus in Cilicia, a Pharisee trained in the Law, a Roman citizen according to Acts, a persecutor of the first disciples of Jesus, is converted around 33 on the road to Damascus and becomes the apostle to the nations. His letters, written roughly between 50 and 60, are the oldest Christian texts, earlier than the Gospels, and his theology has shaped Christianity.

#### The letters

The New Testament contains thirteen letters under his name. Critical scholarship holds seven to be authentic: the first to the Thessalonians, the two to the Corinthians, and those to the Galatians, the Philippians, Philemon and the Romans. The others, Ephesians, Colossians, the second to the Thessalonians and the pastoral letters to Timothy and Titus, are attributed to disciples. The authentic letters respond to concrete situations in communities founded by Paul around the Aegean Sea; they contain the oldest testimonies to the death and resurrection of Jesus, including the received formula of 1 Corinthians 15, and to the Last Supper. The Acts of the Apostles, written by Luke a generation later, recount his missionary journeys, his speech before the Areopagus in Athens and his arrest; his death in Rome under Nero is attested only by tradition.

#### The theology

At the centre stands Christ crucified and risen, foolishness to the Greeks and a stumbling block to the Jews, whose death has reconciled humankind with God and whose return is near: Paul expects the end of time within his own lifetime. Man is justified, that is, made or declared righteous before God, by faith in Christ and not by the works of the Law; the Law of Moses, holy in itself, reveals sin without delivering from it, and converted Gentiles need not submit to it, nor to circumcision, a question that sets him against Peter and James and that the assembly of Jerusalem settles around 49. The baptized person dies and rises with Christ, lives in the Spirit, and the Church is the body of Christ, in which there is neither Jew nor Greek, neither slave nor free, neither male nor female. The letters also contain moral instructions, on marriage, meat sacrificed to idols, obedience to the authorities, and the hymn to charity.

#### The readings

Augustine reads Paul against Pelagius and finds in him grace and original sin; Luther finds in him justification by faith alone and makes the opposition of faith and works the heart of the Reformation; Nietzsche makes him the inventor of Christianity, who betrayed the Gospel of Jesus. Since E. P. Sanders in 1977, the new perspective on Paul has contested the Lutheran reading: first-century Judaism was not a religion of merit, and Paul's problem is not the justification of the anxious individual but the entry of the Gentiles into the people of God. Contemporary philosophers, Taubes, Badiou, Agamben, have reread Paul as a thinker of the universal and of the event.

#### What the essay assumes

The essay assumes familiarity with the list of the seven authentic letters, the testimony of Galatians 1, 19 on James the brother of the Lord, Paul's eschatology as Schweitzer established it, Sanders's new perspective, and the readings of Engels and Nietzsche. It retains from Romans 12, 1 that Paul spiritualizes sacrifice instead of abolishing it, and sees in this the birth certificate of a religion that will be able to demand the whole offering.
