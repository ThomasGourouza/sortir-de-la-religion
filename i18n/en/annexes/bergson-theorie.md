Henri Bergson (1859-1941), a graduate of the École normale supérieure of Jewish origin, a lycée teacher and then professor at the Collège de France, where his lectures drew fashionable Paris, a member of the Académie française, Nobel laureate in literature in 1927 and a diplomat during the Great War, was the most famous philosopher of his time. Drawn towards Catholicism at the end of his life, he refused to convert so as to remain among the persecuted, and died in Paris under the Occupation.

#### Duration and memory

*Time and Free Will* (1889) distinguishes two orders: space, homogeneous and measurable, where intelligence and science cut up and count, and pure duration, the qualitative succession of interpenetrating states of consciousness, which is grasped by intuition. Clock time is space; freedom is the act that issues from the deep self in duration, and determinism rests on a confusion. *Matter and Memory* (1896) applies the distinction to the relation of soul and body: the brain is an organ of action, not a store of images; pure memory is distinct from habit memory, and the past is preserved in itself. *Laughter* (1900) analyses the comic as something mechanical encrusted on the living.

#### The élan vital

*Creative Evolution* (1907) extends duration to life: evolution is neither mechanistic nor finalist, it is the work of an élan vital, a creative impulse that passes through matter and divides into divergent lines, plants, instinct, intelligence. Intelligence is made to act on inert matter and to manufacture tools; it misses life and duration, which intuition, instinct become disinterested and conscious of itself, can grasp again. Metaphysics becomes possible again as the intuition of duration, and the book, which discusses Darwin, Spencer and Kant, criticizes the idea of nothingness as a pseudo-idea: nothing cannot be thought, and the question why there is something rather than nothing is a false problem. Bergson sees God in it as unceasing life, action, freedom.

#### The Two Sources

*The Two Sources of Morality and Religion* (1932), twenty-five years after *Creative Evolution*, distinguishes two sources. Closed morality is social pressure, obligation, which binds the individual to the group and the group against others; static religion, produced by the fabulating function, is a defensive reaction of nature against the dissolving power of intelligence and against the representation of death. Open morality is the call of heroes and saints, which aims at the whole of humanity; dynamic religion is mysticism, whose fulfilled examples are the great Christian mystics, Paul, Teresa, Joan of Arc: they rejoin the élan vital at its source, God is love and the object of love, and the universe is a machine for making gods. The closed society and the open society, and the supplement of soul that mechanism calls for, conclude the book, which answers Durkheim in the name of the second source and discusses Lévy-Bruhl, James and Delacroix.

#### Legacy

Bergsonism marked Péguy, Proust, Maritain, Jankélévitch, Merleau-Ponty, Deleuze, who reread it in the 1960s, and Popper, who borrows the open society from it. It was criticized by Russell, Politzer, Benda and Thomism, and placed on the Index in 1914.

#### What the essay assumes

The essay assumes familiarity with duration, intuition, the élan vital, the critique of nothingness, the fabulating function, the two moralities and the two religions, and the machine for making gods. It reports Bergson's attested response to Durkheim and his constructed responses to Freud and to Nietzsche, the superman is the mystic, and judges that Bergson escapes the divinization of man by a narrow margin, at the price of a metaphysics of life.
