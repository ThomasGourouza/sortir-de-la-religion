Pascal Boyer (b. 1951), a French anthropologist trained in Paris and Cambridge, who did fieldwork among the Fang of Cameroon, professor at Washington University in St. Louis, is one of the founders of the cognitive science of religion. His 2001 book, *Religion Explained*, translated into French as *Et l'homme créa les dieux*, is its most widely read exposition.

#### From anthropology to cognition

Boyer's early work deals with oral tradition and transmission: why do some representations spread better than others? In the line of Dan Sperber's epidemiology of representations, he answers that transmission is selective and that the constraints of the human mind filter what is retained and communicated. *The Naturalness of Religious Ideas* (1994) applies this approach to religious concepts and formulates the thesis of violations of intuitive expectations.

#### Religion Explained

The 2001 book rejects the classical explanations, religion consoles, it explains the world, it founds society, not because they are false but because they do not say why religion takes the forms it takes. The human mind is equipped with specialized inference systems, products of evolution, which handle effortlessly physical objects, living things, persons, cooperation, contamination. Religious concepts are minimally counterintuitive concepts: they violate one expectation of an ontological category, a person without a body, an object that listens, and respect all the others, which makes them memorable and usable. Gods and spirits are, moreover, supernatural agents endowed with full strategic information, that is, they know everything that matters to social interactions, who cheated, who lied, and that is why they are relevant to morality, ritual and cooperation, not because they found morality, but because moral intuition pre-exists and recruits them. Rituals exploit the systems of precaution against contamination; religious specialists form guilds that defend their monopoly; theological doctrines, learned explicitly, differ from the intuitive representations that believers actually use, which Justin Barrett calls theological correctness, a notion Boyer takes up, as opposed to actual religion. Religion is thus a by-product of faculties that have other functions, and neither an adaptation nor a need; it is natural in the sense that it is easy to think, and science, which demands that intuitions be corrected, is unnatural.

#### Later work and debates

*Minds Make Societies* (2018) extends the method to social institutions as a whole. Boyer has been challenged by the adaptationists, for whom religion was selected for cooperation, by anthropologists, who reproach him for neglecting history and texts, and by philosophers, who ask whether explaining the causes of a belief says anything about its truth, the genetic fallacy, which Dennett sometimes forgets and which Boyer, according to the essay, avoids better.

#### What the essay assumes

The essay takes as known inference systems, minimally counterintuitive concepts, agents with strategic information, the by-product and the phrase about science being unnatural. It holds that Boyer adds no new regime of critique but explains why gods are easy to think, that he refutes the idea of an empty place of the exact shape of God, and objects to him, besides the genetic fallacy, that he explains beliefs and not exercises.
