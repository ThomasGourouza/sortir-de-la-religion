Greek mythology is not a sacred book but an open set of narratives about the gods, the heroes and the origin of the world, handed down by poets, vase painters, festivals and sanctuaries, and constantly reinvented for more than a millennium. It has no single author, no canonical version, no clergy charged with guarding its meaning, and that is what allows the philosophers, from the sixth century BCE onwards, to discuss it, correct it or interpret it.

#### The sources

The oldest texts are the Homeric poems, the *Iliad* and the *Odyssey*, fixed around the eighth century BCE, and the poems of Hesiod, the *Theogony* and the *Works and Days*, slightly later. Then come the Homeric hymns, the lyric poetry of Pindar, then the Athenian tragedians of the fifth century, Aeschylus, Sophocles and Euripides, who take up the heroic legends to stage them before the city. In the Hellenistic and Roman period, mythographers compile the versions: the *Library* attributed to Apollodorus, Ovid's *Metamorphoses*, Pausanias's notes on local cults. A large part of what is told today comes from these late compilations, and the mythology Europe received is often Ovid's.

#### The divine generations

Hesiod's *Theogony* recounts the birth of the world and of the gods as a succession of generations and acts of violence. In the beginning is Chaos, then come Gaia, the Earth, and Eros. Gaia gives birth to Ouranos, the Sky, who holds their children back in her womb; their son Cronos mutilates him and takes power. Cronos in turn devours his children, until Rhea gives him a stone in place of Zeus and saves him; Zeus overthrows his father and the Titans at the end of a long war. The reign of Zeus closes the series: he shares out the world with his brothers, the sky for himself, the sea for Poseidon, the underworld for Hades, and founds a lasting order based on justice and the distribution of honours.

#### The Olympians and the heroes

The twelve Olympians form the divine family that dwells on Olympus: Zeus and Hera, Poseidon, Demeter, Athena, Apollo, Artemis, Ares, Aphrodite, Hephaestus, Hermes, and Hestia or Dionysus depending on the lists. These gods have human form, passions, rivalries and loves, but they are immortal and far more powerful than men; they do not create the world, they are a part of it. Below them, the heroes, often born of a god and a mortal woman, perform exploits, found cities and receive a cult at their tomb: Heracles, Theseus, Perseus, Achilles, Oedipus.

#### Myth and logos

The Greek word *muthos* first means speech, narrative, before gradually being opposed to *logos*, argued discourse. Historians have shown that this opposition is itself a construction of the Greek philosophers and historians, who classed under the name of myth what they wanted to hold at a distance. Paul Veyne raised the question of belief: the Greeks believed in their myths in various modes, ranging from literal adherence to learned interpretation, without these ways of believing excluding one another.

#### What the essay assumes

The essay assumes familiarity with the gods of Homer and Hesiod, whom Plato judges unworthy and whom Aristotle says were handed down to the ancients in the form of myth, as well as the reign of Zeus that several literary boxes evoke. It also assumes the distinction between myth and argued discourse, on which the first part and Blumenberg's work on myth rest.
