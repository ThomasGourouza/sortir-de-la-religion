Heracles, whom the Romans call Hercules, is the most popular of the Greek heroes: son of Zeus and of the mortal Alcmene, pursued by the hatred of Hera, he performs twelve labours, dies in the flames and rises among the gods. A hero of strength, he has also become, among the philosophers, a moral figure of effort and virtue.

#### The birth and the labours

Zeus, having taken the appearance of Amphitryon, lies with his wife Alcmene; Hera, jealous, sends two serpents against the newborn, who strangles them in his cradle. Later, driven mad by the goddess, Heracles kills his wife and his children; to atone for this crime, the oracle of Delphi orders him to serve King Eurystheus, who imposes twelve labours on him. He strangles the Nemean lion, whose skin he then wears, kills the Lernaean hydra with its regrowing heads, captures the Ceryneian hind and the Erymanthian boar, cleans the Augean stables in a single day by diverting two rivers, drives off the birds of Lake Stymphalus, captures the Cretan bull and the mares of Diomedes, takes the girdle of the queen of the Amazons, the cattle of Geryon, the golden apples of the Hesperides, and finally brings back from the underworld the dog Cerberus. Other exploits are added, among them the deliverance of Prometheus.

#### The death and the apotheosis

His last wife, Deianira, believing she will win back his love, sends him a tunic soaked in the blood of the centaur Nessus, which burns him beyond all remedy. Heracles has a pyre built on Mount Oeta and has himself burned alive on it; he is then carried up to Olympus, reconciled with Hera, and marries Hebe, youth. A hero become a god, he received a double cult, as a mortal and as an immortal.

#### The choice of Heracles

The sophist Prodicus, whose story Xenophon reports, imagines the young Heracles at the parting of two ways: two women approach him, Vice, who promises an easy life full of pleasures, and Virtue, who promises efforts and lasting glory; he chooses the second. The Cynics, then the Stoics, made Heracles the model of the sage who faces trials and makes himself free through effort; Seneca devotes two tragedies to him. The Fathers of the Church discussed the resemblances between the apotheosis of the hero, son of a god and a mortal woman, and the story of Christ, and the Renaissance often depicted the choice of Heracles as a moral allegory.

#### What the essay assumes

The essay mentions Hercules in the box in the Malinowski section, in connection with La Fontaine's fable in which the carter stuck in the mud calls on him and is told in reply that he must act first. The Stoic hero of effort also sheds light on the spiritual exercises that Pierre Hadot rediscovers in ancient philosophy.
