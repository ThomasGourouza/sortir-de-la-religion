Jean-Jacques Rousseau (1712-1778), citizen of Geneva, self-taught, musician, contributor to the *Encyclopédie* and then adversary of the philosophes, author of the most widely read novel of the century, *Julie, or the New Heloise*, persecuted after 1762 and wandering until his death, thought about man, society, education and religion as a single whole, and his books nourished the Revolution as well as Romanticism.

#### Nature and history

The *Discourse on the Sciences and Arts* (1750) maintains against its century that the progress of enlightenment has corrupted morals. The *Discourse on the Origin of Inequality* (1755) goes back to a hypothetical state of nature in which man, solitary, healthy and limited, is neither good nor wicked, moved by self-love and pity; his perfectibility, property and the division of labour give rise to amour-propre, comparison, inequality and finally an iniquitous contract that legalizes the domination of the rich. Man is born good, it is society that depraves him: the thesis replaces original sin with a history, and Rousseau defends it against the Archbishop of Paris in the *Letter to Christophe de Beaumont*.

#### The contract and the general will

*The Social Contract* (1762) seeks a form of association in which each, uniting with all, obeys only himself. The solution is the total alienation of each to the community, which creates a sovereign body politic whose general will, distinct from the will of all, always aims at the common good; law is the expression of this will, the people alone are sovereign and the government is merely an executor. Whoever refuses to obey the general will shall be compelled to do so, which means, according to the famous formula, that he will be forced to be free. The last chapter, On Civil Religion, distinguishes the religion of man, Christian and pure, but which detaches one from the world, the religion of the citizen, national, and that of the priest, which divides; it prescribes a civil profession of faith whose dogmas, God, the life to come, the happiness of the just, the punishment of the wicked, the sanctity of the contract, and a single negative dogma, intolerance, are required of every citizen on pain of banishment, and of death for anyone who, having acknowledged them, behaves as if he did not believe them.

#### Education and the religion of the heart

*Emile* (1762) follows the education of a child according to nature, far from books and prejudices, up to manhood. Book IV contains the Profession of Faith of the Savoyard Vicar: a priest sets out a natural religion founded not on reasoning but on inner feeling, conscience, a divine instinct, the order of the world felt rather than demonstrated, and rejects both the materialism of the philosophes and the authority of revelations, which place so many men between God and the believer. The book is condemned in Paris and in Geneva. The *Confessions* and the *Reveries of the Solitary Walker* invent modern autobiography and the sentiment of existence.

#### What the essay assumes

The essay takes for granted the state of nature, perfectibility, the general will, the chapter on civil religion, which it regards as the text that most exactly illustrates its criterion, the Profession of Faith, taken up by Kant, and the letter to Voltaire of 1756 on the citizen's catechism. It ranks Rousseau among the strict refoundations, as one who left the Church through the heart and re-entered religion through the city, and sets him against Plato in a constructed confrontation.
