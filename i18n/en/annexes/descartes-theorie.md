René Descartes (1596-1650), educated at the Jesuit college of La Flèche, soldier, traveller, settled in the United Provinces from 1628 to 1649, who died in Stockholm at the court of Queen Christina, is regarded as the founder of modern philosophy. A mathematician, the inventor of analytic geometry, and a physicist, he sought to refound the whole of knowledge on a certain foundation.

#### Method and doubt

The *Discourse on the Method* (1637), a preface to three scientific essays, recounts in the first person the formation of a mind which, disappointed by letters and by the schools, resolves to accept as true only what it knows evidently, and sets down four rules: evidence, division, order, enumeration. The *Meditations on First Philosophy* (1641, in Latin), followed by the objections of Hobbes, Arnauld and Gassendi and by Descartes's replies, put the method to work. The first Meditation pushes doubt to the end: the senses deceive, dreaming cannot be told apart from waking, and an evil genius might deceive the subject even in mathematics. The second discovers the fixed point: whoever doubts thinks, and whoever thinks is, he is a thing that thinks; the cogito, formulated in the *Discourse* as I think, therefore I am, is the first certainty, grasped by the mind alone and not by the senses. The analysis of the piece of wax shows that bodies are known by the understanding and not by the imagination.

#### God, the world, man

The third Meditation proves God from the idea of the infinite: the subject finds in himself the idea of a perfect and infinite being, which cannot come from himself, a finite and doubting being, and whose cause must have as much reality as the idea contains; God therefore exists, and he is no deceiver. The fifth adds the ontological proof, existence belonging to the essence of the perfect being as the sum of its angles belongs to the triangle. God then guarantees the truth of what the mind conceives clearly and distinctly, and science becomes possible; error comes from the misuse of the will, which is infinite and judges beyond what the understanding conceives. The sixth Meditation establishes the existence of bodies and the real distinction between the soul, whose essence is thought, and the body, whose essence is extension: dualism, with the problem of their union, which Descartes locates in the pineal gland and treats in *The Passions of the Soul* (1649). The letters to Mersenne of 1630 add a singular thesis: the eternal truths, mathematics included, were freely created by God, who could have made them otherwise. Cartesian physics is mechanistic: matter is extension, animals are machines, and the world is explained by figures and motions.

#### Reception

The *Meditations* were placed on the Index in 1663; Cartesianism prevailed in the universities from the late seventeenth century, and the dispute over the cogito, circle or intuition, over the proof from the idea of the infinite and over the creation of the eternal truths has never ceased. Pascal reproaches Descartes for a useless and uncertain God, reduced to a flick of the finger; Spinoza starts from him; Leibniz objects that the possibility of God must first be proved; Kant dismantles his proofs; Husserl claims him as a forerunner; Heidegger sees in the Cartesian subject the beginning of modern metaphysics; Foucault places there the Cartesian moment, when truth ceases to require a transformation of the subject.

#### What the essay assumes

The essay assumes the reader knows the order of the *Meditations*, the cogito, the two proofs of God, the function of God as guarantor of science, the creation of the eternal truths and dualism. It retains the dedication to the Sorbonne, which argues from the social usefulness of belief, Pascal's response, Leibniz's attested response, and the difficulty Descartes poses for Foucault's Cartesian moment, since his contemplation of God remains an exercise. Descartes makes God a component of reason, which Kant will dismantle.
