The New Testament brings together twenty-seven books written in Greek between about 50 and 150, which the churches gradually recognized as Scripture. Their order in the Bible is not the order in which they were written, and almost none bears the name of its real author.

#### Four genres, twenty-seven books

Four Gospels, Matthew, Mark, Luke and John, tell the life, death and resurrection of Jesus; the Acts of the Apostles, the sequel to Luke; twenty-one letters, thirteen under the name of Paul, an anonymous one to the Hebrews, seven called catholic; and finally the Revelation of John. The word gospel, good news, first designates the message, then the books.

#### The order of composition

The oldest texts are the letters of Paul, written between about 50 and 60: seven are held to be authentic by almost all specialists (Romans, the two to the Corinthians, Galatians, Philippians, the first to the Thessalonians, Philemon); the others are thought to come from disciples. Mark is the oldest Gospel, written c. 70; Matthew and Luke c. 80 to 90; John c. 90 to 100. The Pastoral Epistles, the second letter of Peter and a few others reach down to the beginning or the middle of the second century.

#### Authors and attributions

The Gospels are anonymous; the titles according to Matthew, according to Mark were added in the second century. The traditional attributions go back to Papias of Hierapolis, c. 120 to 130, who says that Mark put Peter's recollections into writing and that Matthew composed the sayings in the Hebrew language, then to Irenaeus of Lyon, c. 180, who names the four evangelists. Critical scholarship judges these testimonies late and partly incompatible with the texts, since Matthew is written in Greek and depends on Mark.

#### Language, manuscripts, variants

The New Testament is written in koine, the common Greek of the eastern Mediterranean. The oldest witness is a papyrus fragment of the Gospel of John, P52, usually dated to around 125 to 150; the first complete manuscripts, the Sinaiticus and the Vaticanus, date from the fourth century. The thousands of Greek manuscripts present countless variants, which textual criticism compares in order to reconstruct the oldest text. Two famous passages are missing from the best witnesses: the longer ending of Mark and the episode of the woman taken in adultery in John 8.

#### The synoptic problem

Matthew, Mark and Luke are called synoptic because they can be read in parallel columns: they share the order of episodes and often the same words, whereas John differs. Explaining these agreements and divergences is the synoptic problem. The dominant solution since the nineteenth century is the two-source hypothesis: Mark was used by Matthew and by Luke; these two also drew on a collection of sayings of Jesus now lost, designated by the letter Q; each finally has material of its own, such as the infancy narratives. The priority of Mark is very widely accepted.

#### What the Gospels are

The Gospels are not biographies in the modern sense but narratives of faith, composed forty to seventy years after the events, from oral traditions. Historians use them with criteria: embarrassment, that is, what Christians would not have invented, such as the baptism by John or the crucifixion; multiple attestation; coherence with the Judaism of the time. An article in these annexes is devoted to this exegesis.

#### What the essay assumes

The essay states that Paul's seven authentic letters, written between 50 and 60, are the oldest Christian texts, earlier than all the Gospels; for the existence of Jesus it relies on Paul, on the elements of the Gospels that embarrassed the first Christians, on Josephus and on Tacitus; it sets out the notion of myth in Strauss, a narrative produced without intent to deceive by the faith of a community, and Bauer's thesis that the Gospels are the conscious literary work of one author, the evangelist Mark, on whom the others depend. This last point assumes familiarity with the priority of Mark.
