The name apologists is given to the Christian writers of the second century who, in treatises addressed to the emperors or to the educated public, defend Christianity against the accusations aimed at it and present it as the true philosophy. Justin, who died a martyr in Rome around 165, is the most important of them, and the first to think through the relation of Christianity to Greek reason.

#### Justin

Born at Flavia Neapolis in Samaria, into a pagan family, Justin relates at the beginning of the *Dialogue with Trypho* his journey through the schools, Stoic, Peripatetic, Pythagorean, Platonist, up to his meeting with an old man who reveals to him the prophets and Christ. He keeps the philosopher's cloak and opens a school in Rome. His *First Apology*, addressed around 153 to Antoninus Pius, refutes the accusations of atheism, immorality and subversion, describes the baptism and eucharist of the Christians, and maintains that Christ is the Logos, the divine Reason, in which all men have a share: those who lived according to the Logos, Socrates, Heraclitus, were Christians before Christ. The *Second Apology* develops the doctrine of the seminal Logos, and asserts that everything that has been well said belongs to the Christians. The *Dialogue with Trypho*, a two-day discussion with a learned Jew, argues from the prophecies that Jesus is the Messiah and that the Law is abolished, and contains the first formulations of Christian anti-Jewish polemic. Justin also takes over from the Alexandrian Jews, Aristobulus and Philo, the idea that Plato had read Moses, the theft of the Greeks. Denounced, he is tried by the prefect of Rome and beheaded with six companions.

#### The other apologists

Aristides of Athens, around 125, is the earliest. Tatian, a disciple of Justin, writes an *Address to the Greeks* of radical hostility to pagan culture. Athenagoras of Athens, around 177, addresses to Marcus Aurelius a *Plea* that refutes with philosophical method the three accusations of atheism, Thyestean banquets and incest. Theophilus of Antioch, the *Epistle to Diognetus* and Melito of Sardis complete the Greek series. In Carthage, Tertullian (c. 155-c. 220), jurist and rhetorician, writes around 197 an *Apology* in the tone of a courtroom plea and, in the *De praescriptione*, opposes Athens to Jerusalem and rejects philosophy as the mother of heresies, while reasoning as a Stoic. Clement of Alexandria (c. 150-c. 215), at the opposite pole, makes Greek philosophy a preparation for the Gospel given by God to the Greeks as the Law was given to the Jews, and describes the true gnostic, the perfect Christian, in Platonic terms; his pupil Origen will continue along this path.

#### What is at stake

The apologists respond to a legal situation, the condemnation of Christians for the name itself, and to an intellectual situation, the contempt of the educated. Their response commits Christianity to the path of a rational theology: the Logos of John becomes the Logos of the philosophers, the God of the Bible the transcendent God of Middle Platonism, and faith a philosophy. Celsus, around 178, will answer them that this philosophy is a plagiarism.

#### What the essay assumes

The essay takes as known the account of Justin's conversion, the doctrine of the seminal Logos, with its double face of openness and annexation, the *Dialogue with Trypho*, the theft of the Greeks, Tertullian's saying on Athens and Jerusalem, and Justin's martyrdom, of which it says that it is Rome that demands the sacrifice.
