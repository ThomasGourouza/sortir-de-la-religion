The Young Hegelians, or Hegelian left, are the group of German writers who, between Hegel's death in 1831 and the revolution of 1848, drew from his philosophy a radical critique of religion and then of the State, and from whom Feuerbach, Stirner, Marx and Engels emerged. The essay devotes its Part Six to them, and it is among them that the law of refoundations is stated for the first time.

#### The split in the school

Hegel had held religion to be true in its content and philosophy to be its completed form; the question whether this sublation preserved or dissolved Christianity divides his pupils. David Friedrich Strauss, whose *Life of Jesus* (1835) causes a scandal, introduces in 1837 the distinction, borrowed from the French Assembly, between a right, which holds philosophy to be the ally of faith, Göschel, Gabler, a centre, Rosenkranz, and a left, which concludes that they are incompatible: Strauss himself, then Bruno Bauer, who moved from the right to the left, Arnold Ruge, Ludwig Feuerbach, Moses Hess, Max Stirner, Edgar Bauer, Karl Marx, Friedrich Engels. Ruge founds in 1838 the *Hallische Jahrbücher*, then the *Deutsche Jahrbücher*, the organ of the movement, banned in 1843; the *Rheinische Zeitung* of Cologne, which Marx edits in 1842-1843, and the *Deutsch-Französische Jahrbücher*, which Ruge and Marx publish in Paris in 1844, carry it on.

#### From religion to politics

The movement follows a logic: the critique of religion is the premise of all critique, Marx says in 1844. Strauss dissolves the Gospels into myths and transfers the predicates of Christ to humanity; Bauer makes the Gospels works of self-consciousness and attacks the Christian State; Feuerbach, in 1841, discovers that theology is anthropology and that man has projected his own essence into God; Hess transposes the critique to money, the God of bourgeois society; Stirner, in 1844, turns the critique against the critics, Feuerbach's Man being a ghost just as God is; Marx and Engels, in *The Holy Family* (1845) and *The German Ideology* (1845-1846), settle their accounts with Saint Bruno and Saint Max and move from the critique of ideas to that of the relations of production. The accession of Frederick William IV in 1840, who summons Schelling to Berlin against Hegelian pantheism and drives the Young Hegelians out of the universities, makes the critique of religion the obligatory form of political critique in a State that calls itself Christian.

#### Legacy

The group scatters after 1848: Bauer turns to conservatism, Ruge to national liberalism, Feuerbach to retirement in Bruckberg, Marx and Engels to socialism, Stirner to oblivion; Kierkegaard, who read Feuerbach and Strauss, and Nietzsche, who replied to Strauss and read Feuerbach in his youth, inherit their critique. Löwith wrote their history in *From Hegel to Nietzsche*, and recent scholarship, Breckman, Moggach, has rehabilitated Bauer and Ruge as political theorists.

#### What the essay assumes

The essay takes as known the split in the Hegelian school, the sequence Strauss, Bauer, Feuerbach, Stirner, Marx, the *Deutsch-Französische Jahrbücher*, and the role of Frederick William IV and Schelling; the history Löwith wrote of them is not cited there. In this sequence it reads the statement of the law of refoundations, each critic accusing the previous one of having remained religious, and sees in Stirner the one who formulated it without escaping it.
