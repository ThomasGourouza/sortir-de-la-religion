Luc Ferry (b. 1951), agrégé in philosophy and in political science, professor at Caen and then at Paris VII, chair of the Conseil national des programmes, Minister of National Education from 2002 to 2004, columnist and best-selling author, has built since the 1980s a philosophy of Kantian inspiration that he calls transcendental humanism, and has devoted several books to what becomes of the meaning of life after religion.

#### Humanism against the thought of 68

With Alain Renaut, Ferry published in 1985 *La Pensée 68*, an attack on Foucault, Derrida, Bourdieu, Lacan and Althusser, accused of antihumanism, of dissolving the subject and of contempt for democracy; there followed *Système et critique*, *Heidegger et les Modernes* (1988) and a *Philosophie politique* in three volumes, which defend, in the line of Kant and Fichte, a free subject, capable of tearing itself away from nature and history, the foundation of human rights. *Homo Aestheticus* (1990) traces the invention of taste and of the modern individual; *Le Nouvel Ordre écologique* (1992) criticizes deep ecology as an antihumanism that sacralizes nature. The constant thesis is that of a transcendence within immanence: values are neither founded in God nor reducible to nature and society; they impose themselves on consciousness as coming from elsewhere, and human freedom is this capacity to exceed everything that conditions it.

#### The Man-God

*L'Homme-Dieu ou le Sens de la vie* (1996) describes a double movement of modernity: the humanization of the divine, by which Christianity became ethics and love, and the divinization of the human, by which man has become the object of the sacred; people now die for freedom, for their loved ones, for human rights, and these devotions, which reason does not command, bear witness to a horizontal transcendence, in other human beings. The sacred has not disappeared, it has moved, and post-traditional identity must take on this meaning without tradition. *Qu'est-ce qu'une vie réussie ?* (2002) and *Apprendre à vivre* (2006), a history of philosophy for everyone, make philosophy a doctrine of salvation without God, which answers the question of death otherwise than religions do. *La Révolution de l'amour* (2010) sees in the love match and the modern family the source of a new sacredness and of a politics. Ferry has held dialogues with Comte-Sponville in *La Sagesse des Modernes* (1998), Spinoza against Kant, and with Gauchet in *Le Religieux après la religion* (2004).

#### Criticisms

Agnès Antoine asked whether the title Man-God was not naive or perverse, Serge Cantin whether a transcendence lodged in consciousness still deserved the name; opponents of *La Pensée 68* reproached the authors with conflating different philosophies.

#### What the essay assumes

The essay takes for granted transcendental humanism, the double movement of *L'Homme-Dieu*, horizontal transcendence, post-traditional identity, philosophy as a doctrine of salvation without God, the attested criticisms by Antoine and Cantin, and the dialogues with Comte-Sponville and Gauchet, the latter not read. It concludes that this is a soft refoundation, consecrating without demanding, the most liberal form of refoundation, and opposes Ferry's transcendence without God to Comte-Sponville's acceptance without transcendence.
