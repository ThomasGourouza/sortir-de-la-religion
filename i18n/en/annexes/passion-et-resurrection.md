The four Gospels converge in their final part: a week in Jerusalem, an arrest at night, a trial, a crucifixion, a tomb found empty. It is the oldest core of the Christian tradition and the point at which history and faith part most clearly.

#### The week

Jesus enters Jerusalem before Passover, riding a donkey, acclaimed by pilgrims. He drives the merchants out of the Temple. On the Thursday evening he takes a last meal with the Twelve, the Last Supper: in the Synoptic Gospels, he breaks the bread and passes the cup, identifying them with his body and his blood, a gesture that Paul already reports around 55 (1 Co 11, 23-25); John puts the washing of the feet in its place. In the garden of Gethsemane he prays in anguish while the disciples sleep; Judas arrives with a troop and points him out with a kiss. The disciples flee; Peter denies knowing him three times. Before the Sanhedrin, Jesus is questioned about the Temple and his messianic claim, then taken in the morning to Pontius Pilate, prefect of Judaea. Pilate offers to release a prisoner, the crowd demands Barabbas. Jesus is scourged, then crucified at Golgotha between two robbers, under an inscription that calls him king of the Jews. The words on the cross differ: Mark and Matthew keep only the cry of Psalm 22 about being forsaken by God, Luke the forgiveness of the executioners, John the completion. Joseph of Arimathea lays the body in a tomb closed with a stone.

#### The Easter accounts

On Sunday at dawn some women, Mary Magdalene at their head, find the stone rolled away and the tomb empty; one or two messengers announce to them that Jesus has risen. Mark stops there, on their flight and their silence. Matthew places the appearances in Galilee, Luke in Jerusalem, with the disciples at Emmaus, John in the garden and then before Thomas. The witnesses, the places and the duration do not agree; the four accounts agree on the empty tomb, on the women as the first witnesses and on a presence that the disciples do not at first recognize.

#### What history retains

Historians, whatever their convictions, hold it as established that Jesus was crucified in Jerusalem on the orders of Pontius Pilate, around 30, during a Passover festival. Tacitus reports it around 116, Josephus around 93, and crucifixion, a punishment for slaves and a mark of infamy, is not one of those facts that a community invents for its Messiah. The cross is a Roman punishment; the Gospels, written after the break with the synagogue, shift the responsibility towards the Jewish authorities and the crowd. The resurrection belongs to another order: the historical method can record a tomb found empty, experiences of appearances and the birth of a faith, without being able to pronounce on their cause. The oldest testimony is not a Gospel but a formula that Paul says he received and that he passes on around 55: Christ died for our sins according to the Scriptures, he was buried, he was raised on the third day, he appeared to Cephas, then to the Twelve (1 Co 15, 3-5).

#### What the essay assumes

The essay retains, in the section on the existence of Jesus, the crucifixion under Pontius Pilate as the consensus of historians, attested by Tacitus, who gives Pilate an inaccurate title, and by Josephus, and it takes the formula of 1 Co 15 as the oldest testimony to the content of the faith. In Hegel, it reads in *Faith and Knowledge* (1802) the sentence on the death of God himself, which Hegel will connect with the Lutheran hymn only in his lectures, and the speculative Good Friday as a death of God followed by a resurrection in the concept. In the section on Dostoevsky, Holbein's dead Christ (1521-1522), a copy of which hangs in Rogozhin's house, is the painting before which Prince Myshkin says, in *The Idiot*, that one could lose one's faith. In Girard, the Passion is a unanimous lynching, in which even the disciples fail, but told by those who know the victim to be innocent. In Nietzsche, the essay quotes the sentence from *The Antichrist* about the only Christian, who died on the cross.
