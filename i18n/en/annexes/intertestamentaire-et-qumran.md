Between the last books of the Hebrew Bible and the first Christian writings stretch nearly four centuries that tradition calls intertestamental. Today they are seen as the matrix from which rabbinic Judaism and Christianity emerge.

#### The period

Alexander conquers Judaea in 332 BCE. The country passes to the Ptolemies and then, around 200, to the Seleucids. In 167, Antiochus IV Epiphanes forbids Jewish practices and installs a pagan cult in the Temple; the revolt led by Mattathias and his sons, the Maccabees, leads to the purification of the Temple in 164, and then to independence under the Hasmoneans. In 63, Pompey enters Jerusalem; Rome installs Herod the Great (37 to 4 BCE), then Roman prefects govern Judaea, among them Pontius Pilate (26 to 36). The revolt of 66 ends with the destruction of the Temple in 70.

#### The books

The books of the Maccabees, Tobit, Judith, the Wisdom of Solomon, Sirach and Baruch are received in the Septuagint and in the Catholic and Orthodox canon, where they are called deuterocanonical, but not in the Hebrew Bible nor among Protestants. Other writings, called pseudepigrapha because they are attributed to ancient figures, enter no canon except in Ethiopia: the Book of Enoch, which tells of the fall of the angels, the patriarch's heavenly journeys and the judgement by a Son of Man; Jubilees; the Testaments of the Twelve Patriarchs; the Psalms of Solomon, which await a messiah son of David.

#### The currents

The historian Flavius Josephus, at the end of the first century, distinguishes three schools that he compares to philosophies: the Pharisees, masters of the oral tradition, who believe in the resurrection; the Sadducees, a priestly aristocracy bound to the Temple, who accept only the written Law and deny the resurrection; the Essenes, a community of shared goods and purity living apart. He adds a fourth philosophy, that of the Zealots, who acknowledge no master but God.

#### Qumran

In 1947, a Bedouin shepherd finds scrolls in a cave near Qumran, on the shore of the Dead Sea; eleven caves will yield some nine hundred manuscripts. About a quarter are biblical texts, among them a complete scroll of Isaiah, a thousand years older than the Hebrew manuscripts known until then; the rest comprises writings already known, such as Enoch, and texts specific to one community: the Community Rule, the Damascus Document, the Hymns, commentaries that read the prophets as the announcement of their own time and of a persecuted Teacher of Righteousness. Most specialists identify this community with Josephus's Essenes, a hypothesis still debated. The manuscripts show the plurality of ancient Judaism, the expectation of two messiahs, a priest and a king, and a biblical text still fluid before its fixing by the Masoretes. They mention neither Jesus nor the Christians.

#### Apocalyptic expectation and the Septuagint

From the Book of Daniel to Enoch and Qumran, a worldview called apocalyptic takes shape: the present age is evil and dominated by hostile powers; God will soon intervene to judge; the dead will rise; a heavenly envoy or a messiah will establish the reign of God. John the Baptist, Jesus and Paul speak this language, and historians see in it the matrix of Christianity. In parallel, the diaspora of Egypt translates the Bible into Greek in Alexandria from the third century onwards: the Septuagint is the Bible of Philo, of Paul and of the first Christians.

#### What the essay assumes

The essay presents Philo as a Jew of Alexandria who reads the Bible in the Septuagint and comments on it by allegory, without having heard of Jesus; it recalls that Paul calls himself a Jew of the elite, more advanced in Judaism than his contemporaries, and places him, with Sanders, within the Judaism of his time; it adopts, with Schweitzer, Sanders, Meier, Fredriksen and Ehrman, the image of Jesus as an apocalyptic prophet awaiting the imminent end of the world. These three points assume familiarity with the Greek diaspora, the currents of Judaism and eschatological expectation.
