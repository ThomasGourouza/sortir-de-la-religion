The last book of the New Testament, the Revelation of John is also the strangest: a series of coded visions announcing the fall of an empire and the descent of a heavenly city. Its Greek title, apokalupsis, means unveiling, and it is from this book that the word took on its sense of catastrophe.

#### A genre and an author

Apocalyptic is a Jewish genre born in the second century BCE, with the book of Daniel and the books of Enoch: a seer receives visions, an angel interprets them, empires are beasts, numbers have a meaning, and the present crisis is the prelude to a divine intervention. The author names himself John, exiled on Patmos, and presents himself as a prophet, not as an apostle. Justin and Irenaeus identify him with the apostle, the son of Zebedee; as early as the third century, Dionysius of Alexandria distinguishes the seer from the evangelist on grounds of style, and most critics follow him. The most commonly accepted date is c. 95, under Domitian, on the testimony of Irenaeus; some place it under Nero or Vespasian.

#### The structure

The book opens with seven letters to the Churches of Asia, from Ephesus to Laodicea (Ap 2 and 3). Then the seer is carried before the throne of God; only the slain Lamb is worthy to open the sealed book (Ap 4 and 5). Three series of seven plagues follow one another: the seals, the first four of which release the horsemen, the trumpets, the bowls. At the centre, a woman clothed with the sun gives birth to a child whom the dragon seeks to devour (Ap 12); a beast rises from the sea, another from the earth, and the number of the beast, 666, corresponds in Hebrew letters to Nero Caesar according to the most common reading (Ap 13). Babylon the great, a harlot seated on seven hills, falls (Ap 17 and 18). The rider named the Word of God wins the battle; Satan is bound for a thousand years while the risen martyrs reign with Christ (Ap 20), the passage from which millenarianism comes; there follow the judgement before the book of life, then a new heaven and a new earth, and the heavenly Jerusalem coming down from God, without a temple, where every tear is wiped away (Ap 21 and 22).

#### Aim and reception

The book consoles Churches exposed to the imperial cult and to local persecutions, and denounces Rome under the name of Babylon; it calls for the endurance of the martyrs, not for armed revolt. Its reception was difficult in the East, where Eusebius ranks it among the disputed books; it was accepted early in the West. Augustine, in book XX of *The City of God*, reads the thousand years as the present time of the Church and puts an end to the literal millenarianism that Papias, Justin and Irenaeus had professed. Luther, in 1522, denies it apostolic character; Calvin did not comment on it.

#### Political and philosophical legacy

Millenarianism revives in the Middle Ages: Joachim of Fiore, who died in 1202, divides history into three ages, of the Father, of the Son and of the Spirit, and announces for the last a spiritual Church; the Franciscan Spirituals, the Taborites, Thomas Müntzer and the Anabaptists of Münster (1534-1535) await the reign of the saints on earth, movements that Norman Cohn traced in *The Pursuit of the Millennium* (1957). Hence the twentieth-century debate on the secularization of eschatology: Löwith and Voegelin see in the philosophies of history the end of time transposed into history; Blumenberg replies that the apocalyptic end breaks in from outside, whereas modern progress is immanent.

#### What the essay assumes

The essay uses the words eschatology, eschaton, end of time and imminent expectation without describing the book they come from: in Löwith, the philosophy of history is Christian eschatology minus God; in Voegelin, the genealogy runs through Joachim; in Blumenberg, three conditions are lacking for speaking of a transfer. In the section on the existence of Jesus, it retains from Schweitzer a Jesus who is an apocalyptic prophet awaiting the imminent end of the world, and in Paul an expectation of Christ's return within the lifetime of his readers.
