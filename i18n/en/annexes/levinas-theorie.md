Emmanuel Levinas (1906-1995), born in Kaunas in Lithuania into a Jewish family, a student in Strasbourg and then in Freiburg under Husserl and Heidegger, whose phenomenology he introduced into France as early as 1930, a prisoner of war in a stalag while his family in Lithuania was massacred, director of the École normale israélite orientale, professor at Poitiers, Nanterre and the Sorbonne, made ethics first philosophy and the relation to the other the place where God comes to mind.

#### From phenomenology to ethics

The first books, *On Escape* (1935), *Existence and Existents* (1947), *Time and the Other* (1948), start from the experience of anonymous being, the there is, a nocturnal rustling from which the subject tears itself away by positing itself, and seek in time, fecundity and the other what delivers it from the solitude of being. Levinas breaks with Heidegger, whose ontology he judges a philosophy of power and of the neuter, without forgetting what he owes him, and in 1961 publishes *Totality and Infinity*: Western philosophy has been a thought of totality, which reduces the other to the same; the idea of the infinite, which Descartes found in himself without being able to produce it, designates on the contrary a relation in which the other overflows every idea, and this relation is the face-to-face with the face of the other, who speaks to me, commands me not to kill and institutes me as responsible prior to any freedom. Ethics precedes ontology, it is an optics, and justice is born with the third party.

#### Otherwise than Being

*Otherwise than Being or Beyond Essence* (1974) radicalizes the break with the language of being, which Derrida had reproached *Totality and Infinity* for retaining: subjectivity is described there as sensibility, exposure, substitution, the self as hostage of the other, responsible to the point of persecution, and the Saying as prior to the Said. God is neither a being nor a theme: he comes to mind in the trace left by the face, in illeity, and *Of God Who Comes to Mind* (1982) gathers the texts in which Levinas thinks a transcendence that cannot be thematized without being lost. Phenomenology is thus led to its limits, and Janicaud saw in this the beginning of the theological turn.

#### The Jewish writings

Levinas kept his confessional writings separate from his philosophy. *Difficult Freedom* (1963) gathers essays on Judaism as a religion for adults, which has disenchanted the world and refuses the numinous sacred as a violence, on Heidegger, Gagarin and pagan rootedness, on the Torah loved more than God, and on Simone Weil, an adversary. The Talmudic readings, given every year from 1960 at the colloquia of French-speaking Jewish intellectuals and published in several volumes, comment on pages of the Talmud as a philosopher would, and show the rabbinic source of Levinasian ethics. Maimonides, Rosenzweig and Buber, with whom he debates reciprocity, are his Jewish interlocutors.

#### What the essay assumes

The essay takes as known the face, ethics as an optics, substitution and the hostage, the religion for adults, the attested response to Heidegger and what the essay reads as a response to Otto, the dialogue with Buber and the Torah loved more than God. It sets Levinasian asymmetry against Feuerbach's symmetry, hesitates over the verdict and leans towards a believing exception, Levinas not falling under the criterion, though the author is not entirely sure of it.
