Bronisław Malinowski (1884-1942), a Pole from Kraków, doctor in physics and philosophy, converted to anthropology by reading *The Golden Bough*, a student at the London School of Economics, is in New Guinea when the war of 1914 breaks out; an Austrian subject, he is allowed to remain and spends two years in the Trobriand Islands. He brings back from them a method, extended fieldwork, and a theory, functionalism, which founded British social anthropology.

#### Fieldwork and the Trobriands

*Argonauts of the Western Pacific* (1922) describes the kula, a circuit of ceremonial exchanges of necklaces and armbands between the islands, and formulates in its introduction the method of participant observation: living among the natives, speaking their language, recording the imponderabilia of everyday life, and grasping the native's point of view on his world. There follow *Sex and Repression in Savage Society* (1927), which disputes the universality of the Oedipus complex in a matrilineal society, *The Sexual Life of Savages* (1929), *Coral Gardens and Their Magic* (1935), on agriculture and magic, and essays on law and kinship. The private diary published in 1967, with its admissions of boredom and hostility, has troubled the image of the founder. Malinowski teaches in London from 1922 to 1938, trains Evans-Pritchard, Firth, Leach and Fortes, and dies at Yale.

#### Functionalism

Against the evolutionism of Tylor and Frazer, which classified customs as survivals of a reconstructed past, and against diffusionism, Malinowski holds that every institution must be understood through its function in the present culture, and that every culture answers, through its institutions, the biological and derived needs of human beings: food, reproduction, safety, and the needs for cohesion, education and meaning. Radcliffe-Brown will propose a structural version of it, based on the maintenance of the social structure rather than on individual needs.

#### Magic, science and religion

The essay of 1925 argues that the Trobrianders possess empirical and rational knowledge, a science, for everything that technique controls; magic comes in only where uncertainty and danger prevail, in deep-sea fishing and not in the lagoon, and it serves to ritualize optimism, to give confidence when knowledge is no longer enough. Religion, distinct from magic in that it is not a means to an end, answers the crises of life, and first of all death: funeral rites affirm the desire to live against decomposition and reintegrate the survivors; its source is individual before it is collective, against Durkheim. Myth (1926) is neither an explanation nor an allegory but a charter, myth as the charter of institutions, which founds and justifies rights, rites and rules by linking them to a sacred past.

#### What the essay assumes

The essay assumes familiarity with the Trobriand fieldwork, the distinction between the lagoon and the open sea, the formula of ritualizing optimism, death as the source of religion, and myth as a charter. It regards Malinowski as solid, infers from him that belief recedes with uncertainty and not with knowledge, which Norris and Inglehart measure, and notes his responses to Tylor, to Frazer and to Durkheim.
