Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz (1646-1716), born in Leipzig, jurist, diplomat, librarian and historian of the dukes of Hanover, inventor of the infinitesimal calculus at the same time as Newton, founder of the Berlin Academy, pursued mathematics, logic, physics, theology, law and projects for the reunion of the Churches all at once. His philosophical work is scattered across short pieces, letters and two books, the *New Essays*, published posthumously, and the *Theodicy*.

#### The principles

Leibniz reasons from two great principles: the principle of contradiction, which governs truths of reason, which are necessary, and the principle of sufficient reason, which governs truths of fact, which are contingent, and according to which nothing happens without there being a reason why it is so rather than otherwise. To these are added the principle of indiscernibles, there are no two perfectly similar beings, and that of continuity, nature makes no leaps. Applied to the existence of the world, the principle of reason leads to God: the ultimate reason of contingent things must lie outside their series, in a necessary being, and the question why there is something rather than nothing, raised in the *Principles of Nature and Grace*, finds its answer there. God, who has in his understanding all possible worlds, chose to create the best of them, according to the principle of the best.

#### The monads

Against Descartes, who reduced matter to extension, Leibniz reintroduces force and substantial form. True substances are monads, set out in the *Discourse on Metaphysics* (1686) and the *Monadology* (1714): simple unities, without parts or windows, endowed with perception and appetition, each of which expresses the whole universe from its own point of view, with more or less distinctness, from bare monads to souls and minds. Bodies are well-founded phenomena resting on aggregates of monads. Since monads do not act on one another, their agreement, and that of soul and body, is explained by pre-established harmony: God created them in such a way that they correspond, like two clocks set together. The complete notion of each substance contains everything that will happen to it, which raises the problem of freedom, which Leibniz resolves by distinguishing inclination without necessity.

#### The Theodicy

The *Essays on Theodicy* (1710), the only book published in his lifetime, reply to Bayle, who held the problem of evil to be insoluble. Leibniz distinguishes metaphysical evil, the imperfection of every creature, physical evil, suffering, and moral evil, sin; God permits evil without willing it, because the best possible world contains some, a world without evil being either impossible or less good as a whole. Optimism, the name later given to this doctrine, was ridiculed by Voltaire in *Candide* after the Lisbon earthquake, and Kant judged that every philosophical theodicy fails. Russell saw in Leibniz's logic the key to his metaphysics; possible worlds have become a tool of modal logic.

#### What the essay assumes

The essay takes as known the principle of sufficient reason, the question of § 7 of the *Principles of Nature and Grace*, the doctrine of the best of worlds and of the three evils, the attested response to Bayle, Voltaire's response, and the visit to Spinoza in 1676. It judges that theodicy asks the individual to sacrifice his complaint, that Ivan Karamazov is its moral answer, and that Leibniz's question survives his answer.
