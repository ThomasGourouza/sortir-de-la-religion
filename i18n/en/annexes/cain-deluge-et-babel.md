Between the expulsion from the garden and the calling of Abraham, Gn 4 to 11 tells of the earliest times of humankind: a murder, a universal flood, an unfinished tower. These chapters, the primeval history, pose in narrative form the question of human violence and excess, and the moderns still draw their figures from them.

#### Cain and Abel

The narrative relates that Eve gives birth to Cain, a tiller of the soil, then to Abel, a shepherd. Each brings an offering, the fruits of the ground for the one, the firstborn of the flock for the other; God accepts Abel's and not Cain's. Cain leads his brother out into the field and kills him. Questioned, he answers that he is not his brother's keeper; the blood cries out from the ground, Cain is cursed and condemned to wandering, but God puts a mark on him so that no one will kill him. Cain settles east of Eden and builds the first city, which he names after his son Enoch.

#### The generations and the Flood

Ten generations lead from Adam to Noah through Seth, the son born after Abel's death; Gn 6 relates briefly that sons of God unite with the daughters of men and beget giants. Faced with human wickedness, God decides to blot them out; Noah alone finds favour. He receives the order to build an ark of wood and to bring into it his family and a pair of every species. The rain falls for forty days, the waters cover the mountains, everything perishes. Noah releases a raven, then a dove that returns with an olive branch. Having come out of the ark, he offers a sacrifice; God promises never again to curse the ground, makes a covenant with every living creature and gives as its sign the bow in the cloud.

#### The Tower of Babel

Gn 11 relates that the whole earth has one language. People settle in the plain of Shinar and decide to build a city and a tower whose top reaches the heavens. God comes down to see, confuses their language and scatters them over the whole earth; the city is given the name Babel, linked to the Hebrew verb meaning to confuse. The text is aimed at Babylon and its stepped towers, the ziggurats.

#### Parallels and composition

Mesopotamian texts shed light on the composition. The Epic of Gilgamesh contains a flood narrative in which Utnapishtim builds a boat, releases birds and offers a sacrifice; the older poem of Atrahasis explains the flood by the noise of humans, which keeps the gods from sleeping. Most exegetes regard the biblical narrative as a rereading of these traditions, composed during or after the exile in Babylon, in the 6th century BCE, in two interwoven strands whose doublets, the number of animals, the duration of the waters, preserve the trace. No geological evidence attests a universal flood.

#### Readings

Augustine, in Book XV of *The City of God*, makes Cain the founder of the earthly city and Abel the pilgrim of the city of God. Girard sees in Cain and Abel the founding murder from which culture and the city are born, told for once from the point of view of the victim, whose blood cries out. Among the moderns Babel becomes the figure of political excess, and the tower painted by Bruegel in 1563 fixes its image.

#### What the essay assumes

The essay does not set out these chapters; it uses their figures. The section on Girard takes from them the point that the Bible tells the lynching from the point of view of the victim and that the title of the 1978 book comes from the things hidden since the foundation of the world; the Girardian reading of Cain is presupposed there. In the section on Voegelin and Aron, Bruegel's *The Tower of Babel* illustrates the immanentization of the eschaton, salvation carried from heaven into history. Part Two recalls that Origen distinguishes two cities and that Augustine gives this idea its finished form; finally, two literary boxes take up Cain: the one in the section on Leibniz, with Byron's *Cain*, who rejects inherited guilt, and another with Hugo's *La Conscience*, in which an eye open in the sky pursues him even into the tomb.
