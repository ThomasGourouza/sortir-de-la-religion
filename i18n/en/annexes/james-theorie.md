William James (1842-1910), son of a wealthy Swedenborgian theologian and elder brother of the novelist Henry James, trained as a physician, professor of physiology, of psychology and then of philosophy at Harvard, who emerged from a severe depressive crisis through a decision to believe in free will, is the founder of American psychology and, with Peirce and Dewey, of pragmatism.

#### Psychology

The *Principles of Psychology* (1890), twelve years in the making, describe mental life as a stream of consciousness, continuous and selective, and not as an association of ideas; they put forward the theory of the emotions that bears his name, according to which we are sad because we cry; they analyse habit, attention, the self and the will. In them James refuses to reduce the mind to the brain while describing their correlations, and leaves the metaphysical question open.

#### Pragmatism and radical empiricism

Pragmatism, a name borrowed from Peirce and set out in the lectures of 1907, is first a method: the meaning of an idea lies in its practical consequences, and two ideas that have no different consequences are the same idea. It is then a theory of truth: an idea is true if it works, if it leads us successfully through experience; truth happens to ideas, it is a process of verification. Its adversaries, Russell and Moore, saw in this a confusion of truth with utility; James replied that he was describing the way truths are actually accepted. Radical empiricism, set out in posthumous essays, holds that relations are given in experience as much as the terms they relate, and that pure experience is prior to the distinction between subject and object; Bergson, with whom James corresponded, and Whitehead are close to it. *A Pluralistic Universe* (1909) defends an unfinished, open world against the absolute monism of Bradley and Royce.

#### Religion

*The Will to Believe*, a lecture of 1896 collected in a volume in 1897, answers Clifford: when an option is living, forced and momentous and reason cannot decide it, we have the right to let our passional nature decide, at our own risk. *The Varieties of Religious Experience*, the Gifford Lectures given in Edinburgh in 1901-1902, ground the psychology of religion on personal documents, conversions, saints, mystics, defining religion as the feelings, acts and experiences of individuals in their solitude, in relation to whatever they consider divine. James rejects medical materialism, which explains an experience by its pathological origin, judges religions by their fruits, distinguishes healthy-minded souls from sick souls, describes mysticism by four marks, ineffability, noetic quality, transiency, passivity, and concludes, in his own name, with an over-belief: religious experience puts us in contact with something wider, of which the subconscious is the door, and God is real since he produces real effects. He was also interested in psychical research and in mediums.

#### What the essay assumes

The essay takes as known pragmatism, the individual definition of religion, set against Durkheim's in an attested dialogue, medical materialism, fruits and roots, the two types of soul, the four marks of mysticism and *The Will to Believe*. It makes James the representative of the regime of experience and the ally of its thesis in the order of belief, not an exception, and combines his answer to Clifford with Clifford's own demand.
