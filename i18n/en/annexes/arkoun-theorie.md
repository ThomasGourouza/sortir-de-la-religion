Mohammed Arkoun (1928-2010), a Kabyle from Taourirt-Mimoun, educated at the lycée of Oran and at the Sorbonne, agrégé in Arabic, professor of the history of Islamic thought at the Sorbonne nouvelle from 1972 to 1992, sought to apply to Islam the human sciences of his time, history, anthropology, linguistics, semiotics, in order to reopen a tradition he judged closed in on itself. The essay knows him at second hand, through Tauil and Demorgon.

#### Arab humanism and closure

Arkoun's thesis, *L'Humanisme arabe au IVe/Xe siècle* (1970), studies Miskawayh and his circle, the century of Baghdad in which philosophers, theologians, men of letters and jurists debated freely, the Mu'tazilites included: an Arab humanism existed before being repressed. *La Pensée arabe* (1975) and *Lectures du Coran* (1982) trace the formation of what he calls Islamic reason, the system of thought constituted between the eighth and the eleventh centuries, which fixed the Qur'an as a closed official corpus and the religious sciences as orthodoxy, and excluded whatever did not conform to them. This dogmatic closure, of which the profession of faith of al-Qadir is a milestone, produced a sphere of the unthought and the unthinkable: the unthought is what the tradition has not yet thought, the unthinkable what it has forbidden itself to think, the lost debates, the abandoned possibilities, the question of the historicity of the sacred text.

#### The critique of Islamic reason

*Pour une critique de la raison islamique* (1984), a collection of articles, gives the programme its name: to subject Islamic reason to historical and anthropological critique, as Kant criticized pure reason and as biblical criticism treated Scripture, in order to distinguish the Qur'anic fact, the event of the prophetic word, from the Islamic fact, its construction by the following generations, and to free Muslims from dogmatism without handing them over to positivism. Arkoun gives the name applied Islamology to this discipline, which does not merely describe Islam from outside, as Orientalism does, but intervenes in its debates; he speaks of a plural, emergent reason, which would go beyond both Islamic reason and the reason of the Enlightenment. His vocabulary, closure, the unthought, archaeology, is indebted to Foucault, Bourdieu and Derrida. He argued for the teaching of the history of religions, for laïcité and for a Mediterranean of learning.

#### Reception

Little read in the Arab world during his lifetime, where his work, written in French, was translated late and judged too Western, contested by Islamists as well as by certain Orientalists, Arkoun influenced Abdou Filali-Ansary, Rachid Benzine and contemporary reformists, and his name remains attached to the idea that Islam can reappropriate its own history. He is buried in Casablanca.

#### What the essay assumes

The essay takes as known Islamic reason, dogmatic closure, the unthought and the unthinkable, the Mu'tazilites as a lost possibility and the vocabulary borrowed from French thought, while stating that it has read none of Arkoun's books. It concludes that Arkoun leaves the closure, not religion, on the side of Spinoza and Ricoeur, and ranks the closure of al-Qadir among the strict refoundations.
