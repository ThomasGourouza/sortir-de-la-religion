Gn 12 to 50 tells the story of a family, Abraham, Isaac, Jacob and his twelve sons, whom tradition calls the patriarchs. It is to their God that Pascal appeals in the Memorial, and the sacrifice of Isaac gave Kierkegaard and Girard their decisive scene.

#### Abraham: the promise and the covenant

The narrative relates that God commands Abram to leave his country for a land that he will show him, and promises him descendants and a blessing for all the families of the earth (Gn 12). Abram sets out with his wife Sarai and his nephew Lot. God makes a covenant with him, first through the rite of the divided animals (Gn 15), then by changing his name to Abraham and instituting circumcision as its sign (Gn 17). Sarah, who is barren, gives him her servant Hagar, mother of Ishmael, who is sent away into the desert with her son after the birth of Isaac. Abraham bargains with God over the fate of Sodom, from fifty righteous men down to ten; the city is destroyed.

#### The binding of Isaac

Gn 22, which Judaism calls the Aqedah, the binding, relates that God puts Abraham to the test: he asks him to take his only son and offer him as a burnt offering on a mountain in the land of Moriah. Abraham sets out with Isaac and two servants; on the third day he leaves the servants behind, lays the wood on Isaac and carries the fire and the knife. Isaac asks where the lamb is; Abraham answers that God will provide. He builds the altar, binds his son, lays him on the wood and stretches out his hand. The angel of the Lord stops him, and a ram caught by its horns in a thicket is offered in his place. The text is silent about Abraham's thoughts and about Isaac's reaction.

#### Jacob

Isaac marries Rebekah, who gives birth to twins, Esau and Jacob. Jacob buys the birthright for a dish of lentils and obtains his blind father's blessing by deceit. While fleeing, he sees in a dream a ladder between earth and heaven. In the house of his uncle Laban he serves fourteen years for Leah and Rachel. On the night before he meets Esau again, left alone at the ford of the Jabbok, he wrestles until dawn with a stranger who puts his hip out of joint and renames him Israel (Gn 32). His twelve sons give their names to the tribes; Joseph, sold by his brothers, becomes Pharaoh's minister in Egypt, where the whole family joins him: the Exodus follows on.

#### Composition and readings

No external source attests the patriarchs; most exegetes see them as ancestral figures carried by distinct cycles, Abraham in the south, Jacob in the north, brought together between the eighth and the fifth centuries BCE. Judaism reads the Aqedah as the merit of the fathers, invoked in the liturgy of the New Year; Christianity sees in it a figure of Christ carrying the wood; the Qur'an relates it without naming the son. Kierkegaard, in *Fear and Trembling* (1843), makes it the paradox of faith, a suspension of the ethical that cannot be justified before others. Girard reads it as the moment when human sacrifice is suspended and replaced by the animal. Caravaggio and Rembrandt painted the instant when the angel stays the hand.

#### What the essay assumes

The essay cites Pascal's Memorial, the God of Abraham, Isaac and Jacob set against the God of the philosophers and the scholars, and reads in it a critique of religion made in the name of faith, the true God being the God of a history; Rembrandt's *The Sacrifice of Abraham* illustrates the section, and the transition of Part Two says that Pascal appeals to the God who came before the Greek translation. The section on Kierkegaard opens with Delacroix's *Jacob Wrestling with the Angel*, an image of faith as a singular ordeal; *Fear and Trembling* is not analysed there, but is named later among the books that marked Iris Murdoch. In Girard, Caravaggio's *The Sacrifice of Isaac* is a key moment of his reading of the Bible. The section on Maimonides, finally, reads the Bible as a text whose anthropomorphisms call for interpretation: the stories of the patriarchs are full of them.
