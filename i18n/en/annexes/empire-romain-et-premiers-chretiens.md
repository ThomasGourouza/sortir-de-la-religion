For three centuries, from the death of Jesus (c. 30) to the agreement of Milan (313), Christianity was a religion without status in the Roman Empire, tolerated by default and punished intermittently. The persecutions, less continuous than legend claims, shaped its vocabulary, its cult of the martyrs and its first literature, apologetics.

#### A religion without status

Rome did not persecute cults as such, and Judaism enjoyed a long-standing tolerance that exempted Jews from the imperial cult. Christians lost this benefit as they separated from the synagogue, especially after the destruction of the Temple in 70. They refused to sacrifice to the gods of the city and to the genius of the emperor, which Rome took as a refusal of civic loyalty; they were accused of atheism, of hatred of the human race and of secret crimes. The offence was not belief but the obstinate refusal to perform the public gesture.

The first episodes were local. In 64, after the fire of Rome, Nero laid the blame on the Christians of the city and had a great many of them tortured to death; Tacitus, who reports the fact around 116 in the *Annals* (XV, 44), notes in passing that their name comes from a certain Christus executed under Tiberius by the procurator Pontius Pilate. Around 112, Pliny the Younger, governor of Bithynia, asked Trajan how to deal with the Christians denounced to him; Trajan's answer set the rule for a century: do not seek them out, reject anonymous denunciations, punish those who, once denounced, refuse to sacrifice. The martyrs of the second century, Polycarp at Smyrna (c. 155), Justin at Rome (c. 165), the Christians of Lyon (177), Perpetua at Carthage (203), died under this regime of denunciation, not under a general law.

#### The imperial persecutions

The turning point came in the middle of the third century. In 250 Decius ordered all the inhabitants of the Empire to sacrifice to the gods and to obtain a certificate of it; this was the first general persecution, and it produced more apostates than martyrs. Valerian, in 257-258, targeted the clergy; Cyprian of Carthage was executed. Gallienus, in 260, restored the places of worship: forty years of peace followed. Diocletian's Great Persecution, launched in 303 by a series of edicts (destruction of churches and books, arrest of the clergy, obligation to sacrifice), was the harshest, especially in the East; Galerius's edict of toleration (311) brought it to an end.

The word martyr, in Greek the witness, first designates the one who attests his faith before the tribunal; the cult of their tombs made them the first saints. The apologists, Justin, Athenagoras, Tertullian, addressed the authorities to claim the protection of the ordinary law and to present Christianity as the true philosophy.

#### A slow, urban spread

Christianity spread through the cities, along the roads and the networks of the Jewish diaspora: Antioch, Alexandria, Asia Minor, Rome, then North Africa and Gaul. Its followers were at first artisans, women, slaves and freedmen, then men of letters and notables. Around 300 they were still a minority, a tenth of the population, but an organized one, with bishops, property and schools. Celsus wrote around 178, when this minority was beginning to cause concern; Origen answered him around 248, on the eve of the edict of Decius.

#### What the essay assumes

The essay cites Tacitus on Nero as non-Christian testimony to the existence of Jesus, mentions the martyrdom of Justin, a case in which it is Rome that demands the sacrifice, and reports Celsus's mockery of the religion of slaves and Engels's thesis of a movement of the oppressed. It takes it as known that the Empire demanded the gesture of sacrifice, not inner adherence.

#### Key dates

- 64: fire of Rome, first executions of Christians under Nero.
- c. 112: Pliny's letter to Trajan and the imperial rescript.
- c. 165: martyrdom of Justin at Rome.
- 250: edict of Decius; 257-258: persecution of Valerian; 260: peace of Gallienus.
- 303-311: Diocletian's Great Persecution; 311: edict of Galerius; 313: agreement of Milan.
