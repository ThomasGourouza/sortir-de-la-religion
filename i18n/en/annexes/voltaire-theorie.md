François-Marie Arouet (1694-1778), known as Voltaire, poet, playwright, historian, storyteller and polemicist, imprisoned in the Bastille, exiled to England, courtier at Versailles and at Potsdam, then patriarch of Ferney on the Swiss border, was for half a century the most widely read writer in Europe and the symbol of the French Enlightenment. His religious thought is a combative deism.

#### Deism

Voltaire believes in a God who creates and orders, proved by the order of the world, the clock presupposing a clockmaker, and he holds atheism to be a philosophical error and a social danger: he writes against Spinoza, against d'Holbach and his *System of Nature*, and against the atheists of the coterie, to whom he objects in the *Épître à l'auteur des Trois imposteurs* (1769) that if God did not exist, it would be necessary to invent him. This God does not reveal himself, does not become incarnate and has no Church; he guarantees morality, common to all human beings, and justice, which kings must fear. The *Philosophical Letters* (1734), brought back from England, praise the tolerance of the English sects, Locke and Newton, are condemned and burned by the Parlement, and earn their author a lettre de cachet that makes him flee to Cirey. The *Poem on the Lisbon Disaster* (1756) and *Candide* (1759) break with the optimism of Leibniz and Pope: evil is real, Providence incomprehensible, and we must cultivate our garden.

#### The Infamous

From the 1760s onwards, Voltaire campaigns against what he calls the Infâme, the Infamous, fanaticism, superstition and the Church that upholds them, under the motto Écrasez l'Infâme with which he signs his letters. The *Pocket Philosophical Dictionary* (1764), the *Treatise on Tolerance* (1763), *La Bible enfin expliquée*, dozens of anonymous pamphlets attack dogmas, miracles, the contradictions of the Bible, persecutions. The affair of Calas, a Toulouse Protestant broken on the wheel in 1762 for a murder he had not committed, whose rehabilitation Voltaire obtains in 1765, then the Sirven and La Barre affairs, make him the defender of the victims of intolerance and the model of the writer who takes up causes. He nevertheless remains hostile to the education of the people, wishes servants and the tailor to keep a religion that restrains them, and practises Catholic worship at Ferney, building a church dedicated to God.

#### History and posterity

The *Essai sur les mœurs* (1756) writes the first universal history that does not start from the Bible and grants China, India and Islam their place; *The Age of Louis XIV* founds the history of civilization. Placed in the Panthéon in 1791, Voltaire became for the Republic the symbol of the struggle against clericalism, and for its adversaries the father of the Revolution, of which he saw nothing. Historians distinguish the sincere deist from the tactician, and debate how much social fear there is in his religion for the people.

#### The alexandrine of 1769

The line comes from the *Épître à l'auteur du livre des Trois imposteurs* (1769), in which Voltaire answers the *Traité des trois imposteurs*, a clandestine pamphlet that took Moses, Jesus and Muhammad for three frauds. He gives it after presenting belief in God as the bond of society and the curb on the wicked:

“Si Dieu n'existait pas, il faudrait l'inventer.” (If God did not exist, it would be necessary to invent him.)

It is a regular alexandrine of twelve syllables, with its caesura after “pas”. The line that follows fixes its scope: “Que le sage l'annonce, et que les rois le craignent.” (Let the wise man proclaim him, and let kings fear him.) The first has become a proverb and almost always circulates alone; the second recalls that this God must restrain the powerful as much as the people, and the rest of the epistle threatens with his judgement the kings who oppress the innocent. Voltaire was attached to the phrase, which he says in his correspondence he loves as a father loves his child.

#### What the essay assumes

The essay assumes that deism, the Infamous, the Calas affair, *Candide* and the *Épître* of 1769 are known; it makes the last the programme of the Grand Inquisitor and the example of an avowed refoundation, God invented as the sacred bond of society. It situates Voltaire's attested response to d'Holbach, *Candide* against Leibniz, and the attested exchange with Rousseau over the Lisbon disaster, and notes that Ivan Karamazov quotes the line from the Épître.
