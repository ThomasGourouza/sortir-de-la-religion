Christian mysticism designates the experience of union with God in this life and the writings that describe it, from late Antiquity to the seventeenth century. The essay meets in it the precursors of negative theology and of the spiritualities without God, Eckhart first, and the models to which James, Bergson, Bataille and Simone Weil refer.

#### The sources and the Middle Ages

The word comes from the Greek mustikos, hidden, and first designates the hidden sense of Scripture and the sacraments. Origen and Gregory of Nyssa describe the ascent of the soul towards God in the darkness, like Moses on Sinai; Augustine, the inner illumination and the ecstasy at Ostia; Pseudo-Dionysius, at the beginning of the sixth century, in the *Mystical Theology*, the negative theology that goes beyond every affirmation and every negation to reach God in unknowing, and he hands down to the Middle Ages the word hierarchy, which he coined, and the very title of mystical theology. Bernard of Clairvaux, in the twelfth century, sings the love of the soul for the Word in his sermons on the Song of Songs; the Victorines systematize contemplation; women, Hildegard of Bingen, Hadewijch, Mechthild of Magdeburg, Marguerite Porete, burned in 1310, write in the vernacular a mysticism of love and annihilation. Rhineland mysticism, Eckhart, Tauler, Suso, then Ruusbroec in Flanders, reflects on the birth of God in the soul and on detachment; the Devotio moderna and *The Imitation of Christ* give it its practical version, and Nicholas of Cusa, *On Learned Ignorance*.

#### The Spanish golden age and the French seventeenth century

In Spain, in the sixteenth century, Teresa of Ávila describes in her *Life* and *The Interior Castle* the degrees of prayer up to the spiritual marriage, and reforms the Carmelite order; John of the Cross, her companion, composes the poems of the *Dark Night* and the *Spiritual Canticle* and comments on them: the soul must pass through the night of the senses and the night of the spirit, a stripping away of every support, to be united with God in naked faith. Ignatius of Loyola gives the *Spiritual Exercises* their Jesuit form. The French seventeenth century, which Bremond called the mystical invasion, sees Francis de Sales, Bérulle, Pascal with the *Memorial*, Marie de l'Incarnation, Surin, then the quietist controversy: Madame Guyon and Fénelon defend pure love, passive abandonment to God without desire for reward, Bossuet fights them, and Rome condemns Fénelon in 1699. After this controversy mysticism is held in suspicion, and Michel de Certeau read in this century the moment when mysticism, turned into a noun, becomes a separate body of knowledge, detached from theology.

#### Modern rereadings

James classifies mystical states by four marks and makes them the ground of personal religion; Bergson places the great Christian mystics at the summit of dynamic religion; Schopenhauer, Bataille and Weil read them outside the Church; Otto compares Eckhart and Shankara; Hadot recalls what Christian exercises owe to the philosophers.

#### What the essay assumes

The essay assumes familiarity with the negative theology of Dionysius, Eckhart and Rhineland mysticism, John of the Cross, quietism and the controversy over pure love, and the uses made of them by James, Bergson and Bataille. It sees in mysticism one of the anticipations of the regime of experience, in Eckhart the ancestor of the spiritualities without God, and in Weil's decreation a mysticism that refuses consolation.
