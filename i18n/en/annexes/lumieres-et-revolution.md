The France of the Ancien Régime is a monarchy by divine right in which the Catholic Church is the first order of the realm; in ten years the Revolution strips it of its property, its clergy and its calendar, then attempts to replace it. The revolutionary cults of 1793 and 1794 are, for Durkheim as for Comte, the experiment that shows what becomes of religion when one tries to do without it.

#### The Gallican Church and its quarrels

The king, anointed at Reims, holds his power from God alone; Bossuet sets out the theory of this in the *Politique tirée de l'Écriture sainte* (1709). The Church of France is Gallican: the declaration of 1682 limits the pope's authority over the realm, and the king appoints the bishops. It is riven by the Jansenist quarrel: the Augustinian theology of grace, defended at Port-Royal and by Pascal in the *Provinciales* (1656-1657), is condemned by Rome and by the king, the monastery is razed in 1710, and the bull *Unigenitus* (1713) divides the clergy for half a century. The parlements, Jansenist and Gallican, obtain the expulsion of the Jesuits (1764). Protestantism has been outlawed since 1685; the edict of 1787 restores civil status to the Reformed.

#### The Enlightenment and the Infamous

Royal and ecclesiastical censorship strikes the *Encyclopédie* (1759), *Emile* and *The Social Contract* (1762, condemned in Paris and in Geneva), and the *Système de la nature* (1770). Voltaire campaigns against the Infamous, fanaticism and the Church, around the Calas affair (1762); d'Holbach and his coterie privately profess an atheism that Voltaire judges dangerous for the people. Historians have shown that religious practice was already declining, slowly, among the urban elites, but that the mass of the realm remained faithful.

#### The Revolution against the Church

The Assembly nationalizes the property of the clergy (2 November 1789) and votes the Civil Constitution of the Clergy (12 July 1790), which makes priests elected public officials; the oath required in 1791 splits the clergy into jurors and refractories, and the pope condemns the Constitution. War and the Terror turn the rupture into persecution: priests deported or executed, civil registration secularized (1792), the republican calendar (October 1793), dechristianization carried out by the representatives on mission, the closing of churches, the festival of Reason at Notre-Dame on 10 November 1793. Robespierre, who regards atheism as aristocratic and venerates Rousseau, has the Convention decree, on 18 Floréal Year II (7 May 1794), that the French people recognizes the existence of the Supreme Being and the immortality of the soul, and presides over the festival of the Supreme Being on 20 Prairial (8 June 1794). Thermidor, seven weeks later, ends the cult together with its founder. The Directory separates Church and State (February 1795) and lets theophilanthropy, a natural religion with a civic liturgy, and the decadal cult flourish.

#### The Concordat

Bonaparte signs the Concordat with Pius VII (15 July 1801): Catholicism, the religion of the great majority of the French, regains public worship, bishops are appointed by the State and instituted by the pope, and the clergy is salaried; the Organic Articles (1802) add Gallicanism to it. This regime lasts until the separation of 1905. The nineteenth century will oscillate between the alliance of throne and altar and anticlericalism; Saint-Simon, Comte and their disciples will seek a new religion in that century.

#### What the essay assumes

The essay quotes the decree of 18 Floréal and the festival of the Supreme Being, compares Robespierre's cult with Rousseau's civil religion, recalls that Durkheim sees in the Revolution the proof of society's capacity to create gods and that Comte judges the revolutionary cults too metaphysical; it mentions the condemnation of *Emile* and the refuge of Port-Royal. It assumes familiarity with Gallicanism, Jansenism and the dechristianization of Year II.

#### Key dates

- 1682: Gallican declaration; 1713: bull *Unigenitus*; 1764: expulsion of the Jesuits.
- 1790: Civil Constitution of the Clergy; 1793: republican calendar, festival of Reason.
- 7 May and 8 June 1794: decree and festival of the Supreme Being; 27 July 1794: fall of Robespierre.
- 1795: separation; 1801: Concordat.
