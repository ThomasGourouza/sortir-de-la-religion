Narcissus is the young man of perfect beauty who, refusing all loves, falls in love with his own reflection and dies of it. The myth, known above all through Ovid's *Metamorphoses*, was read by the philosophers as an image of the soul that mistakes itself for appearance, and in the twentieth century it gave its name to one of the concepts of psychoanalysis.

#### Ovid's narrative

Narcissus is the son of the river Cephissus and of the nymph Liriope. Consulted at his birth, the seer Tiresias foretells that he will live long if he does not know himself. Grown into an adolescent desired by boys and girls alike, he rejects them all. The nymph Echo, condemned by Hera to be able only to repeat the last words she hears, loves him without being able to speak to him first; rejected, she wastes away with grief until she is nothing but a voice. One of the scorned asks the gods that Narcissus in his turn should love without being able to possess what he loves. Leaning over a spring to drink, Narcissus sees his image, falls in love with it, and understands too late that he loves his own reflection; he pines away at the water's edge, and in his place only a flower is found, the narcissus. Pausanias reports a variant in which he mourns a twin sister whose features his own face recalls to him.

#### The ancient readings

Plotinus, in the treatise on beauty, compares the one who runs after sensible beauties to someone who would try to seize a beautiful image reflected in the water and would drown in it; Pierre Hadot has shown how the Neoplatonists and the Fathers made Narcissus a figure of the soul that loses its way in the world of reflections instead of turning towards its source. In the Middle Ages and the Renaissance, the myth becomes a warning against pride and self-love.

#### Narcissism

The psychiatrist Paul Näcke and the sexologist Havelock Ellis used the word at the end of the nineteenth century to designate a perversion. Freud, in 1914, makes it a stage of development: primary narcissism, in which the child invests his libido in himself before turning it towards objects, and a secondary narcissism, a return of the libido towards the ego. In *Civilization and Its Discontents*, he traces the oceanic feeling that Romain Rolland describes to him back to this primary narcissism, a state in which the infant does not yet distinguish his ego from the world. Beyond psychoanalysis, the word has become a diagnosis of society, notably in Christopher Lasch.

#### What the essay assumes

The essay assumes familiarity with the notion of primary narcissism, by which Freud explains Rolland's oceanic feeling, in the Freud section and in the constructed debate on the reasons for believing: a remnant of childhood in which ego and world are not yet separated.
