Islam is born in Arabia in the seventh century and within a century rules a territory stretching from Spain to the Indus. It is in this territory, between the ninth and the twelfth centuries, that Greek philosophy is translated, commented upon and carried further, before returning to Latin Europe by way of Spain. The essay treats Islam only in passing, through Averroes, Maimonides, Arkoun and Bidar, but it takes the main features of this history as known.

#### Birth and expansion

Muhammad (c. 570-632), a merchant of Mecca, receives from about 610 onwards revelations that he proclaims as the word of the one God; in 622 he emigrates to Medina, where he founds a community that is at once religious and political: this is the hijra. At his death Arabia is unified. The Qur'an, the collection of these revelations, is set down in writing under the caliph Uthman, around 650. The caliphs conquer Syria, Egypt, Persia and, at the beginning of the eighth century, Spain. The succession to Muhammad divides Muslims between Sunnis, the majority, and Shiites, partisans of Ali and his descendants. The Umayyads reign from Damascus (661-750), then the Abbasids from Baghdad (750-1258), while an emirate, then an independent Umayyad caliphate, holds on in Cordoba. Christians and Jews, people of the Book, have there a protected and inferior status, that of dhimmi.

#### The translations and falsafa

In Baghdad, under the Abbasids, a vast translation movement carries into Arabic, often by way of Syriac, Aristotle, Plato, Galen, Euclid and Ptolemy. From this reception is born falsafa, philosophy in the Greek tradition written in Arabic: al-Kindi in the ninth century, al-Farabi in the tenth, Avicenna (Ibn Sina, 980-1037) in Persia, whose metaphysics of the necessary being and of possible beings will mark the whole of Latin scholasticism, and finally Averroes (Ibn Rushd, 1126-1198) in Cordoba, the Commentator on Aristotle par excellence. Alongside the philosophers, the theologians of kalam debate the justice of God, human freedom and the status of the Qur'an. The Mu'tazilites, dominant in Baghdad at the beginning of the ninth century, hold that the Qur'an is created, that God is just in the sense in which reason understands justice, and that man is free; the caliph al-Ma'mun imposes their doctrine through an inquisition, the mihna. The reversal comes with their adversaries: Ash'arism, which affirms the omnipotence of God against human reason, and the profession of faith of the caliph al-Qadir, at the beginning of the eleventh century, which declares the Qur'an uncreated and condemns the Mu'tazilite theses. Arkoun sees in this moment the dogmatic closure of Sunni Islam.

#### Al-Ghazali and Averroes

Al-Ghazali (1058-1111), theologian and mystic, writes the *Tahāfut al-falāsifa*, *The Incoherence of the Philosophers*, which condemns twenty theses of falsafa, three of them as impious: the eternity of the world, God's ignorance of particulars, the denial of the resurrection of bodies. Averroes answers him with the *Tahāfut al-tahāfut*, *The Incoherence of the Incoherence*, and, in the *Faṣl al-maqāl*, *The Decisive Treatise*, maintains that the revealed Law itself commands philosophizing and that truth does not contradict truth. His work is little read in the Muslim world after him, where Sufism and theology prevail, and widely read in Latin Europe, where it nourishes Parisian Averroism and the condemnations of 1277. Philosophy continues in Shiite Iran, with Suhrawardi and Mulla Sadra, in a tradition that Europe long ignored.

#### What the essay assumes

The essay uses without explaining them the distinction between falsafa and kalam, the memory of the Mu'tazilites as the lost possibility of a rationalist Islam, the closure of al-Qadir, which it ranks among the strict refoundations, and the quarrel between al-Ghazali and Averroes, an attested response. It recalls that the double truth attributed to Averroes is a Latin legend, and that Maimonides, a Jew of Cordoba writing in Arabic, belongs to the same intellectual world.

#### Key dates

- 622: hijra, founding of the community of Medina.
- 632: death of Muhammad; 711: landing in Spain.
- 750: accession of the Abbasids; Baghdad founded in 762.
- 833-848: mihna, the Mu'tazilite inquisition.
- 1018: profession of faith of al-Qadir.
- 1037: death of Avicenna; 1111: death of al-Ghazali; 1198: death of Averroes.
- 1258: capture of Baghdad by the Mongols.
