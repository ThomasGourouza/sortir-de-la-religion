The cognitive science of religion is a research programme born in the 1990s, which explains the recurrence of religious beliefs by the ordinary workings of the human mind as studied by cognitive psychology, anthropology and evolutionary psychology. Boyer, whom the essay reads in its ninth part, is one of its founders.

#### The programme

Its guiding idea is that religion needs no special explanation, whether by a need, a function or a faculty of its own: it is a by-product of mental systems that evolved for other tasks. The human mind is made up of specialized inference systems, intuitive and fast, that handle physical objects, living beings, persons and social relations. Religious concepts exploit these systems: they respect almost all of their expectations and violate one or two, which makes them both memorable and usable. Stewart Guthrie, in *Faces in the Clouds* (1993), starts from anthropomorphism: we see faces in the clouds and intentions in the wind, because it costs less to take a rock for a bear than the reverse; gods are the product of this systematic bias, which Hume had described in 1757. Justin Barrett names the corresponding faculty the hypersensitive agency detection device. Pascal Boyer, in *Religion Explained* (2001), and Scott Atran, in *In Gods We Trust* (2002), develop the theory of minimally counterintuitive concepts and of supernatural agents endowed with strategic information about what matters to human beings, which makes them relevant to morality and cooperation. Robert McCauley and Thomas Lawson analyse rituals as actions whose form is constrained by cognition.

#### Internal debates

Two positions clash over function. For the by-product theory, religion was selected for nothing; for adaptationist theories, David Sloan Wilson or Ara Norenzayan, the great moralizing and watchful gods favoured cooperation in large societies, and religious groups thus survived better. Developmental psychology, with Barrett and Deborah Kelemen, studies children's tendency towards teleology and the attribution of intentions. Daniel Dennett, in *Breaking the Spell* (2006), popularizes the programme and draws from it a plea for the naturalistic study of religion, taking care to distinguish the explanation of causes from the question of truth, the genetic fallacy. The discipline has been criticized for its universalism, for the thinness of its data outside the West and for the replication crisis that has struck experimental psychology.

#### What the essay assumes

The essay retains from Boyer that gods are easy to think, that science is as unnatural as religion is natural, and that there is in man no empty place of the exact shape of God; it concludes that Boyer adds no regime to critique but explains the religious disposition. It recalls, after Dennett, the risk of the genetic fallacy, and reproaches him for explaining beliefs, not exercises: a spirituality without belief escapes his model. It assumes familiarity with the vocabulary of inference systems and the by-product, Hume's place as precursor, and Dennett's membership of both camps, the cognitive sciences and the new atheism.
