Since the 1960s, the decline of the Churches in the West has not resulted in the disappearance of the religious but in the growth of spiritualities outside institutions, individual, composite, centred on experience and well-being, which sociologists call the mystical-esoteric nebula, religion à la carte or the spiritual revolution. The essay encounters them in its twelfth part, around Western Buddhism and spiritualities without God.

#### The New Age and the nebula

The New Age, born in California and England in the 1960s and 1970s, announces the entry into the Age of Aquarius, an age of expanded consciousness, and brings together heterogeneous sources: nineteenth-century theosophy, Western esotericism, humanistic and transpersonal psychology, Maslow, Jung, Eastern spiritualities imported by Indian gurus and Zen masters, shamanism, ecology, alternative medicine, channelling and crystals. Its implicit doctrine comes down to a few features: the divine is within oneself, personal experience takes precedence over dogma, everything is energy and interdependence, the transformation of the self will transform the world, and one chooses freely from all traditions. In France Françoise Champion described a mystical-esoteric nebula, Paul Heelas a spirituality of the self, Hervieu-Léger the figure of the pilgrim. The term New Age grew dated in the 1990s, but its contents spread into personal development, coaching, wellness and bookshops.

#### Meditation, mindfulness, yoga

Yoga, practised in the West since Vivekananda in 1893 and popularized by Indian teachers of the twentieth century, has become a mass form of exercise, largely detached from its Hindu metaphysics. Buddhist meditation, Zen, vipassanā, Tibetan, has followed the same path: in 1979 Jon Kabat-Zinn created a stress reduction programme based on mindfulness, which entered hospitals, then schools, prisons, Silicon Valley companies and apps, backed by clinical studies; Matthieu Ricard and the Dalai Lama are the media faces of this Western Buddhism, and some Buddhists criticize a McMindfulness that has stripped meditation of ethics and liberation to make it a performance technique. Corporate meditation is, for the essay, the end point of this transfer.

#### Spiritual but not religious

In the United States, the phrase spiritual but not religious has designated since the 1990s a growing share of the population, about a quarter at the turn of the 2020s, which rejects institutions without giving up a quest for meaning; in Europe, the nones, disaffiliated rather than atheist, likewise improvise their own arrangements. Frédéric Lenoir, in France, has described and accompanied this metamorphosis of God towards a secular spirituality of wisdom and happiness, from Spinoza to Buddhism; pocket Stoics, books of wisdom and retreats are its market. Sociologists debate whether this spirituality is a disguised secularization, a privatization of religion or a new religion, without a Church but with its merchants; the Churches see in it sometimes a competitor, sometimes a field of action, and the French state, through the Miviludes, monitors its sectarian excesses.

#### What the essay assumes

The essay assumes familiarity with the New Age and the nebula, Hervieu-Léger's pilgrim, mindfulness and corporate meditation, the phrase spiritual but not religious, and Lenoir, heir to Renan. It judges Western Buddhism a reinvention and corporate meditation a palliative treatment, sees in these spiritualities the position of the atheists of the marketplace when they believe themselves to be without history, and recalls that a spirituality without a lineage escapes refoundation but is not handed down.
