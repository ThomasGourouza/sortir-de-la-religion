Fyodor Mikhailovich Dostoevsky (1821-1881), the son of a Moscow army doctor, trained as an engineer and famous as a novelist from *Poor Folk* (1846) onwards, was sentenced to death in 1849 for his part in the Petrashevsky circle, reprieved before the firing squad and sent to four years of hard labour in Siberia. He came back converted to Orthodox Christianity and to the Russian people, and in twenty years wrote the novels that made him, with Tolstoy, one of the two greatest Russian novelists and, for philosophers, a thinker.

#### The great novels

*Notes from Underground* (1864) invents the voice of the underground man, who out of caprice rejects reason and the crystal palace of progress. *Crime and Punishment* (1866) follows Raskolnikov, the student who kills a moneylender to prove that he belongs among the extraordinary men to whom everything is permitted, and whom conscience brings back to confession. *The Idiot* (1868-1869) brings into Petersburg society Prince Myshkin, a perfectly good, Christlike man, who fails. *Demons*, long translated as *The Possessed* (1871-1872), a political novel inspired by the Nechayev affair, stages Stavrogin, the empty seducer, Pyotr Verkhovensky, the nihilist conspirator, Shatov, who believes in Russia without believing in God, and Kirillov, the engineer who wants to kill himself to prove that man is god. *The Brothers Karamazov* (1879-1880), completed a few months before his death, is the novel of a parricide: Fyodor Pavlovich, the ignoble father, and his sons, Dmitri the passionate, Ivan the atheist intellectual, Alyosha the novice, and the bastard Smerdyakov, who kills the father by taking Ivan at his word. Book V contains Ivan's rebellion, in which he returns his ticket to God in the name of tortured children, and the legend of the Grand Inquisitor; Book VI, the life and conversations of the elder Zosima, Alyosha's master, an answer to Ivan by example, not by argument.

#### Polyphony and ideas

In 1929 Mikhail Bakhtin described the Dostoevskian novel as polyphonic: its characters are full consciousnesses whose voices are not subordinated to the author's, and ideas live in it like characters, tested to the very end. This is why the atheism of Ivan and Kirillov is as strong in it as Zosima's faith, and why the novel could be read by believers and unbelievers alike. Dostoevsky knew Feuerbach and Strauss through Belinsky, read Renan, and in *A Writer's Diary* (1873-1881) waged the polemics of a Slavophile publicist, nationalist, at times antisemitic, hostile to the Catholic and socialist West, which seemed to him to promise the anthill. His Pushkin speech of 1880 was his triumph.

#### The readers

Nietzsche discovered him in 1887, in French translations, and regarded him as the only psychologist from whom he had learned anything; Freud devoted *Dostoevsky and Parricide* to him in 1928; Shestov, Berdyaev, Gide, Camus, who adapted *The Possessed* for the stage and discussed Kirillov in *The Myth of Sisyphus* and Ivan in *The Rebel*, Bakhtin, and René Girard, whose second book is *Dostoïevski, du double à l'unité*, made him a philosopher in spite of himself.

#### What the essay assumes

The essay assumes familiarity with the structure of the *Karamazovs*, the characters of Ivan, Alyosha, Zosima and Smerdyakov, the figure of Kirillov in *Demons*, polyphony as Bakhtin understands it, the indirect reception of Feuerbach and Strauss, and the *Diary*. It makes the Grand Inquisitor the centre of gravity of its question and its touchstone, points out that the formula everything is permitted never appears as such, and follows the readings of Nietzsche, Camus and Freud.
