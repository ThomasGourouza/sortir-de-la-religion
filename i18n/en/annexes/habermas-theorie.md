Jürgen Habermas (b. 1929), Adorno's assistant in Frankfurt, professor at Heidelberg, Frankfurt and Starnberg, heir and critic of the Frankfurt School, is one of the most influential German philosophers of the postwar period, at once the theorist of communicative reason and a public intellectual engaged in the debates of the Federal Republic, from the historians' dispute to Europe and genetic engineering.

#### The public sphere and critical theory

*The Structural Transformation of the Public Sphere* (1962) traces the birth in the eighteenth century of a bourgeois public sphere in which private persons reason about common affairs, and its decline in mass society. *Knowledge and Human Interests* (1968) distinguishes three knowledge-constitutive interests, technical, practical and emancipatory. *The Theory of Communicative Action* (1981), in two volumes, is the major work: against the instrumental reason that Adorno and Horkheimer had made the fate of the Enlightenment, Habermas distinguishes strategic action, oriented towards success, from communicative action, oriented towards mutual understanding, in which participants raise validity claims, to truth, rightness and sincerity, that they can justify with reasons; rationality lies in this structure of language. The lifeworld is colonized by systems, the economy and the administration, and critical theory must defend its communicative resources. Discourse ethics, developed with Apel, grounds norms in the possible agreement of all those concerned; *Between Facts and Norms* (1992) applies the model to the constitutional state.

#### Postmetaphysical thinking and religion

*Postmetaphysical Thinking* (1988) accepts that philosophy can no longer ground a total worldview and must become fallible and procedural, cooperating with the sciences. Describing himself, according to an often reported formula, as religiously unmusical, Habermas has taken religion seriously since the address *Faith and Knowledge*, delivered in Frankfurt on 14 October 2001: in a post-secular society, where religious communities persist in a secularized environment, secular reason must recognize its limits and translate into a language accessible to all the moral intuitions that religious traditions have preserved, such as creatureliness in the face of genetic engineering; a secularization that does not annihilate is accomplished in the mode of translation. The dialogue with Cardinal Ratzinger (2004), *Between Naturalism and Religion* (2005) and *Also a History of Philosophy*, *Auch eine Geschichte der Philosophie* (2019), in two volumes, which traces the genealogy of postmetaphysical thinking from the axial age onwards and the constellation of faith and knowledge, develop this position. Taylor, Rawls and theologians have discussed the asymmetrical burden that translation places on believers.

#### What the essay assumes

The essay takes as known communicative reason, the public sphere, postmetaphysical thinking, the 2001 address and translation, and specifies that it has read neither the dialogue with Ratzinger nor the 2019 history of philosophy. It sees in Habermas a third way between Löwith and Blumenberg, one that translates only normative contents and does not say how the individual lives without religion, and notes that for him Kant is the first example of a secularizing and saving deconstruction.
