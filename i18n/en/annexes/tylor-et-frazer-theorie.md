Edward Burnett Tylor (1832-1917) and James George Frazer (1854-1941) are the two great names of English evolutionist anthropology, which sought, in Darwin's wake, to write the natural history of religion on the basis of so-called primitive societies. Their books supplied two generations with the vocabulary of the critique of religion, before fieldwork refuted them.

#### Tylor and animism

The son of Quakers, without a university degree, a traveller in Mexico, Tylor published *Primitive Culture* in 1871, which opens with the first anthropological definition of culture, that complex whole which includes knowledge, belief, art, morals, law and custom. Culture evolves everywhere through the same stages, from savagery to civilization, and incomprehensible modern customs are explained as survivals of earlier states. Religion, defined minimally as belief in spiritual beings, arises from animism: the first humans, to explain dreams, fainting and death, conceived of a soul separable from the body, then attributed it to animals, plants and things, and from there came spirits, gods and finally the one God. This primitive philosophy is reasonable but false, and the science of culture, says the last page, is a reformer's science, which marks out harmful survivals for destruction. In 1896 Tylor obtained the first chair of anthropology at Oxford.

#### Frazer and The Golden Bough

A Cambridge Hellenist and editor of Pausanias, Frazer never did fieldwork. *The Golden Bough*, published in two volumes in 1890 and expanded to twelve by 1915, starts from a riddle: why did the priest of the grove at Nemi have to be killed by his successor? The answer mobilizes the whole of humanity: divine kings put to death before their strength declines, vegetation gods who die and are reborn, Adonis, Attis, Osiris, scapegoats laden with the ills of the community, taboos, fire festivals. Frazer orders this compilation according to a law of three stages: magic, which believes it can compel nature through similarity and contact, sympathetic magic, and which is the bastard sister of science; religion, which implores higher beings when magic has failed; science, which rediscovers the idea of law. The book, which places Christ among the dying gods in pages that the third edition relegates to an appendix, nourished the mythicists, Freud in *Totem and Taboo*, and literature, Eliot, Joyce, Lawrence. Frazer regarded his friend William Robertson Smith, who had placed communion sacrifice at the centre of Semitic religion, as his master.

#### The refutations

Durkheim objects that a universal, everyday illusion would not be one, and that religion is a social fact, not an individual error; Malinowski, that the succession of stages is not found in the field; Wittgenstein, in his *Remarks on Frazer's Golden Bough*, that Frazer is more savage than his savages, because he takes for mistaken theories rites that express; Evans-Pritchard sums up these criticisms in 1965. Anthropology has kept the definition of culture, the universality of belief in invisible beings, and a few themes taken up by Girard and by Descola.

#### What the essay assumes

The essay assumes familiarity with animism, survivals, the reformer's science, the sequence of magic, religion, science, and the scapegoats of *The Golden Bough*. It places Tylor and Frazer in the regime of error in its evolutionist form, notes that they explain the birth of beliefs and not their persistence, and reports the refutations by Durkheim, by Wittgenstein, an attested response, and by Malinowski.
