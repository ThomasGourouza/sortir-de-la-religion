From the ninth century onwards, the word Christendom (*christianitas*) designates the whole of the peoples of the West united by the same faith, the same clergy and the same Roman authority: a society in which the Church is everywhere, in law, in the calendar, in the school, at birth and at death. In Kierkegaard, it also designates that religion turned into custom which Christianity must fight. The history of medieval Christendom is the history of how this whole was built, and then of how its borders were policed.

#### From the fall of Rome to Charlemagne

After the end of the Western Empire (476), the barbarian kingdoms are often Arian; the baptism of Clovis, king of the Franks, c. 500, brings the most powerful kingdom over to Catholicism. The bishops, heirs of the Roman administration, and the monks, who follow the Rule of Benedict of Nursia (c. 530), preserve books and evangelize the countryside; Irish and then Anglo-Saxon missionaries win over Germany. With Gregory the Great (590-604), the papacy becomes the centre of the Latin West. The coronation of Charlemagne by the pope, in Rome, on Christmas Day 800, founds the alliance of Empire and Church; the Carolingian renaissance unifies liturgy and script.

#### The Gregorian reform and conquering Christendom

In the eleventh century, the reforming papacy seeks to free the Church from lay powers: Gregory VII forbids the investiture of bishops by princes and humiliates the emperor Henry IV at Canossa (1077); the conflict ends with the Concordat of Worms (1122). The Church equips itself with a law, canon law, a theology, scholasticism, and a capital. The break with Constantinople (1054) and the crusades (1095-1291) give Latin Christendom its outer borders; Cluny, Cîteaux, then the mendicant orders, Franciscans (1209) and Dominicans (1216), renew religious life. The Fourth Lateran Council (1215) requires every believer to confess once a year and defines transubstantiation: Christendom is henceforth a discipline of consciences.

#### Heresies, Inquisition, the stake

The same period invents organized exclusion. The Cathars of Languedoc and the Waldensians are fought first by preaching, then by the Albigensian Crusade (1209-1229). In 1231 Gregory IX entrusts the search for heresy to delegated judges, often Dominicans: this is the Inquisition, a procedure of inquiry with denunciation, interrogation, torture (authorized in 1252), and the handing over of relapsed heretics to the secular arm, which burns them. Jews are marked out and expelled from England (1290), France (1306, 1394) and Spain (1492). The Spanish Inquisition, created in 1478 at the request of the Catholic Monarchs, is an institution of the state, led by Torquemada, and its autos-da-fé target converts suspected of judaizing; it is this Inquisition that Dostoevsky transposes to Seville. The stake accompanies the whole of the late Middle Ages: Jan Hus at Constance (1415), Joan of Arc at Rouen (1431), then Savonarola (1498) and Giordano Bruno (1600). Meister Eckhart, prosecuted in Cologne and then in Avignon (1326-1329), dies before the sentence; the bull *In agro dominico* condemns his propositions, not his person.

#### Crisis and piety

The fourteenth and fifteenth centuries are those of the Black Death (1347-1352), the Avignon papacy (1309-1377) and the Great Schism (1378-1417), in which two and then three popes contend for the Church. Piety becomes more inward, with Rhineland mysticism and the devotio moderna, and more anxious: Jean Delumeau has described this pastoral of fear, sin and death, which prepares the Reformation.

#### What the essay assumes

The essay mentions the bull of 1329 against Eckhart, the Inquisition of the Grand Inquisitor, the burnings at the stake and the Inquisitors of historical Christianity, and the Christendom that Kierkegaard sets against Christianity. It assumes the reader knows that the Law of Plato's *Laws* found in Christendom its most lasting realization, and that Eckhart's negative theology was judged by a tribunal.
