The Bible has never been read in a single way. Jews and Christians have sought in it, in turn, a law, a hidden meaning, a figure of Christ, a rule of life, and then, from the seventeenth century onwards, a document whose authors and dates had to be established.

#### Jewish readings

Rabbinic Judaism reads the Torah through midrash, an inquiry that scrutinizes every word: halakha draws norms from it, haggadah narratives and lessons. The kabbalists of the thirteenth century sum up four levels in the word Pardes, the orchard: the plain sense, the allusion, the homiletic interpretation and the secret.

#### Allegory, letter and charity

Philo of Alexandria, in the first century, distinguishes the literal sense, good for the multitude, from the figurative sense, in which Eden becomes virtue and the exodus the departure of the soul from the body. Origen systematizes three senses, the body, the soul and the spirit of the text, and holds certain passages to be impossible in the letter so as to force the reader towards the spirit. The school of Antioch, on the contrary, defends the literal and historical sense. Augustine, in *On Christian Doctrine*, lays down a rule: any interpretation that does not build up the love of God and of neighbour has not understood the text.

#### The four medieval senses

Cassian and then the medieval masters fix four senses of Scripture: literal, allegorical, moral or tropological, anagogical. A Latin distich of the thirteenth century sums them up: the letter teaches the facts, allegory what one must believe, the moral sense what one must do, anagogy what one must strive towards. Jerusalem is thus the city, the Church, the soul and the heavenly city. Thomas Aquinas requires the spiritual senses to rest on the literal one.

#### Luther and the clarity of Scripture

The Reformation lays down the principle of sola scriptura: Scripture alone has authority, it is clear in what concerns salvation, it interprets itself and every believer can read it, hence Luther's German translation (1522-1534). Luther distrusts allegory and looks for Christ everywhere.

#### The birth of criticism

Spinoza, in chapter VII of the *Theological-Political Treatise* (1670), wants to read Scripture as one reads nature: to establish the language, the history of each book, its author, its addressees, and to draw from it only what the text says, without bending reason to it as Maimonides did; in the following chapter, he concludes with Ibn Ezra that Moses did not write the Pentateuch, of which Ezra would be the compiler. The Oratorian Richard Simon, in the *Histoire critique du Vieux Testament* (1678), destroyed at Bossuet's instigation, attributes the text to successive public scribes. After Jean Astruc (1753), who distinguishes two documents in Genesis according to the divine name, German criticism culminates in Julius Wellhausen, whose *Prolegomena to the History of Israel* (1878) fix the documentary hypothesis: four sources, the Yahwist J, the Elohist E, the Deuteronomist D and the Priestly writing P, the last being the latest, after the exile. Since the 1970s the model has been contested, above all for J and E. On the side of the New Testament, Strauss introduces myth in 1835; Gunkel, Dibelius and Bultmann found form criticism; Bultmann proposes in 1941 to demythologize the message. The Catholic Church, after condemning modernism, accepts literary genres with Pius XII (1943), and at the Second Vatican Council the constitution Dei Verbum (1965) asks that the intention of the human authors be sought. Conversely, American fundamentalism, born at the end of the nineteenth century, holds the inerrancy of Scripture to be an article of faith.

#### A Muslim parallel

Averroes, in the twelfth century, distinguishes in the Qur'an an apparent sense and an inner sense reached through taʾwīl, interpretation, which is reserved for those capable of demonstration; this is the division, between the letter for the crowd and the meaning for the learned, that Spinoza rejects.

#### What the essay assumes

The essay describes in Philo the regime of allegory and its two senses, in Origen the three-level reading inherited from Philo, in Averroes taʾwīl and the three classes of men, in Spinoza the rejection of Maimonides' method as useless, dangerous and absurd, and in Strauss myth without intention; it takes as known the four medieval senses, sola scriptura and the documentary hypothesis, which it describes nowhere.
