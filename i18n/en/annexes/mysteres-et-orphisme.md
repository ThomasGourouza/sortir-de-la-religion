Alongside the public cults of the city, the Greek and Roman world knew mystery cults: secret initiation rites, open to the individuals who chose them, promising a personal relationship with a deity and often a better fate after death. They complement civic religion without replacing it, and they have held a large place in the debates on the originality of Christianity.

#### Eleusis

The mysteries of Eleusis, near Athens, celebrated Demeter and her daughter Persephone, also called Kore. The myth, told by the *Homeric Hymn to Demeter*, relates the abduction of Persephone by Hades, the grief of her mother, who makes the harvests perish, then the compromise of Zeus: the girl will spend part of the year under the earth and the other part with her mother, which has been read as the cycle of the seeds. The initiation, open to men and women, to free people and to slaves who spoke Greek, included a procession from Athens and nocturnal revelations in the hall of the Telesterion. The secret was so well kept that almost nothing is known of what was shown there; the initiates went away with the hope of a better life in the hereafter.

#### Orphism

Under the name of Orpheus, the Thracian singer who went down to the underworld to fetch Eurydice, poems and rules of life circulated. Their central narrative tells that the child Dionysus, sometimes called Zagreus, was torn apart and devoured by the Titans, whom Zeus struck with lightning; men would have been born from their ashes, mixing a guilty Titanic part and a divine part. Hence a doctrine of the soul exiled in the body as in a prison or a tomb, purifications, abstinence from meat, and the promise of escaping the cycle of births. Gold tablets found in tombs give the dead instructions for finding their way in the hereafter. Plato and the Neoplatonists owe much to these ideas.

#### The Oriental cults

In the Hellenistic and Roman period, cults coming from the East spread: Cybele, the Phrygian Great Mother, and her consort Attis, served by eunuch and begging priests; Isis, the Egyptian goddess who finds and revives Osiris, honoured by initiations that Apuleius's *Golden Ass* describes; Mithras, a god of Iranian origin worshipped in underground sanctuaries by soldiers and merchants.

#### The debate over Christian parallels

The history of religions school, at the turn of the twentieth century, explained part of Pauline Christianity by these cults: baptism and the meal as initiation rites, the Lord invoked as a saviour god, death and resurrection as a myth of a dying god. Frazer grouped Attis, Adonis and Osiris into a category of gods who die and are reborn with vegetation. Later work has strongly qualified these comparisons: the sources for the mysteries are often late, Frazer's category is disputed, and Judaism is enough to explain many Christian features.

#### What the essay assumes

The essay assumes familiarity with the mysteries to which Plotinus compares the ineffable union with the One, the mystery cults with which Bousset compares the invocation of the Lord, the god who dies and is reborn whom the mythicists make the model of Christ, and the priests of Cybele to whom Celsus compares the Christians.
