Titus Lucretius Carus (c. 98-55 BCE) is the author of a single book, the *De rerum natura*, *On the Nature of Things*, a poem in six books and more than seven thousand hexameters that sets out the physics and ethics of Epicurus to a Roman, Memmius. Almost nothing is known of his life; the legend of a love potion and a suicide comes from Jerome, four centuries later. Cicero is said to have revised the poem after his death.

#### The poem

Book I invokes Venus, the generative power of nature, then sets out the principles: nothing is born from nothing, nothing returns to nothingness, everything is atoms and void. Book II deals with the motion of atoms, their swerve, the clinamen, and the plurality of worlds; Book III with the soul, material and mortal, and with death, which is nothing to us; Book IV with sensation, simulacra and love, which Lucretius satirizes; Book V with the origin of the world, of living beings and of civilization, in a history of humanity without providence, from the first humans to cities, language and the arts; Book VI with celestial and terrestrial phenomena, lightning, volcanoes, the magnet, and it ends with the plague of Athens. The poem is a lesson in physics with a moral aim: knowing the causes frees us from the fear of the gods and of death, which is the source of the passions and of crimes.

#### The critique of religion

The *De rerum natura* is one of the most violent ancient texts against religion. From Book I onwards, Lucretius celebrates Epicurus as the Greek who first dared to raise his eyes against the religion that crushed humankind, trampled it underfoot and, by his victory, made us equal to heaven; he then recounts the sacrifice of Iphigenia by Agamemnon at Aulis, to conclude that religion could prompt so many evils. Book V explains the birth of belief in the gods by the visions of dreams, by ignorance of the causes of celestial phenomena and by fear, and shows men prostrating themselves before altars without finding peace there. Lucretius does not deny the gods, blessed and distant; he denies that they govern the world and that they are to be feared. True piety is to contemplate all things with a mind at peace.

#### Legacy

Little read in the Middle Ages, the poem is rediscovered in 1417 by Poggio Bracciolini in a German monastery, printed in 1473, and read by Montaigne, who quotes it abundantly, by Gassendi, who rehabilitates Epicurus, by the libertines, by Diderot and d'Holbach, by Hume, by Marx, whose dissertation deals with Democritus and Epicurus, and by Bergson, who published extracts from it. It has become the emblem of a materialist critique of religion, and the line on the evils of religion one of the most quoted formulas of ancient literature.

#### What the essay assumes

The essay quotes the lines of Book I on religion trampled underfoot, on the victory that makes us equal to heaven, in which it sees a divinization of man that announces Feuerbach, and on Iphigenia. It assumes familiarity with the plan of the poem, with the explanation of belief by fear and ignorance, which Hume, Freud, and Norris and Inglehart will rediscover, and with the fact that Lucretius, like Epicurus, leaves religion without leaving the divine, which the essay calls the first form of its paradox.
