Moses ben Maimon (1138-1204), Rambam for the Jewish tradition, Maimonides for the Latins, is born in Cordoba, flees the Almohad persecution, passes through Fez and Palestine and settles in Cairo, where he is physician to Saladin's vizier and head of the Jewish community. Jurist, philosopher and physician, he writes his philosophical works in Arabic and his code of the law in Hebrew.

#### The code and the law

The *Commentary on the Mishnah* (1168) contains the thirteen principles of faith, the first creed of Judaism, which affirm the existence, unity and incorporeality of God, prophecy, the superiority of Moses, the Torah as coming from heaven and immutable, retribution, the Messiah and the resurrection. The *Mishneh Torah* (c. 1180), in fourteen books, is the first complete code of Jewish law, written in clear Hebrew, without discussion or citation of sources: it is meant to make the law knowable without going through the Talmud, an ambition that earns it criticism. Its first book, the *Book of Knowledge*, places at the beginning of the law the knowledge of God through physics and metaphysics.

#### The Guide for the Perplexed

The *Guide* (c. 1190), written in Arabic for a disciple trained in philosophy and troubled by the letter of Scripture, the perplexed, aims to show that the Torah rightly read agrees with the science of Aristotle. The first part explains the terms of the Bible that seem to attribute a body or passions to God: they are homonyms, words with several meanings, whose bodily sense must be set aside. It develops the doctrine of negative attributes: one cannot say of God what he is, but only what he is not, and silence is the most fitting praise; the positive attributes of Scripture describe his actions, not his essence. The second part demonstrates the existence, unity and incorporeality of God from the principles of Aristotle, discusses the eternity of the world, which Maimonides judges indemonstrable either way and decides in favour of creation on the strength of revelation, and presents prophecy as a natural perfection of the intellect and the imagination, which God may withhold, Moses being the exception. The third deals with evil, with providence, proportioned to the intellect, with the book of Job, and with the reasons for the commandments: the sacrifices, in particular, are a pedagogical concession to people accustomed to pagan worship, intended to turn them away from it little by little. The introduction warns that the book contains deliberate contradictions, meant to hide from the vulgar what does not suit them.

#### Legacy

The *Guide*, translated into Hebrew as early as 1204 and into Latin in the thirteenth century, is read by Thomas Aquinas and by Eckhart, who cites Rabbi Moyses, and provokes within Judaism a century-long controversy over philosophy. Spinoza criticizes it in the *Theological-Political Treatise*, Leo Strauss makes it the model of esoteric writing, and Levinas finds in it a theology of attributes in the imperative.

#### What the essay assumes

The essay assumes familiarity with the addressee of the *Guide*, the method of homonyms, the negative theology of attributes, which it treats as an anticipation of the regime of experience, the historical explanation of the sacrifices, which it says is separated by a narrow step from an explanation without God, and the seventh cause of contradiction, Strauss's source. It confronts Maimonides, in a constructed confrontation, with Feuerbach, for whom negative theology is a subtle atheism, and holds that each is right for a different audience.
