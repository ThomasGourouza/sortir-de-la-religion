The Reformation is the rupture which, in the sixteenth century, divides Latin Christendom into rival Churches. It arises from a question of salvation, raised by a German monk, and within a generation becomes an affair of princes, cities and wars. Almost all the German, Danish and Swiss authors of the essay, from Kant to Bonhoeffer, are heirs of this rupture.

#### Luther

Martin Luther (1483-1546), an Augustinian friar and professor of Scripture at Wittenberg, posts or sends on 31 October 1517 ninety-five theses against the trade in indulgences. His doctrine fits into a few formulas: man is justified by faith alone, not by his works; by grace alone; Scripture alone has authority, neither tradition nor the pope; every baptized person is a priest. Excommunicated in 1521, placed under the ban of the Empire at the Diet of Worms, he translates the New Testament (1522) and then the Bible (1534) into German, and printing, invented some sixty years earlier, spreads his writings in tens of thousands of copies: the Reformation is the first mass movement of the age of print. Luther condemns the Peasants' Revolt (1525) and entrusts to the princes the organization of the territorial Churches; the Augsburg Confession (1530) fixes Lutheran doctrine.

#### Calvin and the Reformed tradition

Zwingli in Zurich, then John Calvin (1509-1564), a French jurist in exile, give the Reformation its second form. The *Institutes of the Christian Religion* (1536 in Latin, 1541 in French) systematizes the doctrine: the absolute sovereignty of God, predestination, a sense of the divine inscribed in every human being, a Church governed by pastors and elders. In Geneva, from 1541 until his death, Calvin makes the city a disciplined commonwealth; Michael Servetus, an antitrinitarian, is burned there in 1553. Calvinism wins over France (the Huguenots), the Netherlands, Scotland with John Knox, and nourishes English Puritanism. England follows its own path: Henry VIII makes himself head of the Church (1534), Elizabeth sets a compromise in 1559, Anglicanism. The radical reformers, Anabaptists and spiritualists, who reject infant baptism and any state Church, are persecuted on all sides.

#### Counter-Reformation and confessions

Rome responds with the Council of Trent (1545-1563), which reaffirms tradition, the sacraments and works, reforms the clergy and creates the seminaries; the Jesuits (1540) and the Index (1559) are its instruments. The Peace of Augsburg (1555) leaves each prince of the Empire the choice of his subjects' religion. Europe divides into confessions, each with its catechisms, its schools and its control of morals; historians speak of confessionalization. The wars that follow, in France, in the Netherlands, then the Thirty Years' War, are its consequence.

#### Legacy

Lutheranism becomes the state religion in the Scandinavian kingdoms: the Church of Denmark that Kierkegaard attacks in 1855 is that of the Reformation of 1536. Pietism in the seventeenth century, then the liberal theology of the nineteenth, from Schleiermacher to Harnack, and Barth's dialectical theology in the twentieth, are moments of Protestant history; Bonhoeffer is a Lutheran pastor. Weber sought in Calvinist asceticism the origin of the spirit of capitalism, and in Protestantism the last act of disenchantment.

#### What the essay assumes

The essay says that the Reformation broke the unity of Christendom, sets Kierkegaard against the established Church, cites, through Plantinga, Calvin's sense of the divine and connects Weber with Puritanism; it takes for granted the principles of faith alone and Scripture alone, and the difference between Lutherans and the Reformed.
