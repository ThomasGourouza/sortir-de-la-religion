The name new atheists, or New Atheism, is given to a group of English-speaking authors whose books, published between 2004 and 2007 after the attacks of 11 September 2001, attacked religion with a virulence and a commercial success unseen since the Enlightenment: Sam Harris, Richard Dawkins, Daniel Dennett and Christopher Hitchens, nicknamed the Four Horsemen. The essay treats them together with Onfray, of whom they are the other side, and places them in the poorest version of the regime of error.

#### The books

Sam Harris, trained in philosophy and then a neuroscientist, opens the series with *The End of Faith* (2004): faith, belief without evidence, is intrinsically dangerous, Islam especially, and religious moderation shields fanaticism; he will later defend a spirituality without religion based on meditation. Richard Dawkins, an Oxford biologist, author of *The Selfish Gene* and inventor of the word meme, publishes *The God Delusion* in 2006: the God hypothesis is a scientific hypothesis that is almost certainly false, the argument from the improbability of the designer, religion is a by-product of evolved dispositions, a virus of the mind, the religious education of children a form of abuse, and the atheist can be happy and moral. Daniel Dennett, a philosopher of mind, asks in *Breaking the Spell* (2006) that religion be studied as a natural phenomenon, by the cognitive sciences and evolution, and defends, with Dawkins, the word brights proposed in 2003 for naturalists. Christopher Hitchens, a journalist and polemicist, draws up in *God Is Not Great* (2007) the historical and moral indictment: religion poisons everything. Victor Stenger, A. C. Grayling, Michel Onfray in France, with the *Traité d'athéologie* (2005), and Ayaan Hirsi Ali are associated with them, and public debates, associations and advertising campaigns on buses make it a movement.

#### The common theses

New Atheism holds religion to be a set of false empirical propositions that science refutes; it judges faith harmful in itself, and not only in its abuses; it rejects the respect owed to beliefs and the non-overlapping magisteria proposed by Stephen Jay Gould; it grounds morality in evolution and reason; and it claims the legacy of the Enlightenment, of Hume, of Russell and of Clifford. It is indebted to evolutionary biology and the cognitive sciences, Boyer, Atran, whose results it simplifies.

#### The criticisms

They have come from every side: from believers, Alister McGrath, John Lennox, who point to a caricature theology; from philosophers, Michael Ruse, Thomas Nagel, who judge the arguments threadbare; from the left, Terry Eagleton, for whom Dawkins does not know what he is talking about and serves Islamophobia; from sociologists, who see in it a scientism and one more secular religion, with its prophets and its heretics; and from within, as controversies over sexism and politics divided the movement after 2011. New Atheism ebbed in the 2010s, leaving an organized atheist culture, mostly American.

#### What the essay assumes

The essay assumes familiarity with the four authors and their books, which it does not quote, the thesis of religion as scientific error, the legacy of Clifford and Russell, and the link with the cognitive sciences. It sees in them the poorest version of the regime of error, Reason and Science set up as essences in Stirner's sense, the men of the marketplace of § 125, and against their Clifford-style crusades it sets the combination of James and Clifford: the problem is not belief, it is the Inquisitor.
