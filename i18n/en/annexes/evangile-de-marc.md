The shortest of the four Gospels is also, according to almost all exegetes, the oldest. In sixteen chapters, at a hurried pace, it tells of Jesus' activity in Galilee, his journey up to Jerusalem and his death, and it stops before an empty tomb.

#### An anonymous text, around 70

The tradition that attributes it to Mark goes back to Papias, bishop of Hierapolis at the beginning of the second century, quoted by Eusebius: Mark is said to have been Peter's interpreter and to have written down his recollections, without order. Historians regard this notice as uncertain and speak of an anonymous Greek-speaking author, writing for non-Jewish readers to whom he explains customs and translates Aramaic words. The most common dating places it around 70, just before or just after the capture of Jerusalem by Titus, since the discourse of chapter 13 announces the ruin of the Temple. The place, Rome according to tradition, or Syria, remains disputed.

#### The plan

The narrative opens with neither infancy nor genealogy, with John the Baptist in the desert, the baptism of Jesus in the Jordan and the temptation in a single sentence. Galilee follows: the calling of the disciples, exorcisms, healings, meals with tax collectors, controversies with the Pharisees over the sabbath and purity, parables of the Kingdom, the multiplication of the loaves. At the centre of the book, at Caesarea Philippi (Mc 8, 27-30), Peter recognizes Jesus as the Messiah; Jesus orders him to be silent and immediately announces that the Son of Man must suffer. The journey up to Jerusalem is punctuated by three predictions of the Passion, each time misunderstood. The Passion narrative (Mc 14 and 15) takes up nearly a third of the book. On Easter morning, women find the tomb open; a young man in white tells them that Jesus is risen and sends them to Galilee; they flee, seized with fear, and say nothing to anyone (Mc 16, 8). There the text of the oldest manuscripts stops. The longer ending (Mc 16, 9-20) is a second-century addition.

#### Distinctive features

Mark's Jesus acts more than he speaks; the adverb immediately recurs dozens of times. He is fully human: he grows angry, is astonished, does not know the day of the end, prays in anguish at Gethsemane and dies crying out his abandonment by God. His disciples understand nothing and all desert him. He calls himself the Son of Man, a title drawn from Daniel 7, and imposes silence on the demons, on those he heals and on Peter. William Wrede, in 1901, named this feature the messianic secret and saw in it a construction of the evangelist meant to explain why Jesus had not been recognized as Messiah in his lifetime; the hypothesis, though disputed, left its mark on twentieth-century exegesis.

#### Composition and reading

Mark assembles units first transmitted orally, which he links with summaries and indications of place. The two-source theory, dominant since the nineteenth century, makes him the common source of Matthew and Luke, who take over most of his text while correcting it. Long held to be an abridgement of Matthew, as Augustine claimed, it was rehabilitated by German criticism, which saw in its priority the way of access to the Jesus of history, before Wrede showed that it too is a work of faith and Schweitzer, in 1906, drew from its eschatology a Jesus who was a prophet of the end.

#### What the essay assumes

In the section on the existence of Jesus, the essay places Mark, around 70, at the threshold of the series of Gospels running from Mark to John, and recalls that these are texts of faith composed for communities. It sets out the thesis of Strauss, for whom the Gospel narratives are myths formed without intention in a community awaiting the Messiah, and that of Bauer, who on the contrary makes the evangelist Mark the conscious author on whom the others depend. It quotes Renan seeing Galilee as a fifth Gospel and recovering, through the narratives of Matthew and Mark, a human figure. It takes for granted the order in which the Gospels were composed and the fact that Mark has neither an infancy narrative nor an appearance of the risen Jesus.
