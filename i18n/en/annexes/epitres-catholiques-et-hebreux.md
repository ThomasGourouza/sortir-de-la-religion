Alongside the gospels and the letters of Paul, the New Testament contains eight shorter and less read letters. Seven of them have been called, since Eusebius, the Catholic Epistles, that is, universal, because they are addressed to all the Churches and not to a single community; the eighth, the Epistle to the Hebrews, was long counted among Paul's.

#### Hebrews: Christ the high priest

The text is anonymous; Origen already said that God alone knows who wrote it. It is a learned homily, in the most polished Greek of the New Testament, composed before 95, the date at which Clement of Rome quotes it. Its thesis is that Christ surpasses all the former mediators: the angels, Moses, the priests of the line of Levi. He is high priest according to the order of Melchizedek, the priest-king whom Genesis has meet Abraham and whom Psalm 110 evokes, and he has entered once for all into the heavenly sanctuary with his own blood. This single sacrifice renders unnecessary the sacrifices that the Temple repeated every year (He 7 to 10) and inaugurates the new covenant announced by Jeremiah. Chapter 11 unfolds the gallery of the ancients who believed without seeing the promise.

#### James: faith without works

Attributed to James, whom tradition identifies with the brother of the Lord, the epistle is a series of wisdom exhortations close to the Sermon on the Mount: trial, the poor given preference in the assembly, the tongue that sets ablaze, the stolen wages of the harvesters. Its second chapter maintains that faith without works is dead and that Abraham was justified by works, which seems to contradict Rm 3 and 4; many see in it a response to a distorted reading of Paul. Luther, in 1522, judged it an epistle of straw compared with the writings that preach Christ, and relegated it to the end of his New Testament together with Hebrews, Jude and Revelation.

#### Peter, John, Jude

The First Epistle of Peter is addressed to the elect strangers scattered through Asia Minor: exile, unjust suffering in the image of Christ, and the royal priesthood of the baptized (1 P 2, 9). The Second Epistle of Peter, which takes up Jude, is the latest text of the canon, from around 120 to 150: it answers the scoffers who ask where the promise of the Lord's coming is, and recalls that a thousand years are to him as one day. The three letters of John, close to the fourth gospel, affirm that God is love, denounce antichrists who have gone out from the community and require that Christ be confessed as come in the flesh. Jude, brief and vehement, quotes the book of Enoch.

#### Pseudepigraphy

Writing under the name of a master was common in Jewish and Greek antiquity: the Pythagorean schools, Daniel, Enoch. That 1 and 2 Peter, James or Jude are probably pseudepigraphical tells us about their date and milieu; it does not imply that their content is worthless or that their traditions are late. Ehrman nonetheless judges the practice closer to forgery than classical criticism admits.

#### Readings

The quarrel over faith and works set, in the sixteenth century, the Lutheran reading of Romans against James, which the Council of Trent invoked in its decree on justification (1547). Girard, in *Things Hidden since the Foundation of the World* (1978), rejected Hebrews as the text that reintroduced a sacrificial reading of the Passion; he went back on this judgement in the 1990s and admitted that the epistle conceives sacrifice in a new sense, that of the gift of self which puts an end to sacrifices.

#### What the essay assumes

The essay reports that Girard long refused to call the Passion a sacrifice, then accepted the word in a new sense after his exchange with Raymund Schwager: the vocabulary at stake is that of Hebrews, which the essay does not name. It reads in Paul, in Rm 12, 1, the spiritualized sacrifice; in Weber, a Puritanism that abolishes the sacraments as means of salvation; in Barth, religion defined as an attempt to justify oneself by works, rites and thoughts.
