Michel Foucault (1926-1984), a graduate of the École normale supérieure, agrégé in philosophy, trained as a psychologist, professor at Clermont-Ferrand, Tunis and Vincennes, then at the Collège de France from 1970 until his death, and an activist for prisoners and minority struggles, renewed the history of knowledge, of power and of the subject, and devoted his last years to Antiquity and to the techniques of the self, where he encounters the question of spirituality.

#### The archaeology of knowledge

*History of Madness* (1961) shows how the great confinement of the seventeenth century constituted madness as an object before psychiatry medicalized it. *The Birth of the Clinic* (1963), *The Order of Things* (1966), an unexpected success, and *The Archaeology of Knowledge* (1969) describe epistemes, historical configurations that make possible the knowledge of an age, Renaissance, classical age, modernity, and announce that man, a recent invention, might be erased like a face drawn in sand. This archaeology seeks neither the authors nor the truth of statements, but the rules of their formation; it was classed with structuralism, which Foucault rejected.

#### The genealogy of power

With *The Order of Discourse* (inaugural lecture of 1970, published in 1971) and *Discipline and Punish* (1975), Foucault moves, in Nietzsche's wake, to genealogy: modern power is not only repressive and sovereign, it is disciplinary, it produces docile bodies through surveillance, examination and the norm, of which Bentham's panopticon is the diagram, and it runs through forms of knowledge, power-knowledge. *The Will to Knowledge* (1976), the first volume of the *History of Sexuality*, overturns the repressive hypothesis: sex has been put into discourse, and biopower manages populations. The Collège de France lectures, *Society Must Be Defended*, *Security, Territory, Population*, *The Birth of Biopolitics*, forge the notion of governmentality, the conduct of conducts, and analyse Christian pastoral power and liberalism.

#### The care of the self and parrhesia

The last volumes, *The Use of Pleasure* and *The Care of the Self* (1984), and *Confessions of the Flesh*, posthumous (2018), shift the inquiry to Antiquity and the first Christian centuries: how does the subject constitute itself through techniques of the self, practices of ascesis, examination and confession? *The Hermeneutics of the Subject* (lectures of 1982) contrasts the care of the self, epimeleia heautou, with the precept know thyself, defines spirituality as the transformations the subject must carry out on itself in order to gain access to truth, and places at the Cartesian moment the separation of philosophy from spirituality. The last lecture courses, *The Government of Self and Others* and *The Courage of Truth* (1984), study parrhesia, courageous truth-telling, from Athenian democracy to the Cynics, whose scandalous life is a manifestation of truth. Hadot, whom Foucault had read, would criticize him for an aesthetics of existence too close to dandyism.

#### What the essay assumes

The essay takes as known the definition of spirituality of 6 January 1982, the care of the self, the Cartesian moment, which it paraphrases for want of a reliable page, parrhesia and the attested disagreement with Hadot. It borrows the definition from Foucault and the description from Hadot, notes that Foucault does not say what the true is or whether it goes beyond the subject, and uses the Cartesian moment to explain why modern critique targeted religion as belief.
