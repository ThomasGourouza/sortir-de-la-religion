The word Bible comes from the Greek ta biblia, the books, in the plural: what goes by that name is a library composed over nearly a millennium, not a book written in one stroke. Almost all the authors of the essay, from Philo to Spinoza and Leo Strauss, relate to this library, but they read it neither in the same version nor in the same language.

#### The Tanakh and its three parts

The Hebrew Bible, which Judaism calls the Tanakh after the initials of its three parts, comprises the Torah, the Prophets and the Writings. The Torah, which the Greeks called the Pentateuch, the five scrolls, runs from Genesis to Deuteronomy and tells of the creation, the patriarchs, the exodus from Egypt and the giving of the Law. The Prophets bring together the historical books, from Joshua to Kings, and the prophets proper, from Isaiah to the twelve minor prophets. The Writings run from the Psalms to Chronicles, with Job, Qoheleth and Daniel. The Jewish canon numbers twenty-four books by the traditional count; its closure is gradual, the Torah being regarded as fixed from the Persian period, while some of the Writings were still debated at the beginning of the common era.

#### The Septuagint and the Vulgate

In Alexandria, from the third century BCE, the Greek-speaking Jewish community translates the Torah, then the other books: this is the Septuagint, named after the legend of its seventy-two translators. It contains books absent from the Hebrew Bible, Tobit, Judith, Wisdom, Sirach, Baruch and the two books of Maccabees: Catholics call them deuterocanonical, Protestants apocryphal. Philo reads it, and the New Testament quotes it most often. At the end of the fourth century, Jerome retranslates the Old Testament into Latin from the Hebrew and revises the Gospels; his version, which became the Vulgate, is the Bible of the medieval West, and the Council of Trent declares it authentic in 1546.

#### Four canons

The confessions do not agree on the extent of the Old Testament. The Protestant canon follows the Hebrew Bible, thirty-nine books by its count, Luther having set the deuterocanonical books apart; the Catholic canon numbers forty-six; the Orthodox Churches accept a few more, among them the third book of Maccabees and Psalm 151. The major confessions agree on the twenty-seven books of the New Testament, whose list was settled in the fourth century.

#### The languages

The Old Testament is written in Hebrew, with a few chapters in Aramaic in Daniel and Ezra; several deuterocanonical books survive only in Greek. The New Testament is entirely in common Greek, the koine, whereas Jesus spoke Aramaic.

#### Composition and critical reading

Most exegetes date the writing of the texts between the Israelite monarchy, in the ninth and eighth centuries BCE, and the Hellenistic period, with a decisive moment in the Persian period, after the exile in Babylon, when the Torah takes its written form. The setting down in writing is late: apart from two small silver amulets from Ketef Hinnom, c. 600 BCE, which bear the priestly blessing, the oldest biblical manuscripts are the Qumran scrolls, from the third century BCE to the first century CE. The documentary hypothesis, formulated in the nineteenth century, explains the doublets and variations of the Pentateuch by the combination of several sources; its current forms and its critics are presented in the article on readings of the Bible.

#### What the essay assumes

The essay recalls that Philo reads the Bible in the Greek translation of the Septuagint and comments on it alongside the *Timaeus*; it presents the *Theological-Political Treatise* as the book in which Spinoza subjects Scripture to historical criticism, against Maimonides' method of reading, and says that the exit from religion begins there with a quarrel between readers; it places the birth of Christianity between Athens and Jerusalem, following Tertullian, and gives this subtitle to Leo Strauss, for whom revelation and philosophy do not refute each other. It therefore assumes it known that there are several Bibles, in several languages, and that the question of who wrote it and how to read it comes before any critique of religion.
