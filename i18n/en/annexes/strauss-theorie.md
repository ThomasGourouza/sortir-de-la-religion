David Friedrich Strauss (1808-1874), a pupil of the Tübingen seminary and of Ferdinand Christian Baur, and a tutor there, published at twenty-seven a *Life of Jesus* that deprived him for ever of any chair and made him, in Germany, the symbol of learned unbelief. A journalist, a biographer, a deputy in 1848, he ended as a bourgeois freethinker, mocked by Nietzsche.

#### The Life of Jesus

*Das Leben Jesu, kritisch bearbeitet* (1835-1836), in two volumes, examines the gospel narratives one by one and applies to them a new alternative. The rationalists, like Paulus, explained the miracles by misunderstood natural causes; the supernaturalists held them to be true; Strauss maintains that neither is right, because these narratives are myths: not impostures, but unintentional creations, absichtslos, of the early Christian community, which clothed Jesus in the features expected of the Messiah according to the Old Testament, virgin birth, miracles, transfiguration, resurrection. The concept of myth, borrowed from the study of antiquity and already applied to the Old Testament, is thus extended to the New, and the Gospel of John loses all historical value. There remain a historical Jesus, a Galilean preacher of whom little is known, and a philosophical conclusion of Hegelian inspiration: the predicates the Church attributes to Christ, the union of the divine and the human, death, resurrection, do not belong to an individual but to the idea of the human species; humanity is God made man. The French translation is by Littré, the English by George Eliot.

#### What followed

After the controversies, Strauss published *Die christliche Glaubenslehre* (1840-1841), a Christian dogmatics that shows the dogmas dissolving through their own history, lost a chair at Zurich before he had even taken it up, wrote biographies, *Ulrich von Hutten*, *Voltaire*, and a *Life of Jesus for the German People* (1864) in reply to Renan. His last book, *Der alte und der neue Glaube*, *The Old Faith and the New* (1872), answers four questions: are we still Christians? no; do we still have a religion? yes, a feeling of dependence on the universe; how do we understand the world? through Darwin and science; how do we order our lives? through common morality, the nation and the classics, Lessing, Goethe, Beethoven. Nietzsche, in 1873, made him the type of the Bildungsphilister, the cultivated philistine, who has given up God without changing anything in his life.

#### Legacy

Strauss opened the way to the whole criticism of the gospels, Baur, Renan, Bultmann, and his idea of myth without an author distinguishes his position from that of Bauer, for whom the evangelists invented consciously, and from that of the mythicists of the twentieth century, who deny the existence of Jesus. His conclusion about the human species is Feuerbach's starting point.

#### What the essay assumes

The essay assumes familiarity with the thesis of unintentional myth, the conclusion that humanity is God made man, in which it sees Feuerbach's starting point, the role of George Eliot, *The Old Faith and the New* and Nietzsche's attested response. It ranks Strauss among the transfers of the divine onto humanity, and places his position between that of Bauer and that of the mythicists.
