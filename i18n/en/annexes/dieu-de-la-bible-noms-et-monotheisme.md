The God whom the Bible calls by several names was not conceived from the outset as the one God of the philosophers. Historians reconstruct a long history, from the god of one people among other gods to the one and invisible God, and this history is itself inscribed in the texts.

#### The names

The Hebrew Bible first uses El, the common noun for god in the Semitic languages and the proper name of the head of the Canaanite pantheon, and Elohim, a plural form construed with a singular verb. The proper name of the God of Israel is the tetragrammaton, the four consonants YHWH; its pronunciation, probably Yahweh, ceased to be spoken in the Second Temple period, and Jewish readers replace it with Adonai, the Lord, which the Septuagint translates as Kyrios and French Bibles as the Eternal or the Lord. The story of the burning bush links this name to the verb to be, an enigmatic formula that has nourished the whole of Christian metaphysics. To these are added titles: Shaddai, Sabaoth, the god of hosts, the Most High.

#### A god among the gods

Several texts assume the existence of other gods: the Decalogue forbids having others before the God of Israel, a psalm shows God judging in the midst of the gods, and Jephthah speaks of the god of the Ammonites as a rival. Historians call this worship of a single god without denial of the others monolatry. Archaeology sheds light on it: inscriptions from Kuntillet Ajrud, c. 800 BCE, mention Yahweh and his Asherah, and the prophets fight cults of Baal practised in Israel. Monotheism proper, the assertion that there is no other god, is expressed clearly in Second Isaiah, during the exile, when Yahweh is said to be the creator of all things, of light and of darkness.

#### Images and the ban on images

The Bible forbids representing God, yet it describes him constantly: shepherd, king, judge, husband, father, warrior, potter; he walks in the garden, repents, grows angry. At the same time it asserts that no one can see his face and live, that Moses sees him only from behind, and that he is a hidden God. This tension between the anthropomorphism of the stories and the transcendence they affirm lies at the heart of every learned reading.

#### From the biblical God to the God of the philosophers

Philo reads the name from the bush as being itself and makes God the unknowable, of whom only the Logos is within reach. Maimonides devotes the opening of the *Guide for the Perplexed* to showing that the bodily terms applied to God are homonyms and that only negative attributes are fitting. The Fathers and the scholastics identify the God of the Bible with the first principle of Aristotle and Plotinus. Pascal, in 1654, on the contrary sets the God of Abraham, Isaac and Jacob against the God of the philosophers and scholars. Historians of monotheism, from Hume to Jan Assmann, have finally discussed its link with intolerance.

#### What the essay assumes

The essay cites, in Pascal, the hidden God of Isaiah and the God of Abraham set against that of the philosophers; it sets out, in Maimonides, the homonyms and the negative attributes; it reports, in Hume, monotheism as a late product of fear, intolerant because a single god suffers no rivals; it cites, in Levinas, the idea that monotheism, measured against the mythical gods, is nothing but atheism, and, in Weber, the return of a polytheism of values. It assumes the reader knows the history that leads from the god of one people to the one God.
