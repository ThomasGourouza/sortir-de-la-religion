Plato, who drives the poets out of his ideal city, is also the greatest inventor of myths in philosophy. His dialogues contain some twenty narratives, some taken from tradition, others forged for the occasion, which come in where argument stops: the origin of the world, the fate of the soul after death, the foundation of the city. Their status has been debated since Antiquity: pedagogical ornaments, likely truths, or political instruments.

#### The myths of the soul

At the end of the *Republic*, the Pamphylian soldier Er, left for dead on the battlefield, comes back to life and recounts what he saw: the judgement of souls, the rewards and punishments of a thousand years, then the choice each soul makes of its next life, under the gaze of the Moirai, before drinking the water of Lethe. The narrative makes each person the author of his fate: the god is not responsible, responsibility belongs to the one who chooses. In the *Phaedrus*, the soul is compared to a winged team, a charioteer driving two horses, one docile, the other unruly, which rises to contemplate the intelligible realities or falls back towards the body. The *Gorgias* and the *Phaedo* also have their myths of the judgement of the dead.

#### The myths of the city

The *Protagoras* tells that Prometheus, having given fire and the arts to men, could not give them the political art; Zeus sent Hermes to distribute to all of them shame and justice, without which cities cannot last. In the *Republic*, Gyges, a shepherd who finds a ring that makes him invisible, seduces the queen and kills the king: the narrative raises the question of whether anyone would remain just without fear of being seen. The same dialogue proposes to the founders of the city a noble lie, the myth of the metals: all the citizens would be born of the earth, brothers, but the god would have mixed gold into the soul of the rulers, silver into that of the guardians, iron and bronze into that of the producers. The *Timaeus* and the *Critias*, finally, tell of Atlantis, a maritime power swallowed up after being defeated by primitive Athens.

#### The myths of love and of the world

In the *Symposium*, Aristophanes tells that the first humans were double, round, with four arms and four legs; Zeus cut them in two to weaken them, and each has been looking for his other half ever since. The *Timaeus* describes a demiurge who shapes the world by looking at an eternal model, a narrative that Philo and the Fathers will read as a philosophical version of creation.

#### The readings

The Neoplatonists read these myths as allegories of a higher truth. Karl Popper saw in the noble lie the proof of an authoritarian project, which others dispute. Leo Strauss read in it an illustration of writing on two levels, one for the multitude, the other for the philosophers.

#### What the essay assumes

The essay assumes familiarity with Plato's ambivalence, the legislator of the *Laws* who punishes impiety and the thinker of conversion who tells of the cave, and with the idea that useful narratives can be imposed on those who do not philosophize, an idea it finds again in Averroes, in Leo Strauss and in the Grand Inquisitor.
