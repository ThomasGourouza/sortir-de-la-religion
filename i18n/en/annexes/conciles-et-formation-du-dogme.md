Early Christianity had neither a fixed creed nor a closed Scripture. Between the second and the fifth century, it gave itself a canon, a rule of faith and dogmatic definitions, in councils convened by the emperors, at the end of quarrels that lasted for generations. This work was carried out with the vocabulary of Greek philosophy.

#### The canon and the rule of faith

The communities of the first century read the Jewish Bible and a few letters of apostles. Around 140, Marcion, who rejects the Old Testament and keeps only a Luke and ten letters of Paul, both expurgated, forces the Church to define what it receives. Irenaeus of Lyon, c. 180, defends the four gospels and a rule of faith handed down through the succession of bishops. The list of the twenty-seven books of the New Testament becomes stable in the fourth century; the festal letter of Athanasius, in 367, gives the first complete enumeration of it. The word heresy, which in Greek means choice or school, first designates any current of thought; with Irenaeus and Tertullian it becomes the name of deviation. Walter Bauer argued in 1934 that in many regions the forms later called heretical had preceded orthodoxy, which would thus be the result of a victory and not an origin.

#### The Trinity: Nicaea and Constantinople

The central question is that of Christ: how can Jesus be God if God is one? The theology of the Logos, in Justin and Origen, made the Son a second God, subordinate to the Father. Around 318, Arius, a priest of Alexandria, draws the consequence: the Son is a creature, and there was a time when he was not. The Council of Nicaea (325), convened by Constantine, condemns him and proclaims the Son consubstantial (*homoousios*) with the Father, begotten, not made. The word, foreign to Scripture, divides the East for fifty years; the emperors back the parties in turn, and Athanasius is exiled five times. The Cappadocian Fathers, Basil of Caesarea, Gregory of Nazianzus and Gregory of Nyssa, find the formula that prevails: one essence (*ousia*), three hypostases. The Council of Constantinople (381) extends it to the Holy Spirit; the creed known as the Niceno-Constantinopolitan Creed is its expression.

#### The Incarnation: Ephesus and Chalcedon

It remains to say how the divine and the human are united in Christ. Nestorius, patriarch of Constantinople, refuses to call Mary mother of God and sharply distinguishes the two natures; Cyril of Alexandria has him condemned at Ephesus (431). Eutyches falls into the opposite excess, a single nature after the union. The Council of Chalcedon (451) defines a single person in two natures, without confusion or separation; the Churches of Egypt, Syria and Armenia, which reject this formula, separate for good. Origen himself, three centuries after his death, is condemned for some of his theses (553).

#### Greek philosophy and dogma

Essence, hypostasis, nature, person, consubstantial: the terms of dogma come from Plato, Aristotle, the Stoics and Plotinus. The Fathers defend themselves by saying that they name a revealed mystery with human words; their pagan adversaries, Celsus and Porphyry, and later the moderns, see in this the proof that Christianity thought itself in Greek. Dogma also has a political face: from Theodosius onwards, adherence to the Nicene creed is compulsory, and the heretic is an offender.

#### What the essay assumes

The essay follows the Logos from Philo to the incarnate Word of John and Augustine, and notes that Augustine found the Logos among the Platonists but not the Incarnation; it calls the fusion of the God of the philosophers and the God of the Bible the major fact of the intellectual history of the West. It mentions that Jaspers reads the Trinity as a set of ciphers, and that rational theology was armed by the state. It assumes the reader knows the names of the councils and the meaning of the words consubstantial, heresy and orthodoxy.
