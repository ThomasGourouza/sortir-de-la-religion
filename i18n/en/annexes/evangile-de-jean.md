The fourth Gospel differs from the other three in its language, its plan and its theology: it begins before creation, with the Word beside God, and presents a Jesus who says openly who he is. It is through this Gospel that the Greek vocabulary of the Logos entered the Christian faith.

#### A milieu, successive stages

The text claims the authority of a witness, the disciple whom Jesus loved, never named, whom the tradition of the second century identifies with the apostle John, writing at Ephesus in his old age. Historians prefer to speak of a Johannine milieu, a community whose founding figure this disciple would have been, and distinguish several stages of composition, of which chapter 21 is an appendix. The final redaction is placed around 90 to 100; a papyrus copied in Egypt in the first half of the second century shows that the book circulated early.

#### The prologue

Eighteen verses open the book like a hymn. In the beginning was the Word, the Logos; he was with God, he was God; all things were made through him; in him was life, and the life was the light of men (Jn 1, 1-5). Then the Word became flesh and dwelt among us (Jn 1, 14). The vocabulary meets that of personified Wisdom in the wisdom books and that of Philo of Alexandria, for whom the Logos is the intermediary between God and the world; the affirmation of the incarnation has no equivalent in either.

#### Signs and discourses

John reports neither parables nor exorcisms, but seven signs chosen for their meaning: the water changed into wine at Cana, the multiplication of the loaves, the man born blind, the raising of Lazarus (Jn 11), which leads the authorities to resolve on his death. The signs come with long discourses: to Nicodemus on being born from above, to the Samaritan woman on living water, to the crowd on the bread of life, to the Pharisees on the good shepherd. In the I am sayings, Jesus declares himself the bread of life, the light of the world, the resurrection and the life. The farewell discourse (Jn 13 to 17) promises the Spirit.

#### The Passion and the appearances

The Johannine Jesus remains master of events: he gives himself up, carries his cross alone, entrusts his mother to the disciple and dies saying that all is accomplished. The dialogue with Pilate concerns kingship and truth. On Easter morning, Mary Magdalene finds the tomb empty, then recognizes Jesus when he calls her by name. In the evening he appears to the disciples; Thomas, who is absent, declares that he will not believe without putting his finger into the wounds. Eight days later Jesus invites him to do so; Thomas confesses him as his Lord and his God, and Jesus calls blessed those who believe without having seen (Jn 20, 24-29).

#### Composition and reading

Compared with the synoptics, John spreads the ministry over three Passovers, places the cleansing of the Temple at the beginning rather than the end, has Jesus die on the eve of Passover, at the hour when the lambs are slaughtered, and gives the teaching a meditative rather than proverbial form. Clement of Alexandria called it the spiritual Gospel. The gnostics were the first to comment on it, Origen and Augustine made it the book of the theology of the Word, Bultmann read a gnostic milieu in it; recent scholarship stresses its Jewish roots.

#### What the essay assumes

The essay quotes Jn 1, 1 and Jn 1, 14 in the section on Philo: the concept of the Logos was available to the Jewish philosopher, but the sentence on the Word made flesh is what Philo could never have written, and it is through this encounter that Christianity entered philosophy. In the section on Hume, Caravaggio's painting of the incredulity of Thomas illustrates the faith that demands proof, set against the question of testimony to miracles. Part Ten, which opposes the God of the philosophers to a God before whom one can pray and dance, takes for granted that it was from John's Word that the Fathers united biblical faith with the Greek vocabulary of being.
