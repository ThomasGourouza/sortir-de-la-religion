Historical-critical exegesis reads the Bible as a body of ancient documents, with the methods of history and philology, without presupposing its inspiration. Applied to the Gospels, it has produced over two centuries several quests for the Jesus of history, whose results the essay summarizes in its section on the existence of Jesus.

#### The method

Historical criticism took shape in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, with Spinoza, Richard Simon and the German scholars, then settled in the nineteenth into a set of procedures: textual criticism, which establishes the text from the manuscripts; source criticism, which identifies the documents an author used, and which established the priority of Mark and the probable existence of a sayings source, called Q, behind Matthew and Luke; form criticism, Formgeschichte, which, with Bultmann and Dibelius around 1920, classifies the units of the oral tradition according to their genre and their function in the communities; redaction criticism, which studies the particular theology of each evangelist; and finally the literary, sociological and narrative approaches of recent decades. The quests for the historical Jesus add criteria of authenticity: multiple attestation; embarrassment, for what the first Christians had no interest in inventing, such as the baptism by John or the crucifixion; dissimilarity; coherence; plausibility within first-century Judaism.

#### The three quests

The first quest begins with Reimarus, whose fragments Lessing published from 1774: Jesus was, on this account, a political messianic claimant, and his disciples invented the resurrection. Strauss, in 1835, puts myth in the place of imposture; Renan, in 1863, writes a historical novel; the liberal lives of the end of the century make Jesus a teacher of morality. Schweitzer, in 1906, closes this period by showing that each author painted his own face, and that the real Jesus is an apocalyptic prophet awaiting the imminent end of the world. A period of scepticism follows: Bultmann judges that almost nothing can be known of the life of Jesus and that only the preaching, the kerygma, matters. The second quest opens in 1953 with Ernst Käsemann, his pupil, who argues that a continuity between the Jesus of history and the Christ of faith is both necessary and possible. The third quest, from the 1980s, places Jesus back in the Judaism of his time and draws on archaeology and the Qumran texts: E. P. Sanders makes him a prophet of the restoration of Israel, John P. Meier a marginal Jew whose life he reconstructs in five volumes, Geza Vermes a Galilean hasid, the American Jesus Seminar a subversive sage, a minority reading that Sanders and Meier contest.

#### The results and the mythicist thesis

On the existence of Jesus, agreement is almost unanimous among believing, agnostic and atheist historians: a Jew from Galilee, baptized by John, a preacher of the reign of God, crucified under Pontius Pilate around 30, attested by Paul as early as the 50s, by the Gospels, by Josephus and by Tacitus. The mythicist thesis, sketched by Bruno Bauer, then put forward by Arthur Drews in 1909 and Paul-Louis Couchoud in 1924, and taken up by Richard Carrier in 2014, remains marginal; Bart Ehrman, an agnostic, refuted it in 2012. The disagreement concerns the portrait: apocalyptic or sapiential, and what can be said about the sayings and the miracles.

#### What the essay assumes

The essay takes for granted the vocabulary of the method, the criteria of authenticity, which it applies to the baptism and the crucifixion, the sequence of the three quests, from Reimarus to Schweitzer and then to Sanders and Meier, and the state of the debate on mythicism, which it places in the regime of error and, in the case of Drews, among the refoundations.
