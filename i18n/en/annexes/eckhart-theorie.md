Eckhart of Hochheim (c. 1260-1328), a Thuringian Dominican, twice master of theology in Paris, provincial of his order in Saxony, preacher in Strasbourg and Cologne, left a Latin body of work, scholastic in form, and a German one, sermons and treatises addressed to nuns and to lay people, which made him the father of Rhineland mysticism and of German philosophical prose.

#### God, the Godhead, being

In the Latin works, Eckhart comments on Scripture with the tools of scholasticism and holds that God is being itself, esse est Deus, and that creatures, in themselves, are pure nothingness, having only received being. In the German sermons he goes further: beyond God as he reveals himself in three persons and relates to creatures, there is the Godhead, Gottheit, a groundless ground, a desert, where there is neither Father nor Son nor Spirit, and where God and the soul are one. He takes up the negative theology of Dionysius and of Maimonides, whom he cites as Rabbi Moyses, and pushes it so far as to pray God to rid him of God, that is, of every representation of God that would still be a creature.

#### Detachment and the birth of God in the soul

The practical teaching comes down to one word, Abgeschiedenheit, detachment, to which Eckhart devotes a treatise: to empty oneself of every image, every will of one's own, every attachment, even to God as an object, so that God may be born in the soul. For there is in the soul a ground, a spark of the soul, Seelenfünklein, uncreated and uncreatable, where the Father begets his Son eternally, and what he begets is the soul itself become Son. Sermon 52, on the poor in spirit, defines true poverty: to will nothing, to know nothing, to have nothing, not even a place within oneself where God could act. The treatise *On the Noble Man*, Gelassenheit, the letting-be that follows from detachment, and the without-why of life, which lives because it lives, an image Silesius would take up with the rose, express the same doctrine, which does not despise action: Eckhart prefers Martha to Mary.

#### The trial

In 1326 the archbishop of Cologne opened inquisitorial proceedings against Eckhart, who defended himself, protested his orthodoxy and appealed to the pope. He died before the end of the trial, probably early in 1328. The bull *In agro dominico* of John XXII, dated 27 March 1329, condemned twenty-eight propositions drawn from his works, seventeen as heretical and eleven as suspect, noting that Eckhart had retracted before his death anything that might give rise to error. His work, read by Tauler and Suso, then by Nicholas of Cusa, was rediscovered in the nineteenth century by the Romantics and Franz Pfeiffer, and edited critically from 1936 onwards.

#### What the essay assumes

The essay assumes familiarity with sermon 52, the prayer to be rid of God, the bull of 1329 and the word Gelassenheit. It distinguishes two readings of Eckhart, negative theology pushed to its limit and the ancestor of spiritualities without God, and follows the second line in Schopenhauer, who hails him as a brother of the sages of India, in Heidegger, who displaces Gelassenheit, and in Simone Weil, whose decreation resembles his teaching. It notes that Feuerbach has the same form and the opposite conclusion.
