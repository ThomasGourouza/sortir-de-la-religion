The canon of the New Testament, that is, the list of the twenty-seven books that the Churches hold to be Scripture, was not fixed by a decision but by a usage of more than two centuries, which lists later recorded. The Greek word kanôn designates the mason's rule, then the norm, then the catalogue.

#### The stages

Paul's letters circulated very early from one Church to another and were gathered into a collection before the end of the first century; the second letter of Peter already counts them among the Scriptures. Around 150, Justin reports that on Sundays the memoirs of the apostles, that is, the gospels, are read alongside the prophets. The first to draw up a closed list is Marcion, c. 140: a single gospel, an abridged Luke, and ten letters of Paul, excluding the whole Old Testament; his gesture forces the Churches to say what they receive. Irenaeus of Lyon, c. 180, asserts that there can be only four gospels, as there are four winds, against the Gnostic writings. The Muratorian Canon, a Latin fragment published in Milan in 1740 and dated by most scholars to the late second century, lists the bulk of the present collection, without Hebrews, James or the letters of Peter, and with an Apocalypse of Peter; it admits the Shepherd of Hermas for private reading, because it is recent. Origen, in the third century, distinguishes the books acknowledged by all, the disputed books and the spurious ones; Eusebius, c. 325, takes up this classification. The first list exactly matching ours is the thirty-ninth festal letter of Athanasius of Alexandria, announcing the date of Easter for the year 367. The African synods of Hippo (393) and Carthage (397), in which Augustine took part, confirm it for the West. In Syria, the Peshitta long ignores five of these books; the Church of Ethiopia keeps a broader canon.

#### The criteria

Four criteria recur among the Fathers: apostolicity, a book written by an apostle or one of his companions, Mark for Peter, Luke for Paul; conformity with the rule of faith handed down in the Churches; reception, that is, ancient and widespread liturgical use; antiquity, which excludes Hermas. They serve less to choose than to justify after the fact a usage already established.

#### The disputed books and the excluded books

Seven books were slow to gain acceptance: Hebrews, Revelation, James, 2 Peter, 2 and 3 John, Jude. Four others were for a time read as Scripture and then set aside: the Shepherd of Hermas and the letter of Barnabas, copied in the Codex Sinaiticus in the fourth century; the Didache; the first letter of Clement, present in the Codex Alexandrinus. The apocryphal gospels never appeared in any Church list.

#### The Reformations and Trent

Luther, in 1522, puts Hebrews, James, Jude and Revelation at the end of the volume, unnumbered, but does not remove them; the Reformed exclude from the Old Testament the Greek books absent from the Hebrew Bible, Tobit, Judith, Wisdom, Sirach, Baruch, Maccabees, which they call apocrypha and print separately. The Council of Florence had given the list in 1442; the Council of Trent, on 8 April 1546, defines it for the Catholic Church, deuterocanonical books included, with the Vulgate as the authentic text.

#### A legend to dismiss

The Council of Nicaea, in 325, did not fix the canon: its acts deal with Arius and the date of Easter. The idea of a sorting of the books at Nicaea comes from a late anecdote, mocked by Voltaire and popularized by *The Da Vinci Code*.

#### What the essay assumes

The essay follows the dialogue between Celsus and Origen, and later Porphyry contesting the date of Daniel, without saying which books were then received; it reports that Augustine grounds understanding on prior faith and that religion, according to Montaigne, is received through the custom of the country, which applies first of all to its books. It itself works with a canon sorted by criticism: seven letters of Paul held to be authentic, Mark c. 70, John at the end of the first century, and it contrasts, in Strauss and Bauer, two ways of explaining the formation of the gospels.
