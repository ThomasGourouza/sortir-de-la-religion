Platonism is the longest tradition in Western philosophy: from the Academy founded by Plato around 387 BCE to the Florentine Renaissance, it supplied Hellenized Judaism, Christianity and Islam with the framework in which to think a transcendent God, an immortal soul and an intelligible world. The essay encounters it at every stage of its first and second parts.

#### The Academy and Middle Platonism

The Academy, the school Plato founded in a gymnasium in Athens, passes after him to Speusippus and Xenocrates, who systematize the doctrine of Forms and numbers, then, in the 3rd and 2nd centuries BCE, with Arcesilaus and later Carneades, to scepticism: the New Academy suspends judgement and combats Stoic dogmatism, and it is this Platonism that Cicero knew and handed on. In the 1st century BCE, Antiochus of Ascalon returns to dogmatism, and what the moderns call Middle Platonism begins: Philo of Alexandria, Plutarch, Apuleius, Numenius, Celsus and Alcinous read Plato as a theologian, place the Forms in the intellect of a transcendent God, rank the divine into a first God, a demiurge and a world soul, and absorb Aristotelian and Stoic elements. It is this school Platonism that the Christian apologists and the Gnostics found confronting them, and that Celsus defends against them.

#### Neoplatonism

Plotinus, in the 3rd century, gives Platonism its most rigorous form: the three hypostases, the One, Intellect, Soul, procession and conversion, and union with the One. Porphyry edits and popularizes; Iamblichus, in Syria, and Proclus, in Athens in the 5th century, multiply the intermediaries, systematize, and integrate theurgy, a ritual practice of salvation, in a last effort to give paganism a philosophical foundation; the school of Athens is closed by Justinian in 529, while that of Alexandria survives by becoming Christian. The Neoplatonists read Aristotle as an introduction to Plato, and it is through their commentaries, relayed by Boethius, that Aristotelian logic passed through the Middle Ages.

#### Christian, Jewish and Muslim Platonism

Augustine, through the books of the Platonists translated by Marius Victorinus, finds in Plotinus the Word without the Incarnation, and inwardness; Pseudo-Dionysius, an anonymous author of the early 6th century who passes himself off as the disciple of Paul, Christianizes Proclus and hands down to the Middle Ages negative theology and the hierarchy of angels; Eriugena, the school of Chartres, Bonaventure, Eckhart and Nicholas of Cusa draw their life from it. In Islam, the *Theology of Aristotle*, extracts from Plotinus attributed to Aristotle, and the *Book of Causes*, drawn from Proclus, nourish al-Farabi and Avicenna; in the Jewish world, Ibn Gabirol and the Kabbalah. The Renaissance, with Gemistus Plethon, Ficino, who translates Plato and Plotinus in Florence, and Pico della Mirandola, dreams of a Platonic theology and a concord of wisdoms; the Cambridge Platonists in the seventeenth century, then Schelling, Hegel, Bergson, Simone Weil and Murdoch carry the lineage forward.

#### What the essay assumes

The essay takes as known the distinction between Middle Platonism and Neoplatonism, Nietzsche's formula of Christianity as Platonism for the people, the role of the books of the Platonists in Augustine, the Christianization of Proclus by Pseudo-Dionysius, and the Platonic genealogy of negative theology and of the spirituality of exercise. It regards Plotinus as the consummate example of a spirituality that might not have become religion.
