The second half of the twentieth century sees the Catholic Church reconcile itself with modernity at the very moment when Europe, within a generation, stops practising. Then the religious returns, in other forms, and a new militant atheism answers it. This is the present in which the authors of the eleventh and twelfth parts of the essay write.

#### Vatican II

John XXIII, elected in 1958, convenes a council for an aggiornamento, an updating of the Church. Vatican II sits from 1962 to 1965 under John XXIII and then Paul VI and brings together more than two thousand bishops. Its major texts reform the liturgy, now in the vernacular and facing the congregation (*Sacrosanctum concilium*, 1963), redefine the Church as the people of God (*Lumen gentium*, 1964), recognize religious freedom as a right of the person (*Dignitatis humanae*, 1965), open dialogue with other religions and release the Jews from the charge of deicide (*Nostra aetate*, 1965), and affirm the solidarity of the Church with the world of its time (*Gaudium et spes*, 1965). The council closes the intransigence of the nineteenth century: it accepts democracy, the autonomy of the political and critical exegesis. It also provokes a crisis: mass departures of priests and religious, quarrels over the liturgy, the traditionalist schism of Archbishop Lefebvre (1988), and the restoration of order carried out by John Paul II (1978-2005) and Benedict XVI (2005-2013).

#### The rupture of the 1960s

Throughout Western Europe, religious practice collapses between 1965 and 1975: Sunday Mass, attended by a quarter of the French at the beginning of the 1960s, is attended by only about one in ten around 1980, and ordinations fall by half; the same curves can be read in the Netherlands, Belgium and Quebec. Historians debate the causes: prosperity and the welfare state, sexual liberation and contraception, condemned by *Humanae vitae* in 1968, a culture of autonomy, and the effects of the council itself. Family transmission breaks down, and the following generations are no longer disaffiliated but simply without religion. The sociology of secularization, Berger, Luckmann, Bryan Wilson, theorizes this development, which Norris and Inglehart will measure and which Taylor will call the secular age.

#### The return of the religious and the new atheists

From the late 1970s onwards, the religious returns to the public stage in forms that secularization had not foreseen: the Islamic revolution in Iran (1979), the election of John Paul II (1978) and the role of the Church in the fall of Polish communism, the Christian right in the United States, evangelical and Pentecostal movements in the global South, a revival of pilgrimages and of spiritualities outside institutions. In 1991 Gilles Kepel speaks of the revenge of God, in 1999 Berger retracts his secularization thesis, in 2001 Habermas coins the word postsecular. The attacks of 11 September 2001 give birth to a militant atheism: Sam Harris (2004), Richard Dawkins and Daniel Dennett (2006), Christopher Hitchens (2007), the new atheists, and in France Onfray's *Traité d'athéologie* (2005). At the same time, the share of people without religion keeps growing in Europe, and Islam becomes the second religion of several countries.

#### What the essay assumes

The essay assumes familiarity with the turning point of Vatican II, which makes possible a Catholicism reconciled with democracy, the background to the positions of Taylor and Habermas, the collapse of European practice, which Hervieu-Léger describes as the end of a world, the return of the religious, which motivates postsecular theories, and the context of the new atheists, whom it places in the poorest form of the regime of error.
