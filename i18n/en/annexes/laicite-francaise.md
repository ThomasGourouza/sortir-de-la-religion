Laïcité is the French form of the exit of religion from the State, built between 1880 and 1905 against a Catholic Church then hostile to the Republic. It is the framework of Durkheim, of Gauchet and of contemporary debates on the religious, and the essay takes it as known.

#### The school and the Republic

The word laïcité appears in the 1870s. The Third Republic, founded in 1870 and consolidated in 1879 by the victory of the republicans, comes up against a Church that had supported the Moral Order and had condemned modern liberalism in the *Syllabus* of 1864. The first front is the school. The Jules Ferry laws make primary education free (1881), then compulsory and secular (1882): religious instruction leaves the curriculum, replaced by moral and civic instruction, and one day off is left for catechism outside school. The Goblet law of 1886 entrusts public education to lay staff. The school of the Republic's black hussars trains citizens; secular morality, of which Durkheim is one of the theorists at the Sorbonne, seeks to ground duty without God, on society. At the same time, divorce is restored (1884), and cemeteries and hospitals are secularized.

#### The failed rallying and the Dreyfus affair

In 1892 Leo XIII invites French Catholics to rally to the Republic. The rallying largely fails, and the Dreyfus affair, from 1894 to 1906, reopens the rift: part of the clergy and the Catholic press, the Assumptionists' *La Croix*, side with the anti-Dreyfusard and antisemitic camp. The victory of the Dreyfusards brings the Bloc des gauches to power; the law of 1901 on associations makes religious congregations subject to authorization, and Émile Combes, head of government from 1902 to 1905, closes thousands of their establishments, forbids members of congregations to teach (1904) and breaks with the Vatican.

#### The law of 1905

The law of 9 December 1905 on the separation of the Churches and the State, which Briand and Jaurès carried against the partisans of a punitive separation, rests on two articles of principle: the Republic ensures freedom of conscience and guarantees the free exercise of worship; it recognizes, pays and subsidizes no religion. The Concordat of 1801 is abrogated, and Church property is entrusted to associations for worship. Pius X condemns the law (*Vehementer nos*, 1906) and forbids the associations; the quarrel over the inventories causes incidents, after which the buildings remain at the disposal of the faithful. Appeasement comes after 1914: the union sacrée, the restoration of diplomatic relations (1921), Pius XI's acceptance of diocesan associations (1924). Alsace-Moselle, German in 1905, keeps the concordat regime. Laïcité becomes a constitutional principle in 1946 and 1958.

#### Models and debates

French laïcité differs from the American model, where separation protects the Churches from the State as much as the reverse, and from the European regimes of an established Church or of the recognition of religions. The debate revives from 1989 onwards, with the headscarf affairs at school, the 2004 law on religious symbols and the discussions on Islam, in which a laïcité of neutrality and a laïcité of combat confront each other. Gauchet sees in it the effect of an exit from religion that is not an exit from belief: religion returns in democracy as identity and as choice.

#### What the essay assumes

The essay takes as known the conflict between the Republic and the Church, which explains the tone of the French sciences of religion and Durkheim's secular morality, the content of the law of 1905, and the difference between French laïcité and the secularization that Taylor or Habermas describe for other countries. It relies on Gauchet to think what remains of the religious in a secular society.
