Phenomenology is the philosophical movement founded by Edmund Husserl at the beginning of the twentieth century, which seeks to describe phenomena as they are given to consciousness, prior to any theory. Heidegger, Levinas and Marion, three authors of Part Ten of the essay, come from it, and the debate on what phenomenology can say about God runs through their works.

#### Husserl

Husserl (1859-1938), a mathematician by training, publishes the *Logical Investigations* in 1900-1901, against the psychologism that reduces logic to laws of the mind, then in 1913 the *Ideas*, which fix the method. His watchword is the return to the things themselves. Consciousness is intentionality: it is always consciousness of something, and the phenomenon is neither a thing in itself nor a subjective impression, but the thing as it appears. The phenomenological reduction brackets, epoché, the thesis of the existence of the world in order to describe the way in which the world is constituted for consciousness; the eidetic reduction brings out essences through imaginative variation. In his last works, the *Krisis* (1936), Husserl describes the life-world, Lebenswelt, which the sciences cover over and forget. He leaves few texts on God, whom he regards as a limit idea, but his method was taken up by believing philosophers, Max Scheler, who describes the religious act, Edith Stein, and Rudolf Otto, a phenomenologist in all but name, whose analysis of the numinous proceeds by describing states of consciousness.

#### Heidegger and existence

Heidegger, Husserl's assistant, transforms phenomenology in *Being and Time* (1927): the phenomenon to be described is no longer consciousness but Dasein, the human existent, in its being-in-the-world, its care, its being-towards-death, and the guiding question becomes that of the meaning of being. Phenomenology becomes a hermeneutics of existence, then, after the turn of the 1930s, a thinking of being and of its history, of which metaphysics is the forgetting. Heidegger separates philosophy from faith, and his critique of onto-theology maintains that the God of metaphysics is not a God before whom one can pray. Sartre and Merleau-Ponty introduce the movement into France, the former in an ontology of freedom, the latter in a philosophy of perception and the body.

#### The theological turn

From the 1960s onwards, part of French phenomenology turns towards what exceeds the phenomenon: Levinas describes the face of the other as what overflows all intentionality and where the trace of God is found; Michel Henry seeks in the self-affection of life a phenomenology of Christianity; Jean-Luc Marion develops a phenomenology of givenness, in which the saturated phenomenon, the event, the idol, the flesh, the icon, exceeds the concepts that would contain it, and in which revelation is thought as a possibility. Jean-Louis Chrétien and Jean-Yves Lacoste continue along this path. Dominique Janicaud denounces this theological turn in 1991 as an abandonment of Husserl's methodical neutrality; those concerned reply that description does not presuppose faith. Paul Ricoeur, for his part, grafts hermeneutics onto phenomenology.

#### What the essay assumes

The essay assumes familiarity with the meaning of the phenomenological method, the line running from Husserl to Heidegger, Levinas and Marion, the Heideggerian critique of onto-theology as the background of Part Ten, and the debate on the theological turn, which gives its context to Marion's idol and icon and to Levinas's face. It does not settle the quarrel over method; it asks only whether these philosophies install an absolute that demands.
