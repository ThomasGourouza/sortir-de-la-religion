Between the middle of the fourteenth and the end of the sixteenth century, Europe rediscovers the texts of Antiquity, invents printing and transforms its relation to religion without yet criticizing it head-on. Montaigne, with whom the third part of the essay opens, is the heir of this movement, and Descartes its late contemporary.

#### The humanists and the return to the sources

The word humanist first designates a profession: the teacher of the humanities, grammar, rhetoric, poetry, history and moral philosophy, studied in the ancient authors. Petrarch, in the fourteenth century, searches for the manuscripts of Cicero and sets the tone; in the fifteenth, the Italians recover Lucretius (the *De rerum natura*, copied by Poggio Bracciolini in 1417), Plato, whom Marsilio Ficino translates into Latin in Florence, and the Greek Fathers. The return to the sources, ad fontes, is at once philological and religious: Lorenzo Valla demonstrates in 1440 that the Donation of Constantine is a forgery, and applies criticism to the text of the New Testament; Erasmus (c. 1466-1536) publishes in 1516 the first printed Greek New Testament, with a new Latin translation, and proposes in *The Praise of Folly* and the *Colloquies* an inward piety that mocks monks, relics and pilgrimages, and that prepares the Reformation without joining it: Erasmus breaks with Luther in 1524 over the question of free will. Pico della Mirandola, in his *Oration on the Dignity of Man* (1486), makes man the being without a fixed nature who shapes himself. Humanism is not an atheism; it is another way of being Christian, nourished by the Ancients, and sometimes a dreamed-of concord between Plato, Moses and Christ.

#### Printing

Gutenberg prints in Mainz, around 1455, the first Bible in movable type. By 1500, more than two hundred towns have presses and some twenty million volumes have been produced. Printing fixes texts, multiplies readers, lowers the price of books and makes possible the rapid spread of ideas: Luther's theses go round Germany in a few weeks, and Bibles in the vernacular put Scripture into the hands of the laity. It also creates modern censorship: the Index of Forbidden Books is instituted by Rome in 1559. The culture of the printed book is that of Montaigne, who reads Plutarch, Seneca and Sextus Empiricus in his library, and whose *Essays* (1580, 1588, 1595) are among the first great books written in the first person for a public of anonymous readers.

#### Scepticism and custom

The rediscovery of ancient scepticism, with Sextus Empiricus translated into Latin in 1562 and 1569, supplies the thinkers of the end of the century with a new instrument. Faced with the Wars of Religion, Montaigne draws from Pyrrhonism a lesson of modesty: reason cannot ground faith, which depends on custom and birth, and this is what he calls being Christian as one is a native of Périgord. This position, fideism, is held by sincere believers, Montaigne, Charron, and also serves, a century later, the libertines. Descartes, trained by the Jesuits of La Flèche in humanist culture, will turn away from it to seek in reason alone a foundation that custom does not provide.

#### What the essay assumes

The essay takes for granted the meaning of the word humanist in the Renaissance, different from Feuerbach's humanism, the return of the Ancients, Lucretius and Plato, whom Montaigne reads, the role of printing in the Reformation, and the genealogy of Montaigne's scepticism, which it reads as a sincere fideism whose unintended effect is custom as the sole explanation of faith.
