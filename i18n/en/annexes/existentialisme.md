Existentialism is the name given after 1945 to a family of philosophies, born in the nineteenth century with Kierkegaard and developed in Germany and then in France between the two world wars, which start from the concrete existence of the individual, from his freedom, his anguish and his death, against systems and essences. It had a believing wing and an atheist wing, and the essay meets both in its tenth and twelfth parts.

#### The sources

Kierkegaard, against Hegel, holds that existence does not let itself be thought within the system, that subjectivity is truth and that faith is a leap; Dostoevsky stages the underground man, Ivan and Kirillov; Nietzsche announces the death of God and asks how to live after it; Pascal, Augustine and Job are reread as ancestors. In the twentieth century the word existence takes on a technical sense: the mode of being proper to man, who is not a thing but a relation to himself, to others and to death.

#### The philosophy of existence in Germany

Karl Jaspers, in his *Philosophie* (1932), calls philosophy of existence a thinking that illuminates existence without knowing it, through limit situations, communication and the ciphers of transcendence; Heidegger, in *Being and Time* (1927), analyses Dasein, being-towards-death, anxiety and authenticity, while refusing the label, as he would say in the *Letter on Humanism* (1947). The theologians of existence, Bultmann with demythologization, Tillich, Gogarten, take up these analyses; Martin Buber, in *I and Thou* (1923), reflects on relation; Lev Shestov, a Russian émigré in Paris, sets the faith of Jerusalem and the cry of Job against Athens and necessary reason.

#### French existentialism

Gabriel Marcel, who converted to Catholicism in 1929, a philosopher of mystery, incarnation and fidelity, is regarded as having introduced the word existentialism into French, applying it to Sartre before refusing it for himself. Sartre, in *Being and Nothingness* (1943) and the 1946 lecture, makes it a doctrine: existence precedes essence, man is freedom, condemned to choose himself without God or human nature, and responsible for everything; Simone de Beauvoir draws from it an ethics of ambiguity and *The Second Sex*; Merleau-Ponty a phenomenology of perception and of the body; Camus, who rejects the word, draws the absurd and revolt. In 1947 Mounier distinguishes Christian existentialisms, Marcel, Jaspers, Berdyaev, from atheist ones, Sartre, Heidegger; Pius XII condemns existentialism in 1950 in *Humani generis*, and the Communist Party denounces it as a bourgeois ideology. Saint-Germain-des-Prés, the cellars, the theatre, *Huis clos*, *The Flies*, *Caligula*, and the novels, *Nausea*, *L'Étranger*, make it the first mass philosophical movement. It declines in the 1960s before structuralism, which declares the death of the subject, but its themes, freedom, commitment, authenticity, the choice of oneself, have passed into common culture.

#### What the essay assumes

The essay takes for granted the genealogy Kierkegaard, Dostoevsky, Nietzsche, the difference between philosophy of existence and existentialism, the 1946 lecture, Heidegger's reply and the division between believers and atheists. It places Kierkegaard and Jaspers on the side of faith, Sartre among the atheists of the marketplace and Camus among the exceptions; Gabriel Marcel does not appear in it.
