Mimetic theory is the overall hypothesis that René Girard (1923-2015) developed over half a century, from literary criticism to anthropology, then to the reading of the Bible and the theory of history. It starts from an observation about desire and claims to explain the origin of culture, religion and institutions. The essay retains only its anthropological level; this article presents the whole edifice.

#### Mimetic desire

The first book, *Deceit, Desire and the Novel* (1961), reads Cervantes, Stendhal, Flaubert, Proust and Dostoevsky. Their heroes do not desire spontaneously: they desire what a model desires, and the novel unmasks this mediation, which the romantic illusion of autonomy conceals. When the model is out of reach, mediation is external and peaceful; when he shares the subject's world, it is internal, and the model becomes a rival, an obstacle, then an obsession. Girard later extends the hypothesis to psychology, with the psychiatrist Jean-Michel Oughourlian, and to Shakespeare (*A Theater of Envy*, 1990).

#### The crisis and the surrogate victim

*Violence and the Sacred* (1972) carries mimetic desire over into stateless societies. Rivalry spreads by contagion, the differences that hold the community together are effaced, and reciprocal violence becomes a mimetic crisis. It is resolved by the spontaneous convergence of all against one, designated by some arbitrary sign: the collective murder of the scapegoat restores peace, and the community, unaware of what it has done, attributes the crisis and then the reconciliation to the victim, whom it divinizes. From this mechanism, according to Girard, derive the sacred, the sacrificial rite that repeats it, the prohibitions that forestall the crisis, the myths that recount it from the crowd's point of view, and even institutions: kingship would be a victim whose sacrifice is deferred, domestication and hunting forms of sacrifice. *Things Hidden since the Foundation of the World* (1978) makes the victimary mechanism the driving force of hominization itself.

#### The biblical revelation

The same book and *The Scapegoat* (1982) argue that the biblical texts, from Joseph to the Psalms and the suffering servant, and then the Gospels, recount the lynching from the victim's point of view and proclaim his innocence, unveiling what the myths conceal. Once unveiled, the mechanism loses its efficacy: Christian societies can no longer reconcile themselves over a victim in good conscience, hence the modern concern for victims, but also the loss of the brake that the sacred placed on violence. *I See Satan Fall Like Lightning* (1999) names Satan the principle of accusation; *Battling to the End* (2007) reads in the escalation to extremes of modern war an apocalypse without transcendence.

#### Extensions and criticisms

The theory has spread beyond its field: Jean-Pierre Dupuy and André Orléan apply it to economics (money, speculative bubbles as mimetic contagion), Dupuy to nuclear deterrence and catastrophe, theologians such as Raymund Schwager and James Alison to Christology, and neuroscientists have sought support for it in mirror neurons. An annual conference, the Colloquium on Violence and Religion, has been devoted to it since 1990. Anthropologists raise three objections: the hypothesis of the founding murder is unverifiable; sacrifice, in many societies, is a gift or a meal rather than a lynching; a theory that makes its own religion the key to all the others is open to the suspicion of circularity. Walter Burkert, in the same year 1972, had proposed a related origin of sacrifice in Palaeolithic hunting, without the revelation.

#### What the essay assumes

The essay sets out the theory in its section on Girard, connects it with Frazer, Freud, Durkheim and Nietzsche, and makes it the anthropological foundation of its sacrifice criterion, leaving aside the apologetics. It assumes familiarity with the vocabulary (mediation, double, undifferentiation, misrecognition) and with the breadth of the applications, from the novel to economics, which make mimetic theory a system and not a single thesis on religion.
