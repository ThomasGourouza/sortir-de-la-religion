The nineteenth century is at once the century in which the critique of religion takes its modern forms, from Feuerbach to Nietzsche, and the century of a massive religious revival. The Catholic Church sets itself against the world that issued from the Revolution; German Protestantism invents the historical criticism of the Bible and liberal theology. Almost all the authors of the fifth and sixth parts of the essay write in this context.

#### Religious revival and Catholic intransigence

After the Revolution and the Concordat of 1801, Catholicism undergoes a renewal in France and in Europe: refounded congregations, missions, pilgrimages, the apparitions of Lourdes in 1858, Marian piety and devotion to the pope. This Catholicism is ultramontane, that is, turned towards Rome against the national Churches, and intransigent towards political modernity. Pius IX, pope from 1846 to 1878, proclaims the dogma of the Immaculate Conception in 1854 and publishes in 1864 the *Syllabus*, a catalogue of eighty modern errors, the last of which condemns the idea that the Roman pontiff can reconcile himself with progress, liberalism and modern civilization. The First Vatican Council (1869-1870) defines papal infallibility in matters of faith and morals; it is interrupted by the capture of Rome, which puts an end to the Papal States. In Germany, Bismarck launches the Kulturkampf against the Church (1871-1878), expelling the Jesuits and imprisoning bishops, before backing down. Leo XIII (1878-1903) opens the Church to the social question with *Rerum novarum* (1891) and revives Thomism, but Pius X's condemnation of modernism in 1907 closes the way to critical exegesis among Catholics for half a century.

#### Tübingen, biblical criticism and liberal theology

In the German Protestant universities, the Bible becomes an object of history. After Reimarus, whose fragments Lessing publishes in 1774-1778, the Tübingen school around Ferdinand Christian Baur applies a Hegelian reading to the history of early Christianity: the opposition between the Jewish Christianity of Peter and the universalism of Paul, then the Catholic synthesis. David Friedrich Strauss, a former student at Tübingen, publishes in 1835 the *Life of Jesus*, which reads the gospels as myths, and loses his chair. Julius Wellhausen establishes the documentary hypothesis on the Pentateuch in 1878. At the same time, liberal theology, heir to Schleiermacher, seeks to reconcile faith and culture: Albrecht Ritschl reduces Christianity to a moral experience, Adolf von Harnack, in *What Is Christianity?* (1900), to the fatherhood of God and the infinite value of the soul; this Kulturprotestantismus, cultural Protestantism, will be Barth's target after 1914. In France, Renan's *Life of Jesus* (1863) transposes German criticism into a best-selling narrative and costs its author his chair at the Collège de France.

#### The birth of the sciences of religion

The century sees the birth of the disciplines that study religion as a fact: the comparative philology of Max Müller, the anthropology of Tylor, the history of religions, whose first French chair is created in 1880 at the Collège de France, then the sociology of Durkheim. Comte's positivism, which announces the end of the theological age and founds a religion of Humanity, is its most ambitious form.

#### What the essay assumes

The essay assumes knowledge of the climate of Catholic intransigence, which explains the violence of the conflict between science and faith, the difference between Strauss's historical criticism and the liberal theology it shakes, the scandal of Renan, the position of the cultural Protestantism against which Barth will rise, and the fact that the Young Hegelians write in a Prussian State that calls itself Christian.

#### The dates

- 1835: Strauss's *Life of Jesus*; 1863: Renan's *Life of Jesus*.
- 1854: Immaculate Conception; 1858: Lourdes.
- 1864: *Syllabus*; 1870: papal infallibility and the capture of Rome.
- 1871-1878: Kulturkampf.
- 1891: *Rerum novarum*; 1900: Harnack's *What Is Christianity?*.
- 1907: condemnation of modernism.
