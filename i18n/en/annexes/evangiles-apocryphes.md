The name apocryphal gospels is given to the narratives or collections of sayings of Jesus that the Churches did not receive into their canon. The Greek word apokryphos means hidden: it first designates books reserved for initiates, a sense some gnostics claimed, then, among the Fathers, books not read in the assembly and of suspect origin.

#### The main families

The sayings gospels have no Passion narrative. The most important is the Gospel of Thomas, 114 sayings attributed to Jesus, known from Greek fragments found at Oxyrhynchus since 1897 and from a complete Coptic version discovered at Nag Hammadi in 1945; half of its sayings have a parallel in the synoptics, the other half have an ascetic and gnosticizing colouring. Its date divides the schools, from 60 to 150, depending on whether it is held to be independent of the canonical Gospels or dependent on them. The Gospel of Peter, a fragment of which was found at Akhmim in 1886, tells the Passion with a cross that speaks; around 200, Bishop Serapion of Antioch forbade its reading. The gnostic gospels have the risen Jesus speak to a few disciples: the Gospel of Mary, in which Mary Magdalene receives a revelation that Peter contests; the Gospel of Philip, a Valentinian collection on the sacraments; the Gospel of Judas, in Coptic, published in 2006, in which Judas is read now as the disciple who understands, now as a demon. The infancy gospels fill the silences: the Protevangelium of James, from the second century, tells of the birth of Mary, of her parents Anne and Joachim, of her presentation in the Temple, of old Joseph the widower, and it is the source of several Marian feasts and of their iconography; the Infancy Gospel of Thomas shows a child Jesus bringing clay birds to life. The Jewish-Christian gospels, of the Hebrews, of the Nazarenes, of the Ebionites, are known only through quotations in Clement, Origen, Jerome and Epiphanius. Marcion, around 140, had retained only an abridged Luke. To these are added the agrapha, sayings of Jesus quoted outside the Gospels, as in Ac 20, 35, and the Egerton papyrus, around 150.

#### What they contribute

On the Jesus of history, little: historians date these texts to the second and third centuries, and only Thomas is debated, some looking in it for a few independent sayings. On second-century Christianity, a great deal: the diversity of currents, the conflicts of authority between Peter and Mary or Thomas, popular piety and its taste for the marvellous, the sources of Christian iconography, the ox and the ass of the crib, the descent into hell, Veronica, which come from yet other apocrypha.

#### Why outside the canon

These writings are later than the four Gospels, tied to particular currents, gnostic or encratite, without wide liturgical use, and held to be non-apostolic despite the names they bear. The Churches retained the books read everywhere and for a long time, in conformity with the rule of faith; the rest was copied in the margins, translated, read in private, and sometimes buried, as at Nag Hammadi.

#### Modern readings

Strauss, in 1835, works on the four canonical Gospels and draws nothing from the apocrypha; nor does Bauer, who looks for the conscious author behind the synoptics; Renan barely cites them. The discoveries of Nag Hammadi and the publication of Judas have fed a contemporary fascination, of which *The Da Vinci Code* (2003) is the example, for gospels hidden by the Church; historians, Ehrman among them, reply that these late texts teach nothing new about Jesus and a great deal about his readers.

#### What the essay assumes

The essay reasons on the canonical sources, the letters of Paul, the Gospels from Mark around 70 to John at the end of the first century, Josephus and Tacitus, and does not mention the apocrypha; it takes from Ehrman the consensus on the existence of Jesus, and from Celsus a polemic, Jesus son of a soldier named Panthera, which circulates outside the Gospels. When it opposes Strauss's collective myth to Bauer's conscious author, or describes in Onfray a Jesus as concept, it takes for granted this profusion of second-century gospels, which shows how the life of a founder was written at the time.
