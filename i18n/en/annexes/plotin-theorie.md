Plotinus (205-270), born in Egypt, a pupil of Ammonius Saccas in Alexandria, taught in Rome from 244 before a circle of disciples and senators. His fifty-four treatises, written late in life and with no concern for style, were arranged by his disciple Porphyry into six groups of nine, the *Enneads*. He sees himself as a mere interpreter of Plato; the moderns call him the founder of Neoplatonism.

#### The three hypostases

Reality is ordered into three principles, or hypostases. At the summit is the One, or the Good, absolutely simple, beyond being and thought, of which nothing can be said except what it is not; Plotinus derives it from the Good beyond essence of the *Republic* and from the first hypothesis of the *Parmenides*. From the One proceeds, without the One losing anything or willing it, like light from the sun, Intellect, Nous, which thinks itself and contains all the Forms, true being: it is Aristotle's god, placed in the second rank. From Intellect proceeds Soul, which contemplates Intellect and produces the sensible world, in which it unfolds as World Soul and as individual souls. Matter, the lowest degree, is privation and darkness, what Plotinus calls evil, without making it a principle. This system, procession, descends by degrees from unity to multiplicity; conversion, epistrophê, climbs back along the reverse path.

#### The ascent of the soul

The human soul, having descended into a body, keeps a part of itself in the intelligible. Philosophy is the itinerary of its return: purification through the virtues, detachment from the sensible, dialectic, contemplation of the Forms in Intellect, and finally union with the One, in which the soul ceases to think and to distinguish itself from what it reaches. Porphyry reports that Plotinus knew this union four times during the years he spent with him. This experience, which the moderns call ecstasy, is not a gift coming from elsewhere: it is obtained through an exercise, cutting away everything that is not the One, and the last sentence of the *Enneads* describes it as a flight of the alone to the alone. Plotinus has neither revelation, nor Church, nor cult; he refuses to sacrifice to the gods and writes against the Gnostics, who despise the sensible world, a treatise in which he defends the beauty of the cosmos.

#### Posterity

Porphyry, then Iamblichus and Proclus, who multiplies the intermediaries and reintegrates theurgic rites, hand the system down to the last pagans; Augustine reads it in the Latin translations of Marius Victorinus and finds in it the Word without the Incarnation; Pseudo-Dionysius Christianizes it and passes it on to the whole of medieval mysticism, Eriugena, Eckhart; Ficino translates it in the Renaissance; Hegel, Bergson and Bréhier reread it; Hadot, in 1963, makes it the model of a spirituality of the simplicity of vision.

#### What the essay assumes

The essay takes as known the doctrine of the three hypostases, procession and conversion, the negative theology of the One, which it places at the origin of the regime of experience, and Porphyry's account. It makes Plotinus the consummate example of a spirituality that might not have become religion, religious in James's sense, the ancestor of the spiritualities without God that Hadot describes, and notes that the sacrifice criterion does not apply to him. Levinas, in A Religion for Adults, targets him by name, an attested response: to the Plotinian soul that goes towards nothing other than itself, he opposes a real transcendence.
