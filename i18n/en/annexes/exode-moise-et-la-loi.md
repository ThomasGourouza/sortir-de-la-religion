Exodus, the second book of the Pentateuch, tells of the liberation of the Hebrews from Egypt and the giving of the Law at Sinai; the following books continue it up to the death of Moses. It is the founding narrative of Israel, which the Jewish Passover commemorates every year.

#### From oppression to the burning bush

The narrative tells how the descendants of Jacob are reduced to servitude by a Pharaoh who orders the newborn boys to be drowned. A Levite child is hidden in a basket on the Nile, taken in by Pharaoh's daughter and named Moses. As an adult he kills an Egyptian and flees to the land of Midian. Near Horeb he sees a bush that burns without being consumed; God calls him from it and answers the request for his name with an enigmatic formula, often rendered as I am who I am (Ex 3).

#### The plagues, the Passover, the sea

Pharaoh refuses to let the people go; ten plagues strike Egypt. On the night of the last, each family slaughters a lamb and marks its door with the blood: this is the Passover. Pharaoh gives way, then gives chase; the sea opens before Israel and closes over the chariots (Ex 14). In the desert, God feeds the murmuring people with manna.

#### Sinai and the Law

At Sinai, God utters the ten words, the Decalogue: no other gods, no image, respect for the name and for the sabbath, the honour due to parents, the prohibitions of murder, adultery, theft, false witness and covetousness (Ex 20). Deuteronomy gives a second version, which grounds the sabbath differently (Dt 5). The covenant code follows, civil and ritual laws; Moses then goes up the mountain for forty days.

#### The golden calf and the broken tables

In his absence, the people ask Aaron for gods to go before them; Aaron melts down the gold rings into a calf, which the people acclaim as the god who brought them out of Egypt (Ex 32). Coming down with the tables written by the finger of God, Moses sees the dancing and breaks the tables. He intercedes, is allowed to see God only from behind (Ex 33) and cuts two new tables. Leviticus then regulates sacrifices and purity; Deuteronomy rereads the history in the discourses of Moses, with the Shema, the call to love a single God, and tells of his death on Mount Nebo, in sight of a land he does not enter.

#### History and readings

No Egyptian source or archaeological trace attests a mass departure; most historians regard the narrative as a founding composition elaborated between the eighth and the fifth century BCE, some admitting a historical core, a small group come from Egypt. Maimonides explains the sacrifices as a divine pedagogy adapted to a people accustomed to idolatrous cults (*Guide for the Perplexed*, III, 32). Freud, in *Moses and Monotheism* (1939), makes Moses an Egyptian close to Akhenaten, killed by his people, and monotheism the return of this repressed murder. The golden calf has become the very image of idolatry.

#### What the essay assumes

The section on Maimonides sets out the thesis of the *Guide* III, 32, the sacrifices transferred to the name of God rather than abolished, like the detour through the desert (Ex 13, 17-18); its illustration, Rembrandt's Moses, recalls that it is debated whether he is breaking the tables or presenting them, and reads in the God seen from behind (Ex 33, 23) only the attributes of action of the *Guide* I, 54. In the section on Feuerbach, Poussin's *The Adoration of the Golden Calf* figures projection, man adoring what he has made. Philo makes the exodus the departure of the soul from the body. The section on Freud keeps to *The Future of an Illusion* and *Civilization and Its Discontents*, without discussing the book on Moses; Part One asks what becomes of the revealed Law when a philosopher reads it, and Part Two situates the birth of Christianity between a God of the Law come from Jerusalem and a God of reason come from Athens.
