Gnosticism is the name given to a set of religious currents of the second and third centuries, most of them Christian, which taught salvation through gnosis, a revealed knowledge of the soul's divine origin and of its fall into an evil world. Fought by the Fathers as the first heresy and rediscovered in the twentieth century through the Nag Hammadi texts, the word became, in Jonas and Voegelin, and then in Blumenberg's reply, a category for thinking about modernity.

#### The ancient doctrines

The Gnostic systems, long known through the refutations of Irenaeus, Hippolytus and Epiphanius, vary in detail but share a structure: the supreme God, unknowable and good, is a stranger to the world; the world was created by an inferior demiurge, ignorant or wicked, often identified with the God of the Old Testament, following a fall within the divine pleroma, that of Wisdom, Sophia, among the Valentinians; man carries within him a divine spark imprisoned in matter, and salvation comes from a revealer, Christ, who teaches him where he comes from and where he is going. Valentinus, in Rome c. 140, and his school, Basilides in Alexandria, the Sethians and the Ophites are its representatives; Marcion, c. 144, who opposes the just God of the Old Testament to the good God of the Gospel and rejects the Jewish Scriptures, is related to them without being a Gnostic in the strict sense; the Manichaeism of Mani, in the third century, is its dualist heir, which Augustine followed for nine years. The discovery in 1945, at Nag Hammadi in Upper Egypt, of thirteen Coptic codices, among them the Gospel of Thomas, the Gospel of Truth and the Apocryphon of John, gave access to the texts themselves. Contempt for the world, a negative theology pushed to dualism, and the elitism of the pneumatics against the psychics and the hylics characterize these currents, which Plotinus also combats in the treatise *Against the Gnostics*, in the name of the beauty of the cosmos.

#### Gnosis as a category

Hans Jonas, a student of Heidegger and Bultmann, reads ancient gnosis in 1934 in the light of existentialism, as the revolt of a man thrown into an alien world, and later reverses the comparison: modern nihilism is a gnosis without God. Eric Voegelin makes gnosis the essence of modernity, from Joachim of Fiore to the totalitarianisms, the claim to a knowledge of the meaning of history and to salvation through action; Blumenberg replies that modernity is on the contrary the second victory over gnosis, Augustine having left the problem of evil within the creature. Buber accuses Jung of a psychological gnosis; Simone Weil, in her letters of 1941 to Déodat Roché, admired the Cathars, medieval heirs of dualism; the word also serves historians of esotericism. In 1996 Michael Williams proposed abandoning the category of Gnosticism as too heterogeneous.

#### What the essay assumes

The essay takes as known the Gnostic schema, the demiurge, Marcion, Nag Hammadi, Plotinus's treatise, Voegelin's polemical use of the term, Blumenberg's reply and Buber's criticism of Jung. It sides with Kelsen and Blumenberg against the Gnostic genealogy of modernity, while keeping the function of secular religions.
