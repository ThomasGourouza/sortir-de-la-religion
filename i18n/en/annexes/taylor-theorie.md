Charles Taylor (b. 1931), a Quebec philosopher, a Catholic, educated at Oxford, professor at McGill and at Oxford, a social democratic candidate in federal elections in the 1960s, co-chair in 2007 of a commission on reasonable accommodation in Quebec, winner of the Templeton and Kyoto prizes, has built a philosophy of modern identity, recognition and secularization in dialogue with Hegel, Heidegger, Wittgenstein and the social sciences.

#### Modern identity

After a thesis against behaviourism and a major book on Hegel (1975), Taylor published *Sources of the Self* (1989), a genealogy of modern identity: the inner self, from Plato to Augustine and Descartes, the affirmation of ordinary life, of work and the family, which comes from the Reformation, and the voice of nature, from Romanticism; behind every identity stand constitutive goods, moral sources, which naturalist modernity cannot articulate without borrowing from the theism or the Romanticism it believes it has left behind. *The Malaise of Modernity* (1991) defends the ideal of authenticity against its individualist degradations, and *The Politics of Recognition* (1992) grounds multiculturalism in the need of identities to be recognized, in the wake of Hegel and Herder. Taylor belongs to the so-called communitarian current, with Sandel, MacIntyre and Walzer, although he rejects the label.

#### A Secular Age

*A Secular Age* (2007), eight hundred pages drawn from the Gifford Lectures of 1999, asks how we passed from a society in which it was virtually impossible not to believe in God, in 1500, to a society in which belief is one option among others, often the hardest one. Taylor distinguishes three senses of the secular: the withdrawal of religion from institutions, the decline of practice, and the change in the conditions of belief, which alone interests him. Against subtraction stories, which see in secularization the unveiling of a human nature rid of illusion, he narrates the construction of an exclusive humanism, first through the Reformation and the discipline of morals, then through providential deism, finally through an immanent frame in which the world is understood without transcendence; the porous self of enchanted societies has become a buffered self, and the nova effect has multiplied spiritual positions. Belief persists, weakened and transformed, and Taylor argues, as a believer, for a fullness that exclusive humanism also seeks without naming it. *Varieties of Religion Today* (2002) rereads James; there followed debates with Habermas on religion in the public sphere and with sociologists on the empirical basis of the thesis.

#### What the essay assumes

The essay assumes familiarity with the three senses of the secular, belief become an option, subtraction stories, the immanent frame, exclusive humanism and the nova effect, which it paraphrases. It shares the rejection of subtraction stories, which joins its own rejection of a total exit, but not Taylor's believing conclusion, and it regards him as a loyal believing adversary: Stirner turned around, you have built something else, answer for it.
