The books of Joshua, Judges, Samuel and Kings tell the history of Israel from the entry into Canaan to the destruction of Jerusalem by the Babylonians. They gave the West its royal figures par excellence, David and Solomon, and gave Christianity the idea of a messiah who is the son of David.

#### From the conquest to the Judges

The book of Joshua relates that the tribes, led by the successor of Moses, cross the Jordan, take Jericho, then subdue the land and divide it among themselves. Archaeology does not confirm this picture: Jericho appears uninhabited at the supposed period, and most specialists describe a gradual settlement of populations originating in Canaan itself, around the twelfth century BCE. The book of Judges then presents a period without a king, punctuated by a cycle: the people abandon their God, are handed over to an oppressor, cry out to him, and a deliverer arises, Deborah against Sisera, Gideon against Midian, Samson against the Philistines.

#### Samuel, Saul and the demand for a king

Samuel, the last of the judges and a prophet, is asked for a king, such as all the nations have. The narrative of 1 S 8 gives him a speech of warning: the king will take their sons for his chariots, their daughters for his kitchens, a tenth of their harvests. God nevertheless consents, and Samuel anoints Saul. After Saul's disobedience, Samuel secretly anoints a young shepherd of Bethlehem, David, the youngest of the sons of Jesse.

#### David

Chapter 17 of 1 Samuel tells of the combat against Goliath: a Philistine champion of gigantic stature defies the army of Israel for forty days; David, who has come to bring provisions to his brothers, refuses Saul's armour, steps forward with his sling and five stones from the brook, fells the giant with a stone to the forehead and cuts off his head with his own sword. There follow the friendship with Jonathan, Saul's jealousy, the flight and the death of the king at Gilboa. David reigns seven years at Hebron over Judah, then takes Jerusalem, makes it his capital and brings the ark of the covenant there. Then David takes Bathsheba, wife of Uriah the Hittite, and has the husband placed in the front line so that he will die; the prophet Nathan tells him the parable of the rich man who steals the poor man's only ewe lamb, and David condemns himself before understanding that it is about him. Bathsheba will later give birth to Solomon. The end of the reign is marked by the revolt of Absalom.

#### Solomon, the Temple and the schism

Solomon asks God for wisdom rather than riches, and shows it by deciding between two women who claim the same child. He builds the Temple of Jerusalem in seven years (1 R 6 to 8), where the ark rests. The narrative reproaches him for his foreign wives, who draw him towards their gods. At his death, around 930, the northern tribes break away: Israel, with Samaria as its capital, and Judah, around Jerusalem. The books of Kings follow them until the capture of Samaria by the Assyrians around 722 and that of Jerusalem in 587 by Nebuchadnezzar, who destroys the Temple and deports the elites to Babylon.

#### Composition and reading

Since Martin Noth (1943), criticism has seen in the whole stretching from Joshua to Kings a Deuteronomistic history, composed under King Josiah at the end of the seventh century, then reworked during the exile to explain the catastrophe by the infidelity of the kings. The historical David remains debated: the Tel Dan stele, discovered in 1993, mentions around 840 a house of David, but the Jerusalem of the tenth century looks modest to the excavators. The genealogies of Matthew and Luke make Jesus descend from him.

#### What the essay assumes

The essay takes as known the Davidic descent of the messiah when, in Part Five, it sets out how Strauss explains the birth in Bethlehem and the descent from David by the faith of a community awaiting a Messiah; it opposes, with Tertullian and then Leo Strauss, Jerusalem to Athens, the revealed Law to Greek reason; and, in the section on Girard, it draws on Frazer's sacrificed kings and on the biblical texts that give the victim a voice, among them the Psalms attributed to David.
