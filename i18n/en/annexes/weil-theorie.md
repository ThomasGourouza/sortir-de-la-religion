Simone Weil (1909-1943), a graduate of the École normale supérieure, agrégée in philosophy, a pupil of Alain, a lycée teacher, a trade-union activist, a factory worker at Alsthom and Renault in 1934-1935, a volunteer in Spain in 1936, touched by Christ at Assisi and at Solesmes in 1937-1938, an exile in Marseille, New York and London, where she dies of tuberculosis and exhaustion at thirty-four, having refused to eat more than the people of occupied France, published only articles in her lifetime. Her friends drew from her notebooks a posthumous body of work that has become one of the most widely read of the twentieth century.

#### Work, force, affliction

The *Réflexions sur les causes de la liberté et de l'oppression sociale* (1934) criticize Marx and every revolutionary hope in the name of an analysis of oppression through the division between those who command and those who carry out, and propose a civilization of work in which the worker would understand what he does; the *Journal d'usine*, in *La Condition ouvrière*, describes the ordeal of the assembly line, which branded her soul like a slave's. *The Iliad, or the Poem of Force* (1940) reads the epic as the picture of force, which turns into a thing whoever undergoes it and whoever wields it. Affliction, distinct from mere suffering, is the state in which the soul is crushed, uprooted, degraded, and in which God seems absent; Weil sees in it the condition in which supernatural love, if it survives, reaches its purity.

#### Grace, attention, decreation

The *Cahiers*, from which Gustave Thibon extracted *Gravity and Grace* (1947), develop a religious metaphysics: gravity governs everything in the soul as in matter, except grace, which descends; God created by withdrawing, and the creature must respond by decreating itself, by renouncing being something, so that God may be through it; one must love God through affliction, consent to necessity without justifying it, and one must have no consolation. Attention, which the *Réflexions sur le bon usage des études scolaires* make the aim of school, is the empty and disinterested waiting that lets truth and others enter into us; prayer is its highest form. *Waiting for God* (1950) gathers her letters to Father Perrin, in which she explains why she remains on the threshold of the Church, out of solidarity with everything it excludes, and her essays on the love of God and affliction. Weil holds Greece, the Upanishads and the Cathars to be centres of truth, and reproaches Israel and Rome with the idolatry of the collective, Plato's Great Beast.

#### The Need for Roots

*The Need for Roots* (1949), written in London for Free France, starts from the needs of the soul, order, liberty, obedience, responsibility, truth, rootedness, and describes modern uprootedness, of workers, of peasants, of the nation; it proposes a spiritual refoundation of France through work and sets duties against rights. Albert Camus, who published it in his Espoir collection, Iris Murdoch, who borrows attention from her, Bataille, who put her into a novel, and Levinas, who fought her, form her first posterity; Giorgio Agamben and Emmanuel Gabellieri reread her today.

#### What the essay assumes

The essay assumes that decreation, attention, affliction, the two atheisms one of which is a purification, religion as consolation being an obstacle to faith, and praying while thinking that God does not exist are known. It holds Weil to be the principal believing candidate for the rank of exception, the most radical, because she refuses consolation and gives no meaning to affliction, holds the Nietzschean objection of the ascetic ideal to be partly right but judges that it misses the essential point, since Weil refuses any meaning to affliction, and turns Marx and Feuerbach around in constructed confrontations.
