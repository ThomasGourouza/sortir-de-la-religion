Dionysus is the Greek god of wine, of intoxication, of the theatre and of metamorphoses. Son of Zeus and of the mortal Semele, born a second time from his father's thigh, he appears in the myths as a god who arrives from elsewhere, always a stranger, whom cities refuse before being forced to welcome him. His cult mingles collective festival, trance and a violence that tragedy has put on stage.

#### The god and his retinue

Dionysus is accompanied by satyrs and by maenads, or bacchants, women seized by the god who leave the city for the mountain, dressed in fawnskins, armed with the thyrsus, a stalk crowned with ivy. The narratives lend them extraordinary acts: making milk and wine spring from the ground, tearing animals apart with their bare hands, the ritual dismemberment called *sparagmos*, and eating their raw flesh. In Athens, the god was honoured by civic festivals, the Dionysia, during which tragedies and comedies were performed: Greek theatre was born in his cult.

#### Euripides' Bacchae

Euripides' last tragedy, performed after his death in 405 BCE, tells of the return of Dionysus to Thebes, his native city, where his divinity is denied. The young king Pentheus, his cousin, has the stranger arrested and wants to forbid the rites. The god drives him to disguise himself as a woman to spy on the bacchants on Cithaeron; discovered, Pentheus is torn to pieces by the women of Thebes, and first of all by his own mother, Agave, who comes back to the city carrying his head, which she takes for a lion's. The god then drives out the royal family. The play has been read as an apology for piety, as a critique of religion, and as a study of the crowd.

#### Nietzsche: the Apollonian and the Dionysian

In *The Birth of Tragedy* (1872), Nietzsche contrasts two drives of Greek art: the Apollonian, dream, form, measure, and the Dionysian, intoxication, fusion, the dissolution of the individual. Tragedy is born of their union, and dies when Socrates and Euripides impose reason on it. In his last writings, Dionysus becomes the name of his affirmation of life, which he sets against the Crucified, symbol of life judged and condemned; *Ecce Homo* ends on this opposition.

#### Girard: the lynching seen from within

René Girard reads *The Bacchae* as an almost transparent description of the scapegoat mechanism: a crisis in which differences fade, a unanimous crowd, a victim torn to pieces, then an order restored that the myth attributes to the god. Taking up Nietzsche's formula, he maintains that the difference between Dionysus and Christ lies in the victim: the Dionysian myth proves the crowd right, the Gospels the victim.

#### What the essay assumes

The essay assumes familiarity with Dionysus, the bacchants and the death of Pentheus, which the box in the Girard section evokes, as well as with the closing formula of *Ecce Homo*, Dionysus against the Crucified, which Girard takes up to say that Nietzsche was right in his diagnosis and wrong in his choice.
