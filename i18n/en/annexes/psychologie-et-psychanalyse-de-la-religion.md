The psychology of religion asks what happens in the individual who believes, prays, converts or loses faith. It emerges in the United States at the end of the nineteenth century, takes with Freud and Jung the form of an interpretation of the depths, and becomes in the twentieth century an experimental science. Four of the essay's authors fall within it.

#### American religious psychology

The first studies are American and empirical: Edwin Starbuck surveys the conversion of adolescents by questionnaire (1899), James Leuba the belief of scientists (1916). William James, in the Gifford Lectures of 1901-1902 published under the title *The Varieties of Religious Experience*, founds the discipline by choosing to study personal religion, the experience of individuals in their solitude, on the basis of autobiographical documents: conversions, mystical states, healthy-minded and sick souls. His pragmatism leads him to judge these experiences by their fruits, not by their causes, and to reject the medical materialism that reduces a vision to a pathology. This tradition continues with Gordon Allport, who contrasts as early as 1950 a mature and an immature religion, then, in the 1960s, intrinsic religiosity, lived for its own sake, and extrinsic religiosity, used for other ends, a distinction that has become standard in measurement scales.

#### Psychoanalysis

Freud takes up religion as early as 1907, comparing rites to obsessional ceremonials, then in *Totem and Taboo* (1913), which derives the sacred from the murder of the primal father, *The Future of an Illusion* (1927), which explains belief by infantile helplessness and the need for paternal protection, *Civilization and Its Discontents* (1930), which discusses Romain Rolland's oceanic feeling, and *Moses and Monotheism* (1939). In these works religion is an illusion, defined by the desire that produces it, and a collective neurosis from which humanity can be cured by education to reality. Jung, after his break with Freud in 1913, takes the opposite path: the psyche has a natural religious function, religious images express archetypes of the collective unconscious, and individuation, the relation of the ego to the Self, accomplishes psychologically what religions called salvation; he refuses to say anything about God outside the psyche. Other psychoanalysts extend the debate: Oskar Pfister, a pastor and friend of Freud, answers him in 1928 with *The Illusion of a Future*; Erik Erikson studies Luther and Gandhi; Donald Winnicott and Ana-Maria Rizzuto locate the representation of God in the transitional space of childhood; Antoine Vergote, at Louvain, builds a psychology of faith and unbelief.

#### Experimental psychology

From the 1960s onwards, the discipline adopts the methods of social and cognitive psychology: scales of religiosity, studies on prayer, religious coping in the face of illness, the relation between religion and health, meditation experiences. Ernest Becker, a cultural anthropologist, proposes in 1973 a synthesis of Kierkegaard, Rank and Freud in which culture and religion answer the terror of death; terror management theory draws from it, from 1986 onwards, an experimental programme whose results have been debated in the light of the replications of the 2010s and 2020s. The cognitive science of religion, born in the 1990s, forms a distinct branch.

#### What the essay assumes

The essay makes James the representative of the regime of experience and the ally of its thesis in the order of belief, Freud the regime of illusion, Jung a transfer of the sacred onto the religious function of the psyche, Becker an explanation by death. It takes for granted the method of the *Varieties*, the difference between Freud and Jung on the origin of religious images, and the existence of an experimental psychology that puts these theories to the test.
