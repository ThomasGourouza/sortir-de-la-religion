Christianity is born within Judaism, and several authors of the essay, Philo, Paul, Maimonides, Spinoza, Levinas, Buber, come from it. Yet Judaism did not remain what it was in the time of Jesus: it rebuilt itself after the destruction of the Temple, produced the Talmud, a philosophy and a mysticism, then passed through modern emancipation. The essay takes these landmarks as known.

#### The Second Temple and its currents

The Second Temple of Jerusalem, rebuilt after the return from the Babylonian exile (c. 515 BCE) and enlarged by Herod, is the centre of the sacrificial cult. Around it, in the time of Jesus, Judaism is divided into currents: the Sadducees, a priestly aristocracy attached to the Temple and to the written Torah alone; the Pharisees, masters of the oral law, who believe in the resurrection and practise a piety of everyday life; the Essenes, a separate community whose manuscripts were found at Qumran in 1947; the Zealots, partisans of revolt against Rome. A large diaspora lives in Egypt, in Asia Minor and in Rome; in Alexandria the Bible has been translated into Greek, the Septuagint, and Philo reads it alongside Plato. The revolt of 66 ends with the capture of Jerusalem and the burning of the Temple by Titus in 70; that of Bar Kokhba (132-135) with the ban on Jews residing in Jerusalem.

#### Rabbinic Judaism

Without Temple or sacrifices, Judaism rebuilds itself around the Torah, the synagogue and study, under the leadership of the heirs of the Pharisees, the rabbis. The oral law is set down in writing in the Mishnah (c. 200), on which the Gemara of Palestine and that of Babylon comment: the Babylonian Talmud, completed around 500, becomes the text of reference. Rabbinic Judaism ignores Philo, who was handed down by Christians, and defines itself against Christianity as much as Christianity defines itself against it. In the Middle Ages, in the lands of Islam and then in Christian Spain, there develop a philosophy, Saadia Gaon, Judah Halevi, Maimonides (1138-1204), whose *Guide for the Perplexed* reconciles the Torah with Aristotle and whose *Mishneh Torah* codifies the law, and a mysticism, the Kabbalah, whose major book is the Zohar, in Castile at the end of the thirteenth century. The expulsion from Spain in 1492 scatters the Sephardim towards the Ottoman Empire, Italy and Amsterdam, where Spinoza will be born, to be expelled from his community by the herem of 1656.

#### Emancipation and modernity

From the eighteenth century onwards, the Haskalah, the Jewish Enlightenment of Moses Mendelssohn in Berlin, opens Judaism to European culture; the French Revolution emancipates the Jews of France in 1791, and the German states follow in the nineteenth century. Judaism then divides into Reform Jews, who adapt the liturgy, Orthodox and Conservative Jews. In Eastern Europe, Hasidism, born in the eighteenth century around the Baal Shem Tov, offers a piety of joy and fervour, which Buber studied. The science of Judaism applies historical criticism to the texts. The twentieth century is that of the Shoah, which destroys the Judaism of Eastern Europe, and of the founding of the State of Israel in 1948. Levinas, Buber, Rosenzweig and Scholem think within this heritage.

#### What the essay assumes

The essay takes as known the pluralism of first-century Judaism, in which Paul is a Pharisee, the break of 70, the difference between Philo's Judaism and rabbinic Judaism, Spinoza's herem, and the fact that Maimonides, Spinoza and Levinas think within a tradition of the Law and of commentary, not of dogma. It says itself that it treats Judaism only in passing and knows it is centred on Christian Europe.

#### Key dates

- C. 515 BCE: dedication of the Second Temple.
- 70: destruction of the Temple; 135: end of the Bar Kokhba revolt.
- C. 200: Mishnah; c. 500: Babylonian Talmud.
- 1204: death of Maimonides; 1492: expulsion from Spain.
- 1656: Spinoza's herem in Amsterdam.
- 1791: emancipation of the Jews of France.
- 1948: founding of the State of Israel.
