Baruch Spinoza (1632-1677), born in Amsterdam into a family of Portuguese Jewish merchants, expelled from his community by the herem of 1656, a lens grinder, lives in Rijnsburg, Voorburg and The Hague, declines a chair at Heidelberg and dies at forty-four. In his lifetime he published under his own name only the *Principles of Cartesian Philosophy* (1663), and anonymously the *Theological-Political Treatise* (1670); the *Ethics* appears in his posthumous works.

#### The Ethics: God, the mind, the affects

The *Ethics*, demonstrated in geometrical order, by definitions, axioms, propositions and scholia, has five parts. The first establishes that there exists only one substance, infinite, cause of itself, God, of which everything that exists is a mode; thought and extension are two of its infinite attributes, among an infinity; God does not act by will or with ends in view, but by the sole necessity of his nature, and the appendix denounces the finalist prejudice and the asylum of ignorance. The second deals with the mind, the idea of the body, and with the parallelism of the two attributes, and distinguishes three kinds of knowledge: imagination, the source of error; reason, through common notions; intuitive science, or the third kind, which grasps singular things under the aspect of eternity. The third defines the conatus, the striving by which each thing perseveres in its being, and deduces from it the geometry of the affects, joy, sadness, desire and their compounds. The fourth, *Of Human Bondage*, shows the power of the affects and defines the good as one's own advantage, virtue as the power to act according to reason. The fifth, *Of Human Freedom*, leads to the intellectual love of God, to blessedness, which is virtue itself, and to the eternity of a part of the mind.

#### The Theological-Political Treatise

Written to defend the freedom to philosophize against the theologians, the *Treatise* founds the historical exegesis of the Bible: Scripture must be interpreted by itself, like nature, by studying its language and the history of each book and of its authors; Moses did not write the Pentateuch; the prophets had a vivid imagination, not a superior knowledge; miracles are natural events poorly understood; faith aims only at obedience and justice, and leaves philosophy free. The second part grounds the state in the pact, defends democracy as the most natural regime and concludes that in a free state everyone may think what he wishes and say what he thinks. The *Political Treatise*, unfinished, extends this theory. The book was banned in 1674 and Spinoza held to be an atheist for a century.

#### Posterity

Bayle judges him a systematic but virtuous atheist; Leibniz visits him and distances himself from him; the pantheism controversy (1785) revives him in Germany, where Lessing, Goethe, Herder, Schleiermacher and Hegel celebrate him; Nietzsche recognizes him as a precursor; Freud, Einstein, Deleuze, Comte-Sponville and Hadot claim him as their own; Leo Strauss sees in him the founder of the modern critique of religion.

#### What the essay assumes

The essay assumes familiarity with the structure of the *Ethics*, the doctrine of substance and attributes, the kinds of knowledge, the conatus, the *Treatise*'s thesis on faith and obedience, and the critique of Maimonides in chapter VII. It makes Spinoza the major exception, the position of immanence, who keeps the name of God out of conviction, and discusses the objections of Hegel, of Leo Strauss in the eleventh part, and the one it formulates itself: Spinoza does not explain the need for error.
