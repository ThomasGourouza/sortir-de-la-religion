Before being the Christ of the creeds, Jesus is, in the Gospels, an itinerant preacher from Galilee who proclaims the Kingdom of God, speaks in parables, heals and eats with the excluded. These notes summarize what the first three Gospels report of his teaching and the way historians read it.

#### John the Baptist and the Kingdom

The Gospels begin Jesus' activity with his baptism in the Jordan, received from John, an ascetic prophet who announces an imminent judgement and calls for repentance, and whom Josephus also knows. After John's arrest, Jesus preaches in Galilee that the Kingdom of God has come near (Mc 1, 15); Matthew writes Kingdom of Heaven out of respect for the divine name. The Kingdom is not a place but the reign of God that is coming: one prays for its coming in the Lord's Prayer, and yet it is said to be already present in the exorcisms and in the midst of the listeners. Historians have been divided for a century: Albert Schweitzer (1906) sees in it the expectation of an imminent end of the world, which did not happen; C. H. Dodd (1935) a realized eschatology, the Kingdom already there in the person of Jesus; most today hold the two aspects together.

#### The parables

Jesus teaches in parables, short stories taken from the life of fields, houses and markets: the sower, the mustard seed that becomes a tree, the tares left until the harvest, the hidden treasure, the talents, the labourers of the eleventh hour paid like the first. The parable does not define the Kingdom; it gives a glimpse of it through a comparison that surprises and overturns expectation, and it leaves the listener to decide.

#### The miracles

The Gospels report healings, exorcisms, raisings of the dead and nature miracles. Historians observe that the reputation of healer and exorcist is one of the best-attested traits, acknowledged even by adversaries, who explain it by magic, like Celsus, or by the prince of demons, like the scribes in Mark; they leave the interpretation of the facts open. In the Gospels, the miracles are signs of the Kingdom rather than proofs, and Jesus refuses to give any to those who demand them.

#### The meals, the Law, the enemies

Jesus eats with publicans, tax collectors in the service of Rome, and with sinners, which scandalizes the Pharisees; women follow him and support him. He does not reject the Law but discusses it from within: the sabbath is made for man, purity comes from the heart, the greatest commandment is the love of God and of one's neighbour. He calls for love of one's enemies and for forgiveness without limit. He most often calls himself the Son of Man, an expression that can mean simply a human being, but which also refers to the figure coming on the clouds in Daniel 7, to whom an everlasting kingdom is given.

#### Composition and reading

These materials come from Mark and from the sayings source shared by Matthew and Luke; John replaces them with signs and discourses. Each age has drawn from them a different Jesus, a teacher of morality, a gentle Galilean, a prophet of the end.

#### What the essay assumes

The essay draws on this teaching through its readers. Kant makes Christ the ideal of humanity well-pleasing to God, which reason carries within itself and recognizes in the Gospel narrative, so that historicity becomes indifferent. Renan holds miracle to be impossible to establish, because no wonder-worker has ever performed before a commission of scientists, and rediscovers in Galilee a human figure in the narratives of Matthew and Mark. Nietzsche, in *The Antichrist*, describes a Jesus without resentment or dogma who lives in an inner kingdom without preaching it. The section on the existence of Jesus retains Schweitzer's apocalyptic prophet, taken up by Meier and Fredriksen. Simone Weil, finally, is cited for her refusal of consolation; attention, which Murdoch borrows from her, is for her a religious category, in its highest degree identical with prayer.
