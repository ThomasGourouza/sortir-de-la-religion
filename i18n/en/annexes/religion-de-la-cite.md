Greek religion is first a matter of collective practices rather than of beliefs: honouring the gods of the city with sacrifices, festivals, processions and contests, according to the customs of the ancestors. It has no revealed book, no dogma, no Church, no clergy distinct from the citizens, and it is the city itself, through its magistrates and its assemblies, that organizes the cults and punishes those who threaten them.

#### Sacrifice and festivals

The central rite is blood sacrifice. A domestic animal, an ox, a sheep or a goat, is led to the altar in procession, consecrated, slaughtered; the bones wrapped in fat burn for the gods, and the meat is shared among the participants in a banquet. Sacrifice thus fixes the place of each: the gods receive the smoke, men eat, and the sharing of the meat outlines the civic community. Alongside sacrifices, libations, offerings and prayers accompany daily life. The Athenian calendar counted dozens of festivals, among them the Panathenaea in honour of Athena and the Dionysia, during which the tragedies were performed.

#### Sanctuaries and oracles

The great Panhellenic sanctuaries, Olympia, Delphi, Delos, brought the Greeks together beyond their cities. At Delphi, the Pythia, seated on the tripod, delivered the oracles of Apollo, which priests put into shape; cities and individuals came to consult her there about the founding of a colony, a war, a purification. On the temple were inscribed maxims, the most famous of which invited one to know oneself, and which Socrates took up as the programme of philosophy.

#### Impiety and its trials

In the absence of dogma, impiety, *asébeia*, does not consist in believing wrongly but in offending the gods of the city: profaning a sanctuary, parodying mysteries, mutilating statues, teaching doctrines that turn people away from worship. In Athens, several trials targeted it in the fifth century: the philosopher Anaxagoras, who held the sun to be a red-hot stone, had to leave the city; the sophist Protagoras is said to have been prosecuted for his agnosticism; in 415, the mutilated Herms and the parody of the Eleusinian mysteries set off a wave of trials. In 399, Socrates was accused of not recognizing the gods of the city, of introducing new divinities and of corrupting the young; condemned to death, he drank the hemlock.

#### From Greece to Rome

Roman religion shares this civic character: a public cult regulated by colleges of priests who are also magistrates, a piety defined as ritual exactness. The Christians, who refused to sacrifice to the gods and to the emperor, were accused of atheism, like Socrates before them, and the apologists drew an argument from this parallel.

#### What the essay assumes

The essay assumes familiarity with the trial of Socrates and the notion of impiety, which Plato's *Laws* turn into an offence punished by prison and death, as well as with the accusation of atheism brought against the Christians, which Justin compares to the one brought against Socrates. It also assumes the idea of a religion of rites rather than of beliefs, that of Cicero's *relegere*, and the begging priests of Cybele to whom Celsus compares the Christians.
