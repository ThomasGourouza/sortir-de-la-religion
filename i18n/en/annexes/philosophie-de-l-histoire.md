The philosophy of history asks whether human history has a meaning, a direction and an end. The expression is Voltaire's, but the question comes from Christian theology, and it is this link that Löwith, Voegelin and Blumenberg discuss in Part Eleven of the essay.

#### The history of salvation

The Greeks thought of time as a cycle and of history as repetition; the Bible introduces an oriented time, from creation to judgement, in which God acts through unique events. Augustine, in *The City of God* (413-426), writing against the pagans who blamed Christianity for the sack of Rome, produces the first theology of history: two cities, that of God and that of men, intermingled until the end of time, and a history divided into six ages, on the model of the six days; the meaning of history is invisible and reserved to God. Joachim of Fiore, a Calabrian abbot who died in 1202, breaks with this reserve by announcing a third age, that of the Spirit, after those of the Father and the Son, which is to be realized within history itself; his scheme will haunt millenarian movements and, according to Löwith and Voegelin, the modern philosophies of progress. Bossuet, in the *Discourse on Universal History* (1681), gives the last great Christian version: Providence guides empires in order to prepare and preserve the Church.

#### Progress and reason in history

Vico, in the *New Science* (1725), proposes a history of nations in three ages, of gods, of heroes and of men, governed by an immanent providence, and founds the idea that man can know history because he makes it. The Enlightenment replaces Providence with progress: Turgot, and Condorcet in the *Sketch for a Historical Picture of the Progress of the Human Mind* (1795), written under the Terror, describe a march of humanity towards enlightenment and happiness. Kant looks for a guiding thread in a purpose of nature; Herder sees in each people a singular expression of humanity. Hegel, in his lectures on the philosophy of history, makes universal history the progress in the consciousness of freedom, in which Spirit realizes itself through the cunning of reason, which uses the passions of individuals: the end of history is the modern State in which freedom is recognized. Comte gives it the form of the law of the three stages, Marx that of historical materialism, in which class struggle leads to the classless society; both, according to Löwith, transpose eschatology.

#### The critiques of the twentieth century

After the catastrophes of the century, the philosophy of history is put on trial. Löwith, in *Meaning in History* (1949), shows, by going back from Burckhardt and Marx to Augustine and the Bible, that the modern idea of progress is a secularized eschatology, and concludes with a withdrawal towards the ancient wisdom of the cosmos. Voegelin sees in it a gnosis. Aron, as early as 1938, holds that history has no single meaning and that the choice of one always remains open; Popper denounces, in a book of 1957 drawn from articles of 1944-1945, the poverty of historicism, the claim to predict the course of history; Camus speaks of the divinization of history. Blumenberg replies to Löwith that progress is not a disguised eschatology but a new answer to an old question. Francis Fukuyama revives in 1989 the Hegelian theme of the end of history.

#### What the essay assumes

The essay assumes familiarity with the passage from cyclical to oriented time, Augustine's theology of history and Joachim of Fiore's scheme, the Hegelian doctrine of reason in history, and the debate between Löwith and Blumenberg on secularization, which it settles, with Blumenberg against Löwith, in favour of function rather than filiation: the History of the revolutionaries is a strict refoundation because it demands sacrifice, not because it descends from eschatology.
