David Hume (1711-1776), a Scot from Edinburgh, a successful historian, diplomat and librarian, a friend of Adam Smith and of the Encyclopedists, never obtained a chair because of his reputation as an unbeliever. He is the most consistent of the empiricists and the Enlightenment philosopher who most lastingly shook natural religion, without professing atheism.

#### Empiricism and causation

*A Treatise of Human Nature* (1739-1740), which Hume said fell dead-born from the press, and then *An Enquiry concerning Human Understanding* (1748) ground knowledge in experience: all our ideas derive from impressions, and an idea without a corresponding impression is empty. The relation of cause and effect, on which all reasoning about matters of fact rests, comes neither from reason nor from the experience of a necessary connection: we observe only constant succession, and it is habit that makes us expect the effect and project a necessity onto things. Belief is a feeling, not a demonstration, and induction has no rational foundation. The self itself is only a bundle of perceptions. This scepticism is not a paralysis: nature makes us believe, act and play backgammon, and philosophy must take its rule from common life. The *Enquiry* contains the chapter on miracles: no testimony can establish a miracle, for it is always more probable that the witness is mistaken or lying than that the law of nature has been violated.

#### Morals and politics

Books II and III of the *Treatise* and *An Enquiry concerning the Principles of Morals* (1751) make reason the slave of the passions and ground morality in sentiment: we approve what is useful or agreeable, to ourselves or to others, through sympathy. Justice is an artificial virtue born of conventions. The *Essays* and the *History of England* (1754-1762) develop a moderate political thought, hostile to religious enthusiasm as well as to superstition.

#### Religion

*The Natural History of Religion* (1757) does not ask whether religion is true but where it comes from: not from the contemplation of the order of the world, but from fear and hope in the face of life's events; polytheism is the primitive religion, monotheism emerges from it through the progressive flattery of one god, and the two alternate in a flux and reflux; monotheism, more rational, is more intolerant. The *Dialogues concerning Natural Religion*, completed before his death and published in 1779, have three characters debate: Cleanthes, who defends the argument from design, Demea, who defends the a priori proof and an incomprehensible God, and Philo, the sceptic, who ruins both, poses the problem of evil in the form of Epicurus's trilemma and concludes, in an ambiguous twelfth part, that the cause of the order of the world probably bears some remote analogy to human intelligence, which grounds neither worship nor morality. Hume died serene, without religion, as Boswell observed with dismay.

#### What the essay assumes

The essay assumes familiarity with empiricism, the critique of causation, the chapter on miracles, the plan of the *Natural History* and of the *Dialogues*, and the three characters. It makes Hume the only one of the five Enlightenment authors to put nothing in the place of God, the sceptic of the series who replaces God with no capital letter but offers no way of life, and notes that Boyer, in a constructed confrontation, finds in him the faces in the moon, and Norris and Inglehart fear.
