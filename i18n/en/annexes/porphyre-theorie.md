Porphyry (c. 234-c. 305), born in Tyre in Phoenicia, a pupil of Longinus in Athens and then of Plotinus in Rome from 263 to 268, is at once the editor of his master, a philosopher and an encyclopaedic scholar, and the author of the most feared anti-Christian treatise of Antiquity. His very extensive work survives only in part.

#### The editor and the logician

Porphyry arranged the treatises of Plotinus into six *Enneads* and prefaced them with a *Life of Plotinus*, an almost unique source on the philosopher, which recounts his ecstasies, his refusal to let himself be painted, his remark about the gods who must come to him, and the crisis that Porphyry himself went through, when Plotinus turned him away from suicide by sending him to Sicily. His *Isagoge*, an introduction to Aristotle's *Categories*, translated into Latin by Boethius, was the logic textbook of the whole Middle Ages and raised, concerning genera and species, the question that would become the dispute over universals. He commented on Aristotle and Plato, wrote on abstinence from meat and on the cave of the nymphs in the *Odyssey*, interpreted allegorically, and composed for his wife a *Letter to Marcella* that sums up a philosophical piety: honouring God through silence and virtue rather than through sacrifices.

#### The religious thinker

The *Philosophy from Oracles*, a youthful work, collects and comments on oracles of Apollo and Hecate in order to find in them a way of salvation through rites; it is there that, according to Augustine, the gods declare Christ pious and immortal and the Christians defiled. Later, Porphyry shows himself reserved towards theurgy, which Iamblichus would defend against him in the *De mysteriis*, and places the salvation of the soul in philosophy. He is thus, within Neoplatonism, the representative of an inward and rational religion, and the last before the school's ritual turn.

#### Against the Christians

The treatise in fifteen books, written around 270 according to the most common dating, was banned and burned by Constantine, and again in 448; only fragments remain, quoted by those who refuted it, Eusebius, Methodius, Apollinaris, Jerome, Augustine, Macarius Magnes, and their attribution is often disputed. In it Porphyry applies philological and historical criticism to the Bible: he points out the contradictions between the gospels, the quarrel between Peter and Paul, the ignorance of the apostles, and above all he demonstrates, in Book XII, that the book of Daniel was written not in the 6th century by the prophet but in the 2nd, under Antiochus Epiphanes, by an author who told the past in the form of prophecy; modern exegesis has proved him right. He denies neither the existence nor the wisdom of Jesus, but accuses his disciples of having divinized him. Julian, a century later, would take up his arguments.

#### What the essay assumes

The essay takes as known Porphyry's connection with Plotinus and with Origen, whom he had met when young, the fragment on Daniel, in which it sees an anticipation of Spinoza and of David Friedrich Strauss, the passage of the *Philosophy from Oracles* quoted by Augustine, and the thesis of a wise Jesus betrayed by his disciples, which Renan and Nietzsche would take up. Augustine answers him in the *City of God*, and it is from this answer that the essay dates the form of Western theology.
