Pierre Bayle (1647-1706), son of a pastor of the county of Foix, converted to Catholicism and then returned to Calvinism, professor at Sedan, a refugee in Rotterdam in 1681 after the closure of the Protestant academy, teaches there, edits the *Nouvelles de la République des Lettres* and composes a body of scholarly and polemical work that nourished the Enlightenment. His personal faith is the subject of a debate that still continues.

#### The Various Thoughts on the Comet

The comet of 1680 gives Bayle the pretext for a long anonymous treatise (1682, enlarged in 1683) against the belief that comets portend misfortune. The argument widens: the authority of tradition proves nothing, for an error handed down remains an error; human beings act not according to their principles but according to their passions, and convinced Christians commit every crime; idolatry, which attributes vices to the gods, is worse than atheism; and a society of atheists could subsist as well as a society of pagans, provided the laws punish crimes, for human justice makes the virtue of most people. These theses, which separate morality from religion, cause a scandal; the pastor Jurieu, Bayle's colleague in Rotterdam and long his protector, becomes his relentless adversary and in 1693 obtains his dismissal.

#### Toleration

The revocation of the Edict of Nantes in 1685 and his brother's death in prison lead Bayle to write the *Philosophical Commentary* on compel them to come in (1686-1688), a refutation of the Augustinian interpretation of the parable that justified religious coercion. Bayle argues in it that conscience, even when erring, has rights: whoever errs in good faith must follow his conscience, and no one can be compelled to believe, since coercion produces only hypocrisy; toleration must extend to all confessions, and even to atheists. He goes further than Locke, whose *Letter Concerning Toleration* (1689) excludes atheists and Catholics, and opposes Jurieu, who demands toleration for persecuted Protestants while refusing it to others.

#### The Dictionary

The *Historical and Critical Dictionary* (1697, 1702), an enormous work in which the text of the articles is submerged by remarks in small type, is at once an undertaking to correct the errors of earlier dictionaries and a laboratory of scepticism. The articles David, Manichaeans, Paulicians, Pyrrho and Spinoza, the longest, raise the problem of evil, conclude that reason cannot solve it and that one must rely on faith, a fideist position that his adversaries take for a ruse and that Leibniz sets out to refute in the *Theodicy*. The *Dictionary* was the bedside book of Voltaire, Diderot and Hume; the Enlightenment drew method and arguments from it, and the debate over Bayle, believing sceptic or concealed atheist, still sets Labrousse against Mori.

#### What the essay assumes

The essay assumes familiarity with paragraphs CXIV and CLXI of the *Various Thoughts*, the quarrel with Jurieu, an attested response, Bayle's position on toleration and the problem of evil, and Leibniz's response. It places Bayle among the five answers of the constructed debate on morality without God, morality holds through society, and confronts him with Ivan Karamazov: order against guilt.
