The poetic and wisdom books form the heart of the Writings, the third part of the Hebrew canon after the Law and the Prophets. Less narrative than the others, they give voice to prayer, to lament and to reflection on the human condition.

#### The Psalms

The Psalter gathers one hundred and fifty poems. Since Hermann Gunkel, exegetes have classified them by genre: hymns of praise, supplications, thanksgivings, royal psalms. Nearly half bear the name of David, whom tradition holds to be their author; criticism sees in them a collection formed over several centuries, from the monarchy to the Persian period. They became the daily prayer of Christian monks and the book of the Old Testament most often quoted in the New.

#### Proverbs

Attributed to Solomon, Proverbs collects maxims of practical wisdom. The first nine chapters stage personified Wisdom, begotten before the world: a figure that the Jewish and Christian traditions would later associate with the Torah and with the Logos.

#### Job

The Book of Job raises the problem that Proverbs seemed to solve, that of retribution. A prose prologue relates that Job, an upright man, is put to the test following a wager between God and the Satan: he loses his flocks, his children, his health, and refuses to curse God. Three friends come to console him and maintain that he must have sinned since he suffers; Job protests his innocence, accuses God of injustice and asks to plead his case. A fourth, Elihu, intervenes. God finally answers out of the whirlwind (Jb 38-41), not with an explanation but with a series of questions about creation; Job falls silent and retracts. The epilogue vindicates Job against his friends and restores to him twice his former goods. The question of the suffering of the righteous remains open.

#### Qoheleth and the Song

Qoheleth, in Greek Ecclesiastes, presents himself as son of David and king in Jerusalem. He repeats that all is vanity, vapour, a chasing after wind; that there is a time for everything; that the wise man and the fool die the same death; and he advises eating, drinking and enjoying one's labour, for such is man's portion. The Song of Songs is a collection of love poems between a young woman and her beloved, with no mention of God; the rabbis and then the Fathers read it as the allegory of the love between God and Israel, or between Christ and the Church. The Wisdom of Solomon and Sirach are deuterocanonical, absent from the Hebrew Bible.

#### Composition and readings of Job

Criticism dates Job and Qoheleth to the Persian or Hellenistic period; the dialogue of Job may have been inserted into an older tale. Gregory the Great makes it, in the *Moralia*, a moral allegory; Maimonides, in the *Guide* III, 22 and 23, sees in it a parable on providence in which the friends represent philosophical opinions; Leibniz counts it among the sources of the *Theodicy*; Kant, in 1791, contrasts Job's sincerity with the speeches of his friends; Jung, in *Answer to Job* (1952), reads in it a God unaware of his own shadow, morally surpassed by his creature; Girard, in *Job, the Victim of His People* (1985), sees in Job the scapegoat of a unanimous community whose spokesmen are the friends.

#### What the essay assumes

The essay sets out Jung's reading, for whom Job becomes morally superior to the God who crushes him to win a wager, and compares it with Ivan Karamazov siding with the child against universal harmony; it presents Leibniz's *Theodicy* and the best of all possible worlds as a way of thinking that asks the individual to sacrifice his complaint; it places Job, in Girard's reading, among the biblical texts that proclaim the innocence of the victim; and it notes, in the debate on wonder, that Schelling quotes Qoheleth, the old book according to which all is toil under the sun, before raising the question of being.
