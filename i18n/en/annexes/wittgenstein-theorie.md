Ludwig Wittgenstein (1889-1951), son of one of the richest men in Vienna, engineer, pupil of Russell at Cambridge, volunteer soldier in 1914, village schoolteacher, architect, gardener in a monastery, then professor at Cambridge from 1939 to 1947, wrote two philosophies, that of the *Tractatus* and that of the *Investigations*, and published only one book of philosophy in his lifetime. He read the Gospels and Tolstoy, thought of entering holy orders, and said that his thought was religious without his being so.

#### The Tractatus

The *Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus* (1921), completed during the war, in 1918, sets out in seven numbered propositions a theory of language and the world: the world is the totality of facts, the proposition is a picture of facts, and logic shows the form common to language and the world without being able to say it. What can be said are the propositions of natural science; logic is tautology; metaphysics, ethics, aesthetics, which want to say what can only show itself, are nonsense, but nonsense that matters: the sense of the world must lie outside the world, God does not reveal himself in the world, it is not how the world is that is the mystical, but that it is, and one must keep silent about what one cannot speak of. The Vienna Circle saw in it an antimetaphysical charter; Wittgenstein wrote to his publisher that the essential part of the book was the unwritten part, ethics. The *Lecture on Ethics* (1929) says that ethics runs against the limits of language and names its experiences: wondering at the existence of the world, feeling absolutely safe.

#### The Investigations

Having returned to philosophy in 1929, Wittgenstein undoes his first doctrine. The *Philosophical Investigations*, *Philosophische Untersuchungen*, published in 1953, replace the single picture of language with a multiplicity of language games, rule-governed activities interwoven with forms of life, whose meaning is use; concepts hang together through family resemblances; there is no private language, and following a rule is a practice. Philosophy explains nothing, it describes and dissolves the confusions born of grammar; it leaves everything as it is. The *Remarks on Frazer's Golden Bough* argue that rites are not theoretical errors but expressions, and that Frazer is more savage than his savages; the lectures on religious belief treat faith as a picture that regulates a life, not as a hypothesis; *On Certainty*, notes from 1949-1951 published in 1969, examines the propositions that form the bedrock, beyond doubt, of our language games.

#### Posterity

The *Tractatus* nourished logical positivism, the *Investigations* ordinary language philosophy, Anscombe, Malcolm, Cavell, and a philosophy of religion, Phillips, Winch, that has been called fideist in spite of itself; Bouveresse and Monk have given the standard French and biographical readings of it.

#### What the essay assumes

The essay assumes that propositions 6.4 to 7 of the *Tractatus*, the *Lecture on Ethics* and the *Remarks on Frazer*, an attested response, are known. It makes Wittgenstein the silent and strictest version of its exceptions, a mystic without God and without doctrine, a refusal to let an answer occupy the place of the question, and applies propositions 6.371 and 6.372 to science itself.
