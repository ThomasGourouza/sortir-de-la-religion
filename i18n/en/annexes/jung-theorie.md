Carl Gustav Jung (1875-1961), son of a Swiss Reformed pastor, psychiatrist at the Burghölzli asylum in Zurich, Freud's designated heir from 1907 to 1913, and after their break the founder of analytical psychology, built a depth psychology in which religion is no longer a neurosis to be cured but a function of the soul to be understood. His influence extends from psychotherapy to the history of religions and to mass culture.

#### The break with Freud

*Wandlungen und Symbole der Libido* (1912) widens Freud's libido into a general psychic energy and reads myths as expressions of the unconscious; Freud sees in it a desertion, and the break is consummated in 1913. Jung then passes through a crisis lasting several years, of which the *Red Book*, calligraphed and painted, published in 2009, preserves the trace, and from which his concepts emerge. *Psychological Types* (1921) distinguishes introversion and extraversion and four functions, thinking, feeling, sensation, intuition, from which personality tests derive.

#### Archetypes, collective unconscious, individuation

Beneath the personal unconscious, made of repressed contents, Jung posits a collective unconscious, inherited and common to the species, structured by archetypes, empty and universal forms filled by the images of each culture: the shadow, the anima and the animus, the great mother, the wise old man, the child, the hero, and the Self, centre and totality of the psyche, whose symbol is the mandala. The task of psychic life is individuation, the process by which the ego integrates the shadow and the other figures and enters into relation with the Self; the second half of life is devoted to it, and religions have been its traditional paths. Jung seeks evidence for these structures in his patients' dreams, in myths, in gnosis, in alchemy, which he reads as a projection of the process of individuation, in yoga and in the *I Ching*. Synchronicity, set out in 1952 in a volume shared with the physicist Pauli, posits meaningful coincidences without causal connection.

#### Religion

*Psychology and Religion* (1938), the Terry Lectures given at Yale, defines religion as the scrupulous observation of the numinosum, in the manner of Cicero and Otto, and asserts that the psyche has a natural religious function whose denial makes one ill. Jung refuses to make any statement about God in himself and speaks only of the image of God in the soul; asked on the BBC in 1959 whether he believes in God, he answers that he knows. *Answer to Job* (1952) reads the Bible as the drama of an unconscious God whom Job, through his moral consciousness, surpasses, and who must become incarnate in order to become conscious; the dogma of the Assumption, defined in 1950, seems to him the most important religious event since the Reformation. Buber reproaches him in 1952 with reducing God to the psyche and reviving gnosis. The article Wotan (1936) describes Nazism as the awakening of an archaic god in the German soul, and Jung's conduct in the 1930s remains debated.

#### What the essay assumes

The essay takes as known the break with Freud, the archetypes, the Self, individuation, the definition of religion of 1938, the answer given on the BBC, *Answer to Job* and the controversy with Buber. It sides with Buber, judges that Jung makes religion necessary without making it true, Durkheim transposed to the psyche, and reproaches him with having believed that the religious disposition was in itself a wisdom.
