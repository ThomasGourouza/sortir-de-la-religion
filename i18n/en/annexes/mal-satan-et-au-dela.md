The Bible has no single doctrine of death, the devil and the afterlife: its representations change over nearly a millennium, and most of those that Christianity has made familiar, hell, the devil as chief of the demons, the judgement of souls, appear late in the texts.

#### Sheol and the silence of the dead

In most of the books of the Old Testament, the dead go down to Sheol, an underground and indistinct abode where the righteous and the wicked share the same shadow, far from God, without praise or reward. Retribution takes place here below, in long life, descendants and prosperity, which is precisely what Job and Qoheleth dispute. There is neither paradise nor hell for individuals.

#### The serpent, the Satan and the fallen angels

The serpent of Genesis is not the devil: it is a cunning animal, and its identification with the devil comes later, in the Wisdom of Solomon and then in the Apocalypse. In Job and Zechariah, the Satan, the adversary, is a member of the heavenly court who accuses human beings before God, with his permission. The Persian and Hellenistic period, probably under the influence of Iranian dualism, develops darker figures: the book of Enoch tells of the fall of the Watchers, angels who united with women, the source of the demons, and the Qumran manuscripts set the Prince of Light against Belial. The New Testament inherits this world: Satan tempts Jesus in the desert, exorcisms deliver the possessed, and the Apocalypse shows the dragon, the ancient serpent, cast down from heaven.

#### Resurrection and judgement

The hope of a resurrection of the dead appears clearly in the book of Daniel, c. 165 BCE: many of those who sleep in the dust will awake, some to everlasting life, others to shame. The second book of Maccabees links it to the martyrs. In the time of Jesus, the Pharisees believe in it, the Sadducees do not. The gospels announce the judgement of the nations by the Son of Man (Mt 25), speak of Gehenna, the name of a valley at Jerusalem associated with child sacrifice that became the image of the fire that is never quenched, and of Lazarus carried to Abraham's bosom while the rich man suffers. Paul awaits the resurrection of bodies at the return of Christ.

#### What tradition has built

Hell as an eternal place of punishment, purgatory, whose birth Jacques Le Goff places in the twelfth century, limbo, the natural immortality of the soul taken over from Plato, Dante's geography, are later developments that read the biblical texts in the light of Greek philosophy and medieval pastoral care. Origen hoped for a final restoration of all, condemned in the sixth century; Augustine fixes the eternity of punishment for the West. The problem of evil, finally, is not treated by the Bible as a theoretical question: Job raises it without resolving it, and theodicy is a philosophical invention, which Leibniz names in 1710.

#### What the essay assumes

The essay draws on these figures in several places. In Dostoevsky, Zosima defines hell as the suffering of no longer being able to love, the reverse of a place of punishment; in Feuerbach, the flames of hell are only the sparks of the furious gaze that faith casts on unbelievers; in Rousseau, civil religion replaces hell with exile and the scaffold; in Girard, Satan is the accuser, the principle of the unanimous crowd, whose fall is the end of the power to accuse without being unmasked. It assumes familiarity with the distance between these images and the texts from which they come.
