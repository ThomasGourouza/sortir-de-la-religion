After the death of Alexander in 323 BCE, in a Greek world enlarged to the dimensions of an empire, three new schools share Athens with the Academy and the Lyceum: the Porch, the Garden and Scepticism. All three offer a wisdom, an art of living happily in a world the citizen no longer governs, and it is of them that Hadot thinks first when he defines ancient philosophy as a way of life.

#### Stoicism

Founded around 300 by Zeno of Citium, who taught under the Painted Porch, the Stoa, Stoicism was developed by Cleanthes and Chrysippus, then adapted to Rome by Panaetius and Posidonius, and illustrated under the Empire by Seneca, Epictetus, a freed slave, and Marcus Aurelius, an emperor. The doctrine is divided into logic, physics and ethics. The world is a single living being, pervaded by a divine breath, the logos, reason and artistic fire, which orders everything according to providence and fate; the sage consents to it, and the universe is periodically consumed and reborn. Ethics teaches one to distinguish what depends on us, our judgements and our desires, from what does not, the body, reputation, fortune; virtue alone is a good, the passions are false judgements, and the sage, apathetic in the sense of free from passion, lives according to nature and reason, a citizen of the world. The *Meditations* of Marcus Aurelius and the *Handbook* of Epictetus are written exercises, and Stoic theology, pantheist, rational, tolerant of cults reinterpreted through allegory, nourished the Christian apologists and Spinoza.

#### Epicureanism

Founded in 306 in Epicurus's Garden, Epicureanism offers an atomist and materialist physics, indifferent gods living in the intermundia, a mortal soul, and a happiness defined as the absence of disturbance, obtained through knowledge of nature, the limitation of desires and friendship. Lucretius is its poet, and the school, slandered as libertine, lasted until the fourth century CE before being reborn with Gassendi.

#### Scepticism

Pyrrho of Elis, who wrote nothing, taught the suspension of judgement, epochè, as the source of tranquillity; the New Academy of Arcesilaus and Carneades practised it against the Stoics; Aenesidemus and then Sextus Empiricus, in the second century CE, systematized the modes of suspension, the tropes, in the *Outlines of Pyrrhonism*, whose rediscovery in the sixteenth century supplied Montaigne and the fideists with their arguments, and the moderns, Descartes, Bayle, Hume, with the problem of certainty. Ancient Scepticism is also a way of life, without dogma, following appearances and customs.

#### Legacies

The three schools supplied nascent Christianity with concepts, the logos, providence, moral conscience, conscientia, and with adversaries; the Renaissance and the classical age, Justus Lipsius, Montaigne, Charron, Gassendi, Spinoza, with the model of a wisdom without revelation; Hadot and Foucault, then Comte-Sponville and contemporary Stoics, with the idea of secular spiritual exercises.

#### What the essay assumes

The essay assumes familiarity with the framework of the three schools, Stoic theology, Epicurean ataraxia, sceptical suspension and its rediscovery by Montaigne, and the reading of Hadot, for whom ancient philosophy is a choice of life. It finds there the origin of the spiritualities of exercise that its twelfth part examines, and recalls, as early as the transition of its first part, that these wisdoms presupposed an elite.
