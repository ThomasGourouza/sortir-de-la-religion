Friedrich Engels (1820-1895), the son of a Pietist industrialist from Barmen, in the Rhineland, self-taught, a partner in the family firm in Manchester, was Marx's companion for forty years, his financial support, co-author of the *Manifesto* and of *The German Ideology*, editor of volumes II and III of *Capital* and, after 1883, the intellectual guide of European social democracy. On religion, he wrote more than Marx.

#### The young Engels and lost faith

Brought up in the Pietist revival of the Wupper valley, Engels lost his faith around 1839 through reading Strauss, then Hegel and Feuerbach, and recounted this crisis in his letters to the Graeber brothers. His *Letters from Wuppertal* describe the poverty of the workers and the hypocrisy of the pious manufacturers. *The Condition of the Working Class in England* (1845), the fruit of his stay in Manchester from 1842 to 1844, followed shortly after his decisive meeting with Marx in Paris, in August 1844.

#### Materialism and history

*Anti-Dühring* (1878), a refutation of a Berlin professor, became the manual of Marxism: it sets out the dialectics of nature, the three laws, the passage from quantity to quality, the interpenetration of opposites, the negation of the negation, and the materialist conception of history, then socialism become a science; its extract, *Socialism: Utopian and Scientific*, was the most widely circulated Marxist book. *The Origin of the Family, Private Property and the State* (1884), following Morgan, recounts the passage from the society of the gens to the State. *Ludwig Feuerbach and the End of Classical German Philosophy* (1886) takes stock of the Hegelian left: the fundamental question of philosophy is the relation of thought to being; Feuerbach restored materialism but remained an idealist in morality and religion, seeking to complete religion with a cult of love instead of abolishing it; the 1888 pamphlet edition publishes Marx's *Theses on Feuerbach* as an appendix.

#### The history of early Christianity

Engels wrote three studies of Christianity: *The Peasant War in Germany* (1850), which reads Thomas Müntzer's revolt of 1525 as a class struggle in religious garb; *Bruno Bauer and Early Christianity* (1882), which salutes the radical critic while placing the birth of Christianity in the crisis of the Roman Empire; and *On the History of Early Christianity* (1894-1895), which compares the first Christians to the modern workers' movement: a religion of slaves, freedmen, the poor and subject peoples, promising a salvation which, failing to be earthly, was placed in the beyond, and of which the Apocalypse of John, dated to 68-69, is the oldest document. Engels does not doubt that Jesus existed, and he sees in Christianity a revolutionary movement led astray.

#### What the essay assumes

The essay assumes familiarity with the 1886 critique of Feuerbach, which supplies it with the distinction between soft refoundation and strict refoundation, with the 1894 text on Christianity as a movement of the oppressed, which it cites in connection with Paul and emancipation, and with the 1882 article on Bauer. It takes note that revolutionary emancipation inherits, according to Löwith and Blumenberg, the function of religion, and that Engels, like Nietzsche, took the existence of Jesus for granted.
