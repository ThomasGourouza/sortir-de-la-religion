The sociology of religion studies religion as a social fact: what it does to groups, what groups make of it, and how it changes along with societies. Born with sociology itself, it supplies the essay with several of its major authors and with the background of its eleventh part.

#### The founders

Comte, who coins the word sociology in 1839, makes religion the first state of the human mind and ends by founding one. Tocqueville observes in America a religion that supports democracy instead of fighting it. But the discipline truly takes shape with two contemporary works. Durkheim, in *The Elementary Forms of Religious Life* (1912), defines religion by the distinction between the sacred and the profane and by the Church that unites the faithful, and seeks in Australian totemism the simplest form of the phenomenon: religion is society representing itself, and its rites periodically remake the group. Weber, in *The Protestant Ethic and the Spirit of Capitalism* (1904-1905) and then in his studies of the religions of China, India and ancient Judaism, asks conversely what religious ideas do to the economy and to the conduct of life: Calvinist asceticism shaped the spirit of capitalism, and the disenchantment of the world is the work of the prophetic religions themselves. He constructs types, priest, prophet, magician, Church and sect, and a comparative sociology of the paths of salvation. Ernst Troeltsch extends the typology of Church, sect and mysticism; Marcel Mauss, Durkheim's nephew, studies sacrifice, prayer and the gift.

#### Secularization theory and its crisis

After 1945, the discipline develops mainly around one thesis: modernization, the differentiation of spheres, rationalization, urbanization, pluralism, weakens religion. Bryan Wilson in England, Thomas Luckmann in Germany and Peter Berger in the United States, in the line of Weber, give it its classic formulations: religion loses its social functions, becomes privatized, and pluralism undermines the plausibility of beliefs. As early as the 1980s, this theory is contested. In the United States, Rodney Stark and his colleagues propose a model of the religious market: religious vitality depends on supply, and competition between Churches, not monopoly, stimulates it; this is rational choice applied to religion. Berger himself recants in 1999 in the face of a world that has remained religious. José Casanova distinguishes three senses of secularization, of which only the first, differentiation, seems to him to hold; Grace Davie describes a Europe that believes without belonging, and then belongs without believing; Norris and Inglehart defend a corrected version of the thesis, based on existential security. In France, Hervieu-Léger analyses religious modernity through memory and lineage, and Jean-Paul Willaime the recompositions of Protestantism.

#### Methods and objects

The sociology of religions works through statistical surveys of practice and belief, monographs on communities, and comparative history. Its objects have widened: new religious movements, spiritualities without institutions, religion and politics, Islam in Europe, global Pentecostalism, civil religion in the sense of Robert Bellah, who takes up Rousseau's term for the United States.

#### What the essay assumes

The essay draws on Durkheim and Weber as the two founders of one and the same regime of critique, social function, and sets them in dialogue with James and Bergson. It assumes familiarity with secularization theory, of which the eleventh part examines the philosophers rather than the sociologists, with Stark's critique, which Norris and Inglehart refute, and with Berger's reversal. It treats secularization as a measurable fact in Norris and Inglehart, and the exit from religion as a philosophical question in its eleventh part.
