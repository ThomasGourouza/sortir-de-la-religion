Analytic philosophy of religion applies to religious beliefs the tools of logic, the theory of knowledge and the analysis of language, in the English-speaking tradition stemming from Frege, Russell and Wittgenstein. It provides the essay with the second half of its section on the right to believe.

#### From logical positivism to the revival

In the first half of the twentieth century the tradition is hostile or indifferent to religion. Russell, in *Why I Am Not a Christian* (1927), refutes the classical proofs and explains religion by fear; the image of the teapot in orbit, in a text of 1952, illustrates that the burden of proof lies on whoever makes the assertion. The logical positivism of the Vienna Circle and of Ayer holds theological statements to be meaningless, since they cannot be verified; the 1955 debate on falsification, between Flew, Hare and Mitchell, asks what could refute the assertion that God loves humankind. Wittgenstein, in his lectures on religious belief, and his heirs, Rush Rhees and then D. Z. Phillips, suggest that belief is a language game and a form of life, not a hypothesis, a so-called fideist reading, which its opponents hold against it. From the 1960s onwards the discipline is reborn with believing authors who defend the rationality of theism with full analytic rigour: Alvin Plantinga, Richard Swinburne, William Alston, Nicholas Wolterstorff.

#### The problem of evil

The problem of evil is the most closely followed debate. J. L. Mackie, in 1955, argues that the existence of an omnipotent and perfectly good God is logically incompatible with the existence of evil. Plantinga replies in 1974 with the free will defence: it is possible that a world containing free creatures and evil is better than a world without freedom, which suffices to remove the logical contradiction. The debate then shifts to the probabilistic or evidential problem, raised by William Rowe in 1979: do the quantity and gratuitousness of suffering make the existence of God improbable? Theists respond with theodicies, John Hick's on soul-making, or with sceptical theism, which denies that we can judge God's reasons. Mackie, in *The Miracle of Theism* (1982), examines all the arguments and concludes in favour of atheism; Swinburne, through Bayesian reasoning, concludes that theism is probable.

#### Evidentialism and Reformed epistemology

The second debate concerns the right to believe. Evidentialism, to which Clifford gave its strictest formulation in 1877, requires proportionate evidence for every belief. Plantinga's Reformed epistemology, from 1983 onwards, maintains that belief in God can be properly basic, rational without being inferred, in the manner of belief in other minds or in the past, and that it is warranted if it is produced by a reliable faculty, the sense of the divine of which Calvin spoke. The Great Pumpkin objection and the question of religious disagreement follow from it. Other fields, the divine attributes, miracles, W. L. Craig's kalam cosmological argument, the fine-tuning of the constants of the universe, religious experience as perception in Alston, make up an active discipline, above all in the United States.

#### What the essay assumes

The essay draws on Russell and the teapot, Mackie and Plantinga on evil, and Reformed epistemology to conclude that this school saves the coherence and rationality of faith at the price of its universality, and that there still remains Ivan Karamazov. It assumes familiarity with evidentialism as a position, the difference between the logical and the evidential problem of evil, and the fact that these authors reason about propositions, which the section on Foucault connects with the Cartesian moment.
