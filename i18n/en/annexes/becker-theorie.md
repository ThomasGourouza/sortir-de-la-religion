Ernest Becker (1924-1974), an American cultural anthropologist, an unsettled teacher driven out of several universities for his independence, who died of cancer at forty-nine, two months before receiving the Pulitzer Prize for *The Denial of Death*, proposed a synthesis of psychoanalysis, Kierkegaard and anthropology in which the fear of death is the hidden mainspring of culture and religion. The essay reads him by paraphrase, not having consulted the original text.

#### The denial of death

*The Denial of Death* (1973) starts from a paradox: man is an animal that knows it will die, and this awareness, unbearable, must be repressed for life to be possible. Becker borrows from Otto Rank, Freud's dissident disciple, the idea that the fundamental anxiety is not sexual but existential, and from Kierkegaard the description of man as a synthesis of angel and worm, a spirit shut up in a body that rots. The repression of death works through the vital lie of character, an armour of defences that gives each person a sense of his own worth, and through the causa sui project, the effort to be one's own cause, to escape contingency and make oneself immortal. Culture supplies the means of this project: it is a heroic system, a set of roles, rites and narratives that allows each person to feel important, to take part in something greater than himself and to last symbolically beyond his death. Religion is the vastest of these systems, and Becker, a reader of Kierkegaard and Tillich, holds it to be the most honest answer, since it faces the creature in its finitude; psychoanalysis itself, romantic love and science are substitute heroic systems, often less lucid.

#### The escape from evil

*Escape from Evil* (1975), completed on his deathbed, draws the social consequences: heroism needs enemies, and evil in history comes less from wickedness than from the will to triumph over death by inflicting it on others; sacrifice, war, the scapegoat and nationalism are the forms through which human beings buy their symbolic immortality at the price of victims. Here Becker joins Rank, Norman O. Brown and, without citing him, Girard.

#### The experimental legacy

From 1986, three social psychologists, Greenberg, Solomon and Pyszczynski, drew from Becker terror management theory: cultural worldviews and self-esteem serve as a buffer against death anxiety, and reminders of death should strengthen attachment to one's own worldview and hostility to others. Hundreds of experiments have supported it; preregistered replications, among them Many Labs 4 in 2022, have not found the basic effect, and the theory is now disputed.

#### What the essay assumes

The essay assumes familiarity with the denial of death, the causa sui project, the heroic system, Rank and Kierkegaard, *Escape from Evil* and the experimental fate of the theory. It retains the idea that refoundations are substitute heroic systems, reproaches Becker for confusing the need for meaning, which is universal, with the need for immortality, and notes that the spiritualities of exercise, which accept death without a system, put his theory to the test.
