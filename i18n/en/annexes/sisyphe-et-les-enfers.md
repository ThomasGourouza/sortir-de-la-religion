The Greeks pictured the abode of the dead as an underground world ruled by Hades, where most souls lead an existence of shades without strength or memory. A few great culprits undergo eternal punishments there, of whom Sisyphus has become, through Camus, the most famous figure.

#### The kingdom of Hades

Hades, brother of Zeus, received the underworld as his share, and he rules it with Persephone. To reach it, the dead cross the Acheron or the Styx in the boat of Charon, who was paid with an obol placed in the mouth of the deceased; the three-headed dog Cerberus guards its entrance. In the *Odyssey*, in Book XI, Odysseus calls up the dead on the shore of Ocean: they are shades who must drink the blood of victims to recover speech for a moment, and the shade of Achilles tells him that he would rather be the servant of a poor man on earth than reign over all the dead. Below lies Tartarus, prison of the defeated Titans. Later there develops the idea of a judgement of the dead by Minos, Aeacus and Rhadamanthus, and of a happy abode, the Elysian Fields or the Isles of the Blessed, reserved for heroes and the just, then for initiates of the mysteries.

#### The great punished

Sisyphus, the cunning king of Corinth, deceived the gods and, according to some versions, chained up Death himself, or obtained leave to go back up to earth and refused to return; he is condemned to push a rock eternally to the top of a slope from which it endlessly rolls back down. Tantalus, who had served the gods the flesh of his son to test their perspicacity, stands in water up to his chin beneath branches laden with fruit, without ever being able to drink or eat. The Danaids, who had killed their husbands on their wedding night, endlessly fill leaking jars. Ixion, who had tried to seduce Hera, turns bound to a flaming wheel, and Tityos has his liver devoured by vultures.

#### The philosophical readings

Lucretius, in Book III of his poem, maintains that these torments do not exist after death but in this life: Tantalus is the man who dreads the gods, Sisyphus the ambitious man who endlessly seeks power, the Danaids those whom nothing satisfies. The myth becomes the description of the passions from which philosophy delivers. Camus, in 1942, makes Sisyphus the hero of the absurd: the human condition is a task without end and without hope, but the man who looks it in the face and takes it up again, at the moment when he goes back down towards his rock, is superior to his fate, and one must imagine him happy.

#### What the essay assumes

The essay assumes familiarity with the punishment of Sisyphus and with the conclusion of *The Myth of Sisyphus*, which it quotes, and in which it reads a spirituality without consolation, as well as with the fear of punishment after death, which the *Letter to Menoeceus* and Lucretius's poem seek to dispel at the same time as the fear of the gods.
