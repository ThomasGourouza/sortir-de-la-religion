How did a Jewish sect from Galilee become, in three centuries, the religion of the Empire, and then of Europe? The question is as old as critical history, and the answers have followed one another without cancelling each other out: each singles out one factor, doctrine, organization, society, power, and the debate concerns their respective weight.

#### Gibbon and the secondary causes

In chapter XV of his *History of the Decline and Fall of the Roman Empire* (1776), Gibbon sets aside the first cause, providence, and retains only the secondary causes. He counts five: the uncompromising zeal inherited from Judaism, which refused any compromise with other cults; the doctrine of a future life, promised with a precision that philosophy did not offer; the miracles ascribed to the primitive Church; the austere morals of the first Christians; and finally the discipline and government of a community that formed, within the Empire, an independent state. His list has remained the starting point.

#### Harnack and Nock: networks and conversion

At the beginning of the twentieth century, Harnack reconstructs the geography of the mission: Christianity follows the routes of the Jewish diaspora, whose synagogues and pagan sympathizers serve it as a bridge, and it settles in the cities before reaching the countryside. He stresses care for the poor, widows and the sick, monotheism, simpler than polytheism for a world already worked on by philosophy, and universalism, which addressed everyone regardless of origin. Nock, in 1933, adds a decisive observation: the ancient cults were cumulative, one could honour Isis without leaving Jupiter; only the philosophical schools and the religions stemming from Judaism demanded exclusive adherence, a conversion, that is, a change of life. Christianity offered what philosophy offered the educated, but to everyone.

#### Sociology: Stark and networks

Rodney Stark, a sociologist of religion, proposed in 1996 an explanation through the ordinary mechanisms of religious movements. A growth of forty per cent per decade is enough to go from a thousand believers in 40 to six million around 300. Conversions happen through networks of kinship and friendship, not through mass preaching. The Christians, by nursing their sick during the epidemics of 165 and 251, had a higher survival rate and won grateful survivors. Women, more numerous in the communities, enjoyed a better status there and married pagans whom they converted; the rejection of infanticide and abortion favoured demographic growth. Martyrdom, finally, was a signal of sincerity that made the doctrine credible. Stark disputes that Christianity was the religion of slaves: its recruits came mainly from the urban middle classes.

#### Power: Veyne and MacMullen

Other historians shift the emphasis towards the end of the process. For Paul Veyne, Christianity in 312 was a minority of one tenth, and it was the conversion of Constantine, the personal decision of an emperor attracted by an ambitious and organized religion, that tipped the Empire; without it, nothing guaranteed victory. Ramsay MacMullen stresses the part played by miracle in popular conversions, and then, after 312, that of constraint, favours and prohibitions. Peter Brown describes a world of Late Antiquity in which bishops become the patrons of cities and Christianity provides a common culture for an Empire in transformation. The old thesis of an age of anxiety, which supposedly made people eager for salvation, is now disputed: the paganism of the 3rd century was alive, and the Christians did not fill a void.

#### What the essay assumes

The essay reports three old readings of this spread: Celsus mocking the audience of ignorant people and the religion of slaves, Engels seeing in it a movement of the oppressed comparable to the labour movement, Nietzsche making Paul the inventor of Christianity. It does not decide between the historians, but its ninth part, with Norris and Inglehart, assumes that belief recedes with existential security; Antiquity, a world of insecurity, is its implicit counter-example. Stark appears in it as a theorist of the religious market, which is the other side of his sociology.
