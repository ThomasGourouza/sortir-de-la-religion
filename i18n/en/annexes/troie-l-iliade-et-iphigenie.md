The Trojan War is the great legendary cycle of the Greeks: ten years of siege of a city of Asia Minor by a coalition of Achaean kings, to take back Helen, the wife of Menelaus abducted by the Trojan prince Paris. The *Iliad* recounts only a few weeks of it, but the poems of the cycle, the tragedians and the mythographers fixed all its episodes, from the quarrel of the goddesses to the fall of the city.

#### The origins

At the wedding of Peleus and Thetis, Discord throws down a golden apple meant for the most beautiful. Hera, Athena and Aphrodite contend for it; the Trojan shepherd Paris, chosen as judge, awards it to Aphrodite, who promises him the love of the most beautiful of women. He carries off Helen from Sparta, and the Greek princes, bound by an oath, gather under the command of Agamemnon, brother of Menelaus.

#### The sacrifice of Iphigenia

At Aulis, the Greek fleet is held back by contrary winds. The seer Calchas reveals that Artemis, offended by Agamemnon, demands the sacrifice of his daughter Iphigenia. Agamemnon sends for the girl on the pretext of marrying her to Achilles, and leads her to the altar. Depending on the versions, her throat is cut, or the goddess substitutes a hind for her at the last moment and carries her off to Tauris. The sacrifice of Iphigenia is the first cause of Clytemnestra's hatred and of the murder of Agamemnon on his return. Euripides drew from it *Iphigenia at Aulis*, and the scene has often been compared to the sacrifice of Isaac.

#### The Iliad

The poem begins with the wrath of Achilles, the best of the Achaeans, from whom Agamemnon has taken his captive Briseis. Achilles withdraws from the fighting, and the Greeks are routed by Hector, son of Priam. When Hector kills Patroclus, Achilles' friend, Achilles returns, kills Hector and drags his corpse around Patroclus's tomb. In the last book, the old king Priam, guided at night by Hermes, comes to Achilles' tent to beg that the body of his son be given back to him; the two enemies weep together, each for his own dead, and Achilles gives back the body. The gods are present everywhere, but partial, and guarantee no justice.

#### The fall of Troy

After the death of Hector and then that of Achilles, killed by an arrow of Paris guided by Apollo, the Greeks take the city by the ruse of the wooden horse, sack it and burn it. The *Odyssey* and the tragedies then tell of the difficult and bloody returns of the victors.

#### What the essay assumes

The essay assumes familiarity with the sacrifice of Iphigenia, which Lucretius recounts as the example of the crimes engendered by religion, and with the scene of Priam and Achilles in the last book of the *Iliad*, which the box in the Weil section quotes after *L'Iliade ou le poème de la force*, and in which it sees the refusal to give a meaning to misfortune.
