The Acts of the Apostles is the only book of the New Testament that narrates what follows the death of Jesus: the birth of a community in Jerusalem, then the spread of the message as far as Rome. The second volume of the Gospel of Luke, it was composed c. 80 to 90 by an anonymous author whom tradition identifies with the physician who was Paul's companion.

#### Jerusalem: the Spirit and the community

The narrative opens with the Ascension: forty days after Easter, the risen Christ is taken up into heaven and promises the Spirit (Ac 1). At Pentecost, a Jewish feast celebrated fifty days after Passover, the Spirit descends on the disciples in the form of tongues of fire; pilgrims who have come from the whole diaspora each hear them in their own language (Ac 2). Peter preaches and baptizes. Luke then describes the Jerusalem community in idealized tableaux: prayer, the breaking of bread, and believers who sell their goods and lay the proceeds at the apostles' feet, so that no one is in need (Ac 2 and 4). This community of goods has its reverse side: Ananias and Sapphira, who keep back part of the price of their field while claiming to give everything, fall dead (Ac 5). Seven men, among them Stephen and Philip, are chosen to serve the Greek-speaking widows; Stephen, after a speech that rereads the history of Israel as a series of refusals, is stoned before the eyes of a young man named Saul (Ac 6 and 7).

#### Saul, Cornelius and the assembly of Jerusalem

The conversion of Saul on the road to Damascus is told three times, by the narrator and then by Paul himself (Ac 9, 22 and 26): a light, a voice, three days of blindness, baptism. Peter, warned by a vision, baptizes the centurion Cornelius, the first pagan received without circumcision (Ac 10). The question of the pagans is settled at the assembly that tradition calls the Council of Jerusalem, c. 48 or 49: Peter, James and Paul agree not to impose circumcision on converts from the nations, asking them instead to abstain from meat sacrificed to idols, from blood and from sexual immorality (Ac 15).

#### Paul's journeys and the road to Rome

The second half follows Paul: Asia Minor, Philippi, Athens, where he delivers before the Areopagus a speech on the unknown god (Ac 17), Corinth, Ephesus. Back in Jerusalem, he is arrested in the Temple and held for two years at Caesarea. As a Roman citizen, he appeals to Caesar; the crossing ends in a shipwreck on Malta. The book closes on Paul preaching in Rome; his death is not narrated.

#### History and apology

Historians compare Acts with Paul's letters, which are older and direct. The agreements are many, such as the proconsul Gallio, whom an inscription at Delphi dates to c. 51, but so are the discrepancies: in the letter to the Galatians, Paul insists on his independence from Jerusalem, where Luke shows him compliant; the conflict at Antioch between Peter and Paul is passed over in silence; the speeches are the author's compositions, after the custom of ancient historians. Luke writes an apologetic history: a unanimous Church, led by the Spirit, harmless to Rome, whose magistrates always release the Christians.

#### Readings

Engels, in 1894, rereads the first communities as a movement of the oppressed comparable to the workers' movement; the community of goods in Ac 2 and 4 nourished, from Thomas More to the Christian socialisms of the nineteenth century, the idea of an original communism, which historians reduce to a local and short-lived practice. The Athens speech served as a model for the apologists of the second century, Justin first among them, who present the faith in the language of the philosophers.

#### What the essay assumes

The essay reads Paul through his letters and treats Acts, written a generation later, as a narrative to be handled with caution; it draws on the Areopagus speech, which it takes to be Luke's composition, as the scene in which Athens laughs at the resurrection, and on Engels's formula about early Christianity. It takes as known the setting of Paul's mission, and martyrdom as the refusal of a cult, of which Stephen is the first case.
