Between the great collections of the Law, the Prophets and the Psalms, the Bible contains short books, often narrative, which have had a considerable fortune in liturgy, art and literature. Most date from the Persian or Hellenistic period and raise, each in its own way, the question of the faithfulness of a scattered people.

#### Ruth

In the time of the Judges, a famine drives a family from Bethlehem into Moab. The men die; Naomi returns, and her Moabite daughter-in-law, Ruth, refuses to leave her and adopts her people and her God. She gleans in the field of Boaz, a kinsman of her husband, who marries her according to the custom of redemption. Their son is the grandfather of David. The book, dated by many to the Persian period, makes a foreign woman the ancestor of the king, at a time when other texts forbid mixed marriages. It is read at the Jewish feast of Weeks.

#### Jonah

God sends Jonah to preach against Nineveh, the Assyrian capital; the prophet flees by ship, is thrown into the sea during the storm, swallowed by a great fish and cast up on the shore three days later. He preaches at last, Nineveh repents, and God forgives, to the great vexation of Jonah, to whom he teaches the lesson of a castor-oil plant that withers. A story with a didactic aim rather than a chronicle, probably from the Persian or Hellenistic period, it opens mercy to the nations. The gospels make the sign of Jonah a figure of Jesus's three days in the tomb.

#### Esther

At the court of the Persian king, a young Jewish woman, Esther, who has become queen without revealing her origin, foils with her uncle Mordecai the plan of the minister Haman to exterminate the Jews of the empire; Haman is hanged on the gallows he had set up, and the Jews take revenge on their enemies. The Hebrew text never names God; the Greek version adds prayers. The book founds the feast of Purim, and critical scholarship sees in it a diaspora story from the Persian or Hellenistic period, without historical support.

#### Tobit and Judith

These two books, absent from the Hebrew Bible, are deuterocanonical. Tobit tells of a pious Jew of the deportation, struck blind, whose son travels in the company of the angel Raphael, heals his father and marries Sarah, delivered from a demon: a tale of family piety and almsgiving. Judith, a widow of Bethulia besieged by Holofernes, general of Nebuchadnezzar, goes to his camp, seduces him and cuts off his head; the town is saved. The anachronism, Nebuchadnezzar as king of Assyria, signals an edifying story from the time of the Maccabees. Judith and Holofernes have become a major subject of painting, from Caravaggio to Artemisia Gentileschi.

#### Lamentations, Ezra and Nehemiah

Lamentations are five poems of mourning over Jerusalem destroyed in 587, four of which are acrostics; tradition attributes them to Jeremiah, critical scholarship to anonymous authors of the exile. Ezra and Nehemiah recount the return under the Persian kings, the rebuilding of the Temple and then of the walls, the public reading of the Law by Ezra and the sending away of foreign wives. Ezra, priest and scribe, became in Jewish tradition the second Moses, the one who restored the Torah; Spinoza, following Ibn Ezra, would later make him the probable compiler of the Pentateuch. Chronicles retells the history from Adam to the exile, centred on David and the Temple.

#### What the essay assumes

The essay cites none of these books. They nevertheless shed light on two of its threads: Part One, which asks what becomes of the Law when a philosopher reads it, and the section on Maimonides, which distinguishes the apparent sense of Scripture, for the multitude, from the sense reserved for the sage; Ezra, restorer of the Torah according to tradition and compiler of the Pentateuch according to Spinoza, stands at the meeting point of the two. Above all, these books show how the Bible tells the survival of a people without king or land, a question that precedes that of the exit from religion.
