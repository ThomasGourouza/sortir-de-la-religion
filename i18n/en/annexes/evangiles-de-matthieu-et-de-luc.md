Matthew and Luke, written a generation after Mark, take over the substance of his narrative and enrich it with sayings of Jesus, infancy narratives and parables that have become the best-known pages of the Gospels. They give two distinct portraits of him, one turned towards Israel, the other towards the Greek world.

#### Two books built on Mark

Historians date both Gospels to the years 80 to 90. They are anonymous: the attribution to Matthew, the tax collector who became an apostle, and to Luke, the companion of Paul, comes from the tradition of the second century. According to the two-source theory, each used Mark, keeping his order and most of his text, and a collection of sayings now lost, which exegetes call Q, from the German Quelle, the source of the Beatitudes, the Lord's Prayer and the love of enemies. Each adds material of his own.

#### Matthew, the Gospel of Israel

Matthew writes for Christians of Jewish origin, probably in Syria, after the break with the synagogue. He opens with a genealogy going back to Abraham, tells of the visit of the magi, the massacre of the children of Bethlehem and the flight into Egypt, and shows at every stage that the Scriptures are being fulfilled. He groups the teaching of Jesus into five great discourses, like the five books of the Law. The first is the Sermon on the Mount (Mt 5 to 7): the Beatitudes, which call blessed the poor in spirit, the meek, the merciful, the peacemakers, the persecuted; a rereading of the Law that radicalizes its demands; the love of enemies; prayer in secret, with the Lord's Prayer. The last discourse culminates in the judgement scene (Mt 25), in which the Son of Man separates the nations according to what they did to the least. The book ends with the sending of the disciples to all nations.

#### Luke, the Gospel of the poor and of the nations

Luke, a cultivated Greek writer, opens with a historian's preface. His infancy narrative centres on Mary: the annunciation, the Magnificat, in which God casts down the mighty and lifts up the lowly, the birth in a manger, the shepherds. Luke alone reports the Good Samaritan, who makes a despised foreigner the model of the neighbour, the prodigal son welcomed by his father, Lazarus and the rich man, the good thief. The poor, women and sinners hold a place there that they do not have in the other Gospels. Luke is also the author of the Acts of the Apostles, the second volume of a single work, which carries the proclamation from Jerusalem to Rome.

#### The temptations in the wilderness

Matthew and Luke develop into three scenes the temptation that Mark mentions in a single sentence (Mt 4, 1-11 ; Lc 4, 1-13). After forty days of fasting, the devil proposes that Jesus turn the stones into bread, throw himself down from the top of the Temple so that the angels will bear him up, and receive all the kingdoms of the world in exchange for a prostration. Each time Jesus answers with a verse from Deuteronomy and refuses bread, wonder and power.

#### Composition and reading

The infancy narratives do not agree, with two genealogies, magi on one side and shepherds on the other: criticism sees in them theological compositions, which say who Jesus is rather than what happened. The sayings common to the two Gospels, on the contrary, are held to be one of the oldest points of access to the preaching of Jesus.

#### What the essay assumes

Dostoevsky's Grand Inquisitor, whom the essay regards as the centre of gravity of its question, comments on the three temptations: the cardinal reproaches Christ for having refused bread, miracle and power in the name of freedom; the essay illustrates this with Duccio's panel of the temptation on the mountain. It takes for granted, in Kierkegaard, the gap between the demanding Christianity of the New Testament and the Christendom that abolished it by flourishing; in Engels, the reading of early Christianity as a movement of slaves and the poor, for which Luke's Beatitudes, which call the poor blessed without further qualification, are the usual support; in Simone Weil, who commented on the Lord's Prayer in *Waiting for God*, a prayer made while thinking that God does not exist.
