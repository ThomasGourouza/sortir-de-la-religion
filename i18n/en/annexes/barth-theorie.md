Karl Barth (1886-1968), a Swiss from Basel, a student of Harnack and Herrmann, pastor in Safenwil from 1911 to 1921, professor in Göttingen, Münster and Bonn, driven out of Germany in 1935 and professor in Basel until 1962, is the most important Protestant theologian of the twentieth century. He broke with the liberal theology of the nineteenth century and put revelation back at the centre, in a work of thirteen volumes that remained unfinished.

#### The break and the Römerbrief

The war of 1914 and the manifesto of the German intellectuals, signed by his teachers, convince Barth that the theology which grounded faith in experience, culture and history had handed God over to man. His commentary on the Epistle to the Romans, the *Römerbrief*, whose second edition of 1922 is entirely rewritten, has, in Karl Adam's phrase, the effect of a bomb in the world of theologians: God is God, man is man, and between them lies Kierkegaard's infinite qualitative difference; revelation comes vertically from above, as the tangent touches the circle, and judges every religion, every culture, every ethics, Christian ones included. With Gogarten, Thurneysen, Brunner and Bultmann, Barth leads dialectical theology in the 1920s, so called because it can speak of God only by affirming and denying, around the journal *Zwischen den Zeiten*; the group breaks up after 1933.

#### The Church Dogmatics

From 1932, Barth undertakes the *Kirchliche Dogmatik*, the *Church Dogmatics*, a systematic exposition of the faith on the basis of the Word of God alone, in four parts: the doctrine of the Word, of God, of creation and of reconciliation, the part on redemption never having been written. § 17 of volume I/2 (1938) defines religion as unbelief, the concern of godless man, man's effort to take hold of God, and only revelation can raise religion up. Barth rejects the Catholic analogia entis and all natural theology, there being no knowledge of God outside Christ, which earns him a controversy with Brunner, to whom he replies in 1934 with a resounding No!, and with Przywara. His doctrine of election, Christocentric, makes Christ the elect and the reprobate for all, and leans towards a universal hope. Anthropology, ethics and creation are thought in it on the basis of the covenant. Barth drafts most of the Barmen Declaration (1934), the foundation of the Confessing Church, refuses the oath to Hitler and after 1945 takes positions against rearmament and the Cold War.

#### Legacy and critiques

Barth trained generations of pastors, influenced Bonhoeffer, who reproached him with a positivism of revelation, and the Catholicism of Balthasar and of Küng, and drew the critiques of Tillich, for whom he neglected culture, of Bultmann, and of those who, like Glasse, object that a theology intelligible only from inside faith cannot answer Feuerbach, whom Barth had nevertheless declared right all along the line in an essay of 1927.

#### What the essay assumes

The essay assumes familiarity with the *Römerbrief*, the infinite qualitative difference, § 17 on religion as unbelief, the 1927 lecture on Feuerbach, an attested response, and the refusal of natural theology. It sees in Barth the exact inverse of Stirner, the pious man is godless, retains his critique of the appropriation of transcendence, and leaves him within revelation, without making him an exception.
