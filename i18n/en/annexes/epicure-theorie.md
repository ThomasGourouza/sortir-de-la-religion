Epicurus (341-270 BCE), born on Samos, founded the Garden in Athens in 306, a school open to women and slaves, where people lived as friends according to a doctrine that makes philosophy a therapy of the soul. Of his three hundred scrolls only three letters, two collections of maxims and some fragments remain, and Lucretius's poem is the fullest exposition of his doctrine.

#### Canonic and physics

The canonic is the theory of the criterion of truth: sensations are always true, errors come from judgement; to them are added the preconceptions, concepts formed by the repetition of sensations, and the feelings of pleasure and pain. The physics takes up the atomism of Democritus: the universe is made of atoms, indivisible, eternal bodies, infinite in number, and of the void, through which they fall; everything else, worlds, souls, gods, is composed of atoms and dissolves. Epicurus adds to Democritus the swerve, Lucretius's clinamen: a tiny, uncaused deviation of the atoms in their fall, which explains their encounters and grounds freedom against fate. Celestial and meteorological phenomena admit several explanations, all acceptable provided they exclude divine intervention. The soul is bodily and mortal; death is nothing to us, for while we are, it is not, and when it is, we are no longer.

#### The gods and religion

Epicurus is not an atheist. The gods exist, as common evidence attests; they live in the intermundia, blessed and incorruptible, indifferent to human affairs, concern for which would disturb their bliss. The impious man is not the one who denies the gods of the crowd, but the one who attributes to the gods the opinions of the crowd: anger, favour, providence. Popular religion, with its fears of punishment and its hopes of reward, is the main source of disturbance, and knowledge of nature frees us from it. The gods nonetheless remain models of the happy life, whom the sage contemplates and honours.

#### The ethics of pleasure

Pleasure is the beginning and the end of the happy life, but it is defined negatively: the absence of pain in the body, aponia, and of disturbance in the soul, ataraxia. Epicurus classifies desires as natural and necessary, natural and unnecessary, neither natural nor necessary, and prescribes satisfying the first, moderating the second and fleeing the third. Prudence is worth more than philosophy itself, friendship is the greatest of goods, and the sage lives hidden, away from politics. The *Letter to Menoeceus* sums up the whole, and later Epicureans condensed it into the fourfold remedy, tetrapharmakos: the gods are not to be feared, death is not to be dreaded, the good is easy to obtain, evil easy to endure. The school lasted until the fourth century CE, opposed by the Stoics and the Christians, and was reborn in the seventeenth century with Gassendi.

#### What the essay assumes

The essay assumes familiarity with atomism, the mortality of the soul, the doctrine of the gods of the intermundia and the therapeutic meaning of the knowledge of nature. It places Epicurus in the regime of error while stressing his piety and his spiritual dimension, ataraxia as an exercise, and judges that Onfray inherits only half of him. Hume discusses Lucretius, and the trilemma of evil attributed to Epicurus returns in the *Dialogues*.
