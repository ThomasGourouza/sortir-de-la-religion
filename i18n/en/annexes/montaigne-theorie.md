Michel Eyquem de Montaigne (1533-1592), a magistrate in Bordeaux, then withdrawn into his library from 1571, mayor of Bordeaux from 1581 to 1585, a mediator between Henry of Navarre and Henry III during the Wars of Religion, wrote a single book, the *Essays*, published in 1580, enlarged in 1588 and after his death in 1595, and endlessly rewritten in the margins. A practising Catholic, he is the first to make himself the matter of his book.

#### The book

The *Essays* are not a treatise: a hundred and seven chapters of unequal length, on subjects drawn from his reading of Plutarch, Seneca and the historians, *Of Cannibals*, *Of Friendship*, devoted to La Boétie, *Of Coaches*, *Of Vanity*, *Of Repentance*, *Of Experience*, in which thought advances by digressions, quotations and returns upon itself. To essay is to weigh, to put one's judgement to the test on everything, and first on oneself, who changes at every instant: Montaigne does not portray being but passing. The book forms a single body with its author, consubstantial, and its sincerity, which does not exclude art, has made it the model of writing about the self.

#### Scepticism and faith

The *Apology for Raymond Sebond*, the longest chapter (II, 12), starts from the defence of a fifteenth-century Catalan theologian who claimed to demonstrate the faith by reason, and turns it into a demolition of reason itself: man is not superior to the animals, the senses deceive, the philosophers contradict one another, science is opinion, and we hold our religion only by birth and country, Christians in the same way as we are Perigordians or Germans. Montaigne takes up the tropes of the Pyrrhonism of Sextus Empiricus, which he has engraved in his library, and his motto, Que sçay-je ?, in the form of a question. This scepticism is not unbelief: it serves to humble reason so that faith may be received from God alone, a position called fideism and held by several Catholics of the time. But its effect is to make custom the sole explanation of belief, and Pascal, Bayle and Hume will each draw different consequences from it.

#### Custom, experience, wisdom

Custom is for Montaigne the queen of the world: it makes laws, morals and religions, and the wise man conforms to it outwardly while keeping his judgement free inwardly, in a back shop all our own. Faced with the civil wars, he condemns cruelty, torture, witch trials, and doubts that a man should be burned for a conjecture. The last chapter, *Of Experience*, proposes a wisdom of acceptance of the body, of pleasure and of the human condition: nothing is so fine as to play the man well and properly, and the greatest thing in the world is to know how to belong to oneself. This wisdom does without salvation and the beyond, without denying them.

#### What the essay assumes

The essay assumes familiarity with the structure of the *Apology*, the motto, the formulas on custom and on faith received by birth, the friendship with La Boétie, and the chapter *Of Experience*, which it reads as a spirituality without salvation. It holds Montaigne to be a sincere fideist and not a masked atheist, notes the unintended effect of his scepticism, Hume before Hume, and reports Pascal's attested response in the *Entretien avec Sacy* and the *Pensées*.
