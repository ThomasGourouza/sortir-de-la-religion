Immanuel Kant (1724-1804), who was born and died in Königsberg, which he never left, professor at its university, brought up in Lutheran Pietism, published after the age of fifty-seven the three Critiques that displaced the whole of philosophy: the question is no longer what the world is, but what the mind can know, must do and may hope.

#### Theoretical reason

The *Critique of Pure Reason* (1781, revised in 1787) asks how synthetic a priori judgements are possible. The answer is the Copernican revolution: it is not knowledge that conforms to objects, but objects that conform to the forms of our knowledge. Space and time are the a priori forms of sensibility, the categories, among them causality, the a priori concepts of the understanding; knowledge results from their application to the data of the senses, and bears only on phenomena, never on things in themselves. Reason, which wants to go beyond, goes astray: the Transcendental Dialectic shows that the soul, the world and God, the objects of traditional metaphysics, cannot be known, and refutes the three proofs of the existence of God. God remains a regulative idea, and knowledge is limited in order to make room for faith.

#### Practical reason

The *Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals* (1785) and the *Critique of Practical Reason* (1788) ground morality on reason alone: an action is moral only if it is performed from duty, according to a maxim one can will to be raised into a universal law, the categorical imperative, another formula of which commands us to treat humanity always also as an end and never merely as a means. The moral law, a fact of reason, reveals freedom, and the will that gives itself its own law is autonomous; every morality grounded on God, happiness or feeling is heteronomous. Practical reason nevertheless postulates what theoretical reason could not know: freedom, the immortality of the soul and the existence of God, necessary so that virtue and happiness may be brought into accord in the highest good. Morality thus leads to religion. The *Critique of the Power of Judgement* (1790) deals with the beautiful, the sublime and the purposiveness of nature.

#### Religion and the Enlightenment

*Religion within the Boundaries of Mere Reason* (1793) defines religion as the recognition of our duties as divine commands, examines radical evil, the propensity of the will to subordinate the law to self-love, makes Christ the ideal of a humanity pleasing to God, whose historicity matters little, and contrasts the invisible Church, an ethical community, with the visible Churches; its fourth part denounces false worship, the fetishism of rites and Pfaffenthum, priestcraft, which earns Kant a reprimand from the King of Prussia. The short essay *What is Enlightenment?* (1784) defines Aufklärung as man's emergence from his immaturity, under the motto Sapere aude, and *Toward Perpetual Peace* (1795) proposes a federation of republics.

#### What the essay assumes

The essay takes as known the distinction between phenomenon and thing in itself, the refutation of the proofs, the categorical imperative, autonomy, the postulates and the content of the *Religion* of 1793. It holds that Kant dismantles Descartes's God and reinstalls him through morality, and reports the objections of Schleiermacher, of Hegel and of Nietzsche, for whom Kant sacralized morality, before noting that Habermas makes him the first example of a secularizing translation.
