Plato (428/427-348/347 BCE), a disciple of Socrates and the founder of the Academy, wrote some thirty dialogues in which almost all later philosophy found its questions. The essay is concerned above all with the *Laws*, the *Republic* and the *Euthyphro*; here is the framework within which these texts take their place.

#### The Forms and the intelligible world

Socrates asked what courage, piety and justice are, without being content with examples. Plato answers that these definitions aim at realities: the Ideas or Forms, the Beautiful itself, the Just itself, the Equal itself, unchanging, intelligible, distinct from the sensible things that participate in them and bear their name. The sensible world, changing and imperfect, is the image of an intelligible world that the soul contemplated before birth and that it recollects: to learn is to remember. At the summit of the Forms stands the Good, which the *Republic* compares to the sun: it gives the Forms their being and the soul its capacity to know them, and it is itself beyond essence. The allegory of the cave describes education as the liberation of a prisoner who turns round from the shadow towards the light. The *Parmenides* and the *Sophist*, late dialogues, subject the theory of Forms to objections that Plato does not entirely resolve, and the Neoplatonists will draw their system from them.

#### The soul

The soul is immortal, and Plato gives several proofs of this: in the *Phaedo* through the kinship of the soul with the Forms, in the *Phaedrus* and the *Laws* through the fact that it moves itself and that a principle of motion cannot perish. The *Republic* divides it into three parts, reason, spirit and appetite, whose harmony under the government of reason is justice. The *Phaedrus* compares it to a team of horses; the *Timaeus* situates it within the cosmos, the work of a demiurge who shaped the sensible world by looking at the intelligible model and endowed it with a world soul. The salvation of the soul passes through philosophy, which is an exercise in dying, a separation of soul from body, and through contemplation.

#### The city

The *Republic* builds the just city on the model of the soul: three classes, producers, guardians, philosophers, and the rule of philosopher-kings, the only ones who know the Good. Education is controlled there, the poets expelled for having attributed vices to the gods, and a purified theology taught. The *Laws*, the last dialogue, describe a second-best city, more achievable, governed by laws each of which is preceded by a persuasive preamble; Book X deals with impiety, refutes three forms of it, atheism, the denial of providence and the belief that the gods can be swayed, punishes them with imprisonment and, for repeat offenders, with death, and entrusts the guarding of the doctrine to the nocturnal council. In the *Euthyphro* Plato raises the question whether the pious is loved by the gods because it is pious, or pious because it is loved by the gods.

#### What the essay assumes

The essay reads Book X of the *Laws* as the pure case of its criterion and the ancestor of the Grand Inquisitor, and the *Republic* as the first name of spirituality, the conversion of the soul towards the Good; it sees between these two Platos a tension that is its own. It takes as known the theory of Forms, the hierarchy of the Good beyond essence, which Murdoch will take up, the proof from the self-moving soul and the place of the *Euthyphro* in the debate on morality without God. It sets Plato against Lucretius and Rousseau in constructed confrontations, and recalls Popper's reading.
