Pierre Hadot (1922-2010), ordained a priest in 1944 and having left the Church in 1952, a philologist, a historian of ancient philosophy and of Latin patristics, director of studies at the École pratique des hautes études and then professor at the Collège de France from 1982 to 1991, elected with Foucault's support, transformed the reading of ancient philosophies by showing that they were first of all ways of life, and gave contemporary spiritualities without God their historical point of reference.

#### From the philologist to the spiritual exercises

His early work concerns Marius Victorinus, Porphyry and Plotinus; *Plotinus or the Simplicity of Vision* (1963), a short book written for a general readership, presents Plotinus's experience as an inner transformation that can be described. The article Spiritual Exercises (1977), included in *Exercices spirituels et philosophie antique* (1981), formulates the thesis: in antiquity, philosophy was not a theoretical discourse but a way of life, a choice of life that discourse justifies and that exercises make effective: meditation, examination of conscience, premeditation of evils, attention to the present, the view from above on human affairs, learning to die, reading, dialogue. The term spiritual exercises comes from Ignatius of Loyola, but the thing comes from the philosophers, Christianity having presented itself as a philosophy before reversing the debt. In this light Hadot also reads Wittgenstein, on whom he had published the first French articles as early as 1959, and Goethe.

#### Ancient philosophy as a way of life

*What Is Ancient Philosophy?* (1995) develops the thesis as history: from Socrates to the Hellenistic schools, Epicureans, Stoics, Sceptics, Cynics, Platonists, each school offers a form of life, and theory, physics, logic, ethics, serves that form; in the Middle Ages, philosophy becomes the handmaid of theology and is reduced to discourse, and the moderns inherited this reduction, with some exceptions: Montaigne, Spinoza, Nietzsche, Bergson. *The Inner Citadel* (1992) reads the *Meditations* of Marcus Aurelius as written exercises, not as the confidences of a melancholy emperor, structured by the three disciplines of Epictetus's Stoicism: judgement, desire, action. *The Veil of Isis* (2004) follows the history of the idea of nature, between the Promethean attitude, which unveils through technology, and the Orphic attitude, which contemplates. *N'oublie pas de vivre* (2008) meditates on Goethe and the tradition of the exercises.

#### The debate with Foucault

Foucault read Hadot and made use of him in his last lectures on the care of the self. Hadot, in two texts written after Foucault's death, expressed both his esteem and his disagreement: the Foucauldian culture of the self, in which the subject constitutes itself as a work, seems to him too aesthetic, a new form of dandyism, whereas the ancient exercises aimed at going beyond oneself towards the Whole, universal reason, the cosmos, and not at cultivating a self. Hadot himself professes a spirituality of the oceanic feeling, of wonder before existence, without a personal God or a Church, close to Spinoza and to Marcus Aurelius.

#### What the essay assumes

The essay assumes familiarity with the thesis of the spiritual exercises, the debt reversed from Ignatius to the philosophers, the list of exercises, the attested disagreement with Foucault, the Whole as a horizon of lucidity and not as an object of adoration, and the 1963 reading of Plotinus. It places Hadot, together with Spinoza, among the exceptions, in the position of immanence, and holds his historical description to be the most exact account of the spirituality of exercise that the conclusion defends.
