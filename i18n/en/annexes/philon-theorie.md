Philo (c. 20 BCE-c. 45 CE), a Jew of Alexandria from a rich and influential family, is the principal witness to a Greek-speaking Judaism that read the Bible in the Septuagint translation and thought with Plato and the Stoics. In 39-40 he led the embassy of the Jews of Alexandria to Caligula, after the pogrom of 38, and left an account of it.

#### The work and the method

Some fifty treatises survive, almost all commentaries on the Pentateuch: the *Allegorical Commentary*, including the *Legum allegoriae*, which follows Genesis verse by verse; the *Exposition of the Law*, which runs from the *De opificio mundi*, on the creation, to the lives of the patriarchs and the particular laws; the *Questions on Genesis and Exodus*; and philosophical or historical treatises, including the *De vita contemplativa*, which describes the Therapeutae, a community of Jewish ascetics near Alexandria devoted to study and prayer. The method is allegorical exegesis, inherited from the Stoic interpreters of Homer and already practised by Alexandrian Jews: the text has a literal sense, which Philo does not reject and whose observance he maintains, and a deeper sense, in which the characters and events represent the powers of the soul, the virtues and the journey towards God. Abraham is the soul that learns, Isaac the soul that possesses virtue by nature, Jacob the soul that acquires it through exercise.

#### God, the Logos, the world

God is Being, he who is, according to Exodus read in Greek, transcendent, without name or quality, knowable only in his existence; his relation to the world passes through his powers, the creative and the royal, and above all through the Logos, the divine Reason, at once God's thought, the place of the Ideas, the model of the world, the instrument of creation and the mediator between God and humankind, which Philo calls firstborn, image of God and, once, second God. The *De opificio* reads the Genesis account with the *Timaeus*: God first conceived the intelligible world, then fashioned the sensible world in its image. Man created in the image of God is so through his intellect, the image of the Logos. Philo's ethics is that of an ascent of the soul, through virtue, contemplation and sober ecstasy, towards the vision of God.

#### Legacy

Rabbinic Judaism did not preserve Philo; it was the Christians, Clement of Alexandria, Origen, Eusebius, Ambrose, who copied, quoted and transmitted him, to the point that legend made him a Christian. His doctrine of the Logos supplied the prologue of John and the apologists with a vocabulary, and his allegory supplied the method of Alexandrian exegesis. Modern scholars have seen in him the first to attempt the synthesis of biblical revelation and Greek philosophy that Justin, Augustine, Maimonides and Averroes would each take up for their own tradition.

#### What the essay assumes

The essay assumes familiarity with the reading of Genesis with the *Timaeus*, the doctrine of the Logos as intelligible world and second God, the link with the prologue of John, the two-level allegory that foreshadows Averroes, the Therapeutae, and the fact that Philo was transmitted by Christians and not by rabbinic Judaism. It places him in a regime of allegory prior to any critique: Philo does not criticize religion, he translates it into philosophy, and refounds nothing.
