Between 1560 and 1690, Europe learns at its own cost that a society divided into confessions cannot be governed by a single truth. From this experience come the two ideas the essay follows in Montaigne, Spinoza and Bayle: that religion is not the necessary foundation of political order, and that conscience cannot be coerced.

#### France: eight wars and an edict

From 1562 to 1598, Catholics and Huguenots wage eight wars interrupted by precarious peaces. The St Bartholomew's Day massacre, in Paris on 24 August 1572 and then in the provinces, leaves several thousand dead; Montaigne, who writes the *Essays* during these years and will be mayor of Bordeaux (1581-1585), draws from it his distrust of any certainty that arms men. A party of jurists, the Politiques, among them Jean Bodin, maintains that the State must survive religious division. Henry IV, a Protestant converted to Catholicism (1593), promulgates the Edict of Nantes (1598): freedom of conscience for the Reformed, worship in designated places, places of safety. This is not toleration as a principle, but coexistence imposed by weariness.

#### The Empire and the United Provinces

The Thirty Years' War (1618-1648) devastates Germany; the treaties of Westphalia (1648) establish the parity of the confessions within the Empire and transfer Church property to the princes: this is where the legal use of the word secularization appears, which the confiscations of the Napoleonic era will generalize. In the Low Countries, the provinces in revolt against Spain proclaim as early as 1579 that no one shall be persecuted for his religion. Amsterdam welcomes the Jews expelled from the Iberian Peninsula, among them Spinoza's family, Protestant dissenters and printers from all over Europe. Toleration there has its limits: Spinoza is struck in 1656 by the *herem* of his community, his friend Koerbagh dies in prison in 1669, and the *Theological-Political Treatise*, published anonymously in 1670, is banned in 1674.

#### The Revocation and the Refuge

Louis XIV, absolute and Most Christian King, curtails the rights of the Reformed, then persecutes them: dragonnades, forced conversions, and the revocation of the Edict of Nantes by the Edict of Fontainebleau (1685). Two hundred thousand Huguenots flee to the United Provinces, England, Prussia and Switzerland. Pierre Bayle, a pastor's son, converted to Catholicism and then returned to Protestantism, is one of these exiles: a refugee in Rotterdam since 1681, he learns that his brother has died in a French prison. His *Philosophical Commentary* on the words of the Gospel, compel them to come in (1686), maintains that conscience, even when mistaken, must not be forced; the *Various Thoughts on the Comet* (1682-1683) had already argued that a society of atheists could subsist. His quarrel with Pierre Jurieu, a pastor of the Refuge who demands against Rome the intolerance of the righteous, shows that toleration divided the persecuted themselves.

#### Libertines, Locke and toleration

In Paris, between 1620 and 1650, the erudite libertines, Gabriel Naudé, La Mothe Le Vayer, Gassendi, readers of Montaigne and Charron, practise a discreet scepticism and treat religion as a matter of public order; clandestine manuscripts, among them the *Treatise of the Three Impostors*, circulate until the eighteenth century. In England, after the civil war and the revolution of 1688, Locke's *Letter Concerning Toleration* (1689) separates the ends of the State, which are civil, from those of the Church, which concern salvation; it still excludes Catholics and atheists. The Enlightenment will inherit these three justifications of freedom of conscience, sceptical, political and theological.

#### What the essay assumes

The essay names the Wars of Religion, Bayle's exile, the *herem* and Spinoza's Amsterdam, and the libertines who made Montaigne's *Apology* their breviary; it takes as known St Bartholomew's Day, the Edict of Nantes and its revocation, and the legal origin of the word secularization.
