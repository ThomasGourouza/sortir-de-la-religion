Received revelations, or channeling in the vocabulary of the New Age, are texts presented as dictated to a human intermediary by an outside entity: a dead person, a spirit, an ascended master, an angel, Christ or God himself. The intermediary claims to be a transcriber, not an author. The genre is old, but it took its modern form in the nineteenth century and has flourished since the 1970s in the nebula of contemporary spiritualities.

#### From table-turning to spiritism

In the eighteenth century Swedenborg published the account of his visions of the world of spirits. In 1848 the rappings heard in the house of the Fox sisters, in New York State, launched American spiritualism; the fashion for table-turning reached Europe in 1853. Allan Kardec, a teacher from Lyon, founded spiritism in 1857 with *The Spirits' Book*, a doctrine built from the answers obtained from spirits through mediums: God, the immortality of the soul, reincarnation and moral progress through successive lives. Spiritism presents itself as an experimental science and a philosophy rather than a Church; it remains very much alive in Brazil. Victor Hugo, on Jersey, made the tables speak from 1853 to 1855. Scientists were divided: Faraday and Chevreul explained the movements by unconscious muscular effort, while others, such as William Crookes or Charles Richet, studied mediums, and the Society for Psychical Research, founded in London in 1882, counted William James among its members.

#### Modern channeling

In the twentieth century the genre detached itself from spiritism and from the dead. The Seth messages, received by Jane Roberts from 1963 onwards, teach that each person creates their own reality. *A Course in Miracles*, dictated from 1965 to 1972 to the New York psychologist Helen Schucman by a voice presenting itself as Jesus, proposes a rereading of Christianity in which the world is an illusion and salvation a forgiveness that undoes guilt. Neale Donald Walsch's *Conversations with God*, from 1995 onwards, are written dialogues with God that became worldwide bestsellers.

The *Christ Letters* belong to this family. They are nine letters, accompanied by articles, presented as dictated by Christ to an anonymous South African woman, his Recorder, who says she prepared for the task over decades; circulated online in English, they appeared in French from Interkeltia in 2010, from Atlantes in 2012 and then in paperback. In them Christ corrects the interpretations of the Churches: no sin against God, no Trinity, no salvation through the blood of the cross; in their place, a universal Consciousness, an ego that is the source of suffering, a law of cause and effect described in terms of electromagnetism, and a practice of meditation and purification of thoughts.

#### Common features and scholarly readings

These texts share a source declared to lie outside the intermediary, a correction of the instituted religions in the name of an older or purer teaching, a cosmology of consciousness or energy, and an ethics of self-transformation. Historians of esotericism such as Wouter Hanegraaff and Olav Hammer study the genre's strategies of authority: the appeal to experience, to a lost tradition and to scientific vocabulary. Psychologists invoke dissociation, automatic writing and unconscious creativity; as early as 1900 Théodore Flournoy had shown in *From India to the Planet Mars* how a medium recomposed her forgotten reading. The Churches generally see in these texts a private revelation without guarantee, and the bodies that monitor sectarian abuses keep watch on the groups that form around certain messengers.

#### What the essay assumes

The essay assumes familiarity with spiritism, the channeling genre and the existence of the *Christ Letters*, which it examines in Part Twelve together with quantum mysticism. It reads in them a replacement revelation that contests the authority of the Churches in the name of a higher authority, a new Jesus made to the measure of his age, and a law of retribution that refuses the sacrifice of the cross but makes the sick responsible for their sickness.
