Between 1917 and 1945, the Christian Churches faced regimes that laid claim to the whole of man. They resisted, accommodated themselves or collaborated, and contemporary thinkers, Voegelin and Aron, described these regimes as religions without God, while Camus would speak of the divinization of history. Barth, Bonhoeffer and Schmitt, read in the essay, took positions within this history.

#### The Soviet Union and state atheism

The October Revolution made atheism a policy. The decree of January 1918 separated the Church from the State and the school from the Church, confiscated ecclesiastical property and stripped the Churches of legal personality. Persecution struck the Orthodox clergy from the civil war onwards, then peaked in the 1930s: the closure or destruction of most churches, tens of thousands of priests and monks shot or deported, and the League of Militant Godless, founded in 1925, entrusted with anti-religious propaganda. The Orthodox Church survived by submitting, through Metropolitan Sergius's declaration of loyalty in 1927, then enjoyed a respite during the war, when Stalin restored the patriarchate in 1943. After 1945 state atheism was extended to the people's democracies, with uneven violence, from Albania, which proclaimed itself the first atheist state in 1967, to Poland, where the Church remained powerful.

#### Nazi Germany: concordat, Deutsche Christen, Barmen

Hitler came to power in January 1933. In July the Holy See signed with the Reich a concordat guaranteeing freedom of worship and denominational education in exchange for the clergy's withdrawal from politics; the Catholic Centre Party had dissolved itself two weeks earlier. The concordat of 1933 was violated almost at once, and Pius XI protested in 1937 with the encyclical *Mit brennender Sorge*, read from the pulpit throughout Germany. On the Protestant side, the movement of the Deutsche Christen, the German Christians, wanted a Reich Church conforming to Nazism, purged of the Old Testament and of pastors of Jewish origin. Against them arose Martin Niemöller's Pastors' Emergency League, then the Confessing Church, whose synod at Barmen, in May 1934, adopted a theological declaration drafted for the most part by Karl Barth: Jesus Christ is the one Word of God, and the Church cannot recognize other events, powers or figures as revelation. Barth, who refused the oath to the Führer, was expelled from his chair at Bonn in 1935 and returned to Switzerland. Bonhoeffer directed the clandestine seminary of Finkenwalde, entered the resistance through the Abwehr, was arrested in 1943 and hanged at Flossenbürg on 9 April 1945. Carl Schmitt, a Catholic jurist, joined the party in May 1933 and justified the murders of June 1934 in an article asserting that the Führer protects the law, before being sidelined in 1936.

#### Italian Fascism and political religions

In 1929 Mussolini signed the Lateran Accords, which created the State of Vatican City and made Catholicism the religion of the Italian State; conflicts over youth followed in 1931. Faced with these regimes, contemporary observers forged the concepts the essay discusses: Voegelin, in *The Political Religions* (1938), describes the divinization of the people and of the State; Aron speaks in 1944 of secular religions, doctrines that take the place of faith and locate salvation here below; Berdyaev saw in communism an inverted religion; Emilio Gentile would speak of the sacralization of politics.

#### What the essay assumes

The essay assumes familiarity with the Barmen Declaration as the context of Barth's theology, with Bonhoeffer's commitment and death, which give the prison letters their weight, with Schmitt's adherence to Nazism, and with the fact that the secular religions of Aron and Voegelin refer first of all to communism and Nazism, the most massive cases of the sacrifice criterion.
