Eros, the Greek god of love, has had two very different histories: a cosmic power born at the origins of the world, and the young winged god, son of Aphrodite, who wounds with his arrows. The philosophers made him the name of the desire that raises the soul towards the beautiful and the good, and the tale of Eros and Psyche, handed down by Apuleius, became an allegory of the soul.

#### The primordial god and the winged god

In Hesiod's *Theogony*, Eros is one of the first beings, appearing with Chaos and Gaia, the most beautiful of the immortals, who loosens the limbs and overpowers the reason of gods and men; he is the force that makes beings unite and engender the world. The Orphic cosmogonies have him born from a primordial egg. In lyric poetry and then in the Hellenistic period, he becomes a winged child, son of Aphrodite and often of Ares, armed with a bow, whom the Romans call Cupid or Amor.

#### Plato's Symposium

In the *Symposium*, each guest delivers a eulogy of Eros. Socrates reports the teaching supposedly given to him by a priestess of Mantinea, Diotima: Eros is not a god but a daimon, an intermediary between gods and men, son of Penia, poverty, and of Poros, resource; he is desire for what he does not have, and therefore a philosopher. Diotima describes an ascent: from the love of one beautiful body to that of all beautiful bodies, then to the beauty of souls, of institutions and of the sciences, up to the sudden contemplation of beauty itself, eternal and unmixed. The *Phaedrus* takes up this ascent in the form of the winged soul that remembers the realities seen before birth.

#### Apuleius's tale

In the second century, Apuleius inserts into his *Metamorphoses* the tale of Cupid and Psyche. Psyche, a princess of a beauty that makes Venus jealous, is exposed on a rock, then carried off to a palace where an invisible husband joins her each night on condition that she does not try to see his face. Urged on by her sisters, she lights him with a lamp, discovers Cupid asleep, lets fall a drop of oil that wakes him, and he flees. After a series of trials imposed by Venus, among them a descent to the underworld, she is restored to her husband, receives immortality and gives birth to Pleasure. The Neoplatonists and the Christians read in it the journey of the soul, in Greek *psychè*, towards the divine.

#### Eros and Agape

In the twentieth century, the Lutheran theologian Anders Nygren contrasts Greek eros, the ascending desire of the soul seeking its own good, with Christian agape, the descending and gratuitous love of God for men, and sees the history of Christianity as their mixture. Freud names Eros the life drives, which he sets against the death drives.

#### What the essay assumes

The essay mentions the *Symposium* among the works that had marked Iris Murdoch most, whose sovereignty of the Good takes up the Platonic ascent: a desire that purifies itself into attention, and that tends towards a Good it does not possess.
