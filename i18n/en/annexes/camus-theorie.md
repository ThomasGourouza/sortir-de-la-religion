Albert Camus (1913-1960), born in Algeria into a poor family, a war orphan, a pupil of Jean Grenier, a journalist at *Alger républicain*, a member of the Resistance and editor-in-chief of *Combat*, novelist, playwright, Nobel laureate in literature in 1957, killed in a car accident, built his work in cycles, each made up of a novel, an essay and plays, and thought as a moralist about the condition of a man without God in the face of injustice.

#### The cycle of the absurd

*The Stranger* (1942), *The Myth of Sisyphus* (1942), *Caligula* and *The Misunderstanding* form the first cycle. The absurd arises from the confrontation between the human call for meaning and the silence of the world; it justifies neither suicide, which abolishes one of the terms, nor the leap into faith or hope, a philosophical suicide for which Camus reproaches Kierkegaard, Shestov and Jaspers. One must live the absurd by maintaining it, in revolt, freedom and passion, and the essay ends on Sisyphus rolling his rock, whom one must imagine happy. Meursault, who refuses to lie about his feelings and dies without God, is its fictional hero. *Nuptials* (1939) had expressed, in the light of Tipasa, the sensual harmony with the world that underlies everything.

#### The cycle of revolt

*The Plague* (1947), *The Rebel* (1951), *The State of Siege* and *The Just Assassins* form the second. Revolt is the movement by which a man says no and, through this no, affirms a value common to all: I revolt, therefore we are. *The Rebel* retraces its history: metaphysical revolt, from Sade to Nietzsche by way of Stirner, Ivan Karamazov and surrealism, which contests creation; historical revolt, from the regicides of 1793 to the deicides and the State terrorism of the twentieth century, which ends by divinizing history and killing in the name of the future; the nihilism in which both end when they forget their origin. Against this drift, the thought of noon, a thought of limit and measure inherited from Greece and the Mediterranean, refuses divinity in order to share the common struggles and destiny. The book earns Camus the attack of Francis Jeanson in *Les Temps modernes* and the break with Sartre in 1952: Sartre reproaches him for his moralism and his refusal of history, Camus reproaches the intellectuals for their indulgence towards the camps. Doctor Rieux, in *The Plague*, embodies revolt without God, and Father Paneloux faith before the death of a child.

#### The last texts

*The Fall* (1956), the monologue of Clamence, judge-penitent, turns irony against good conscience; *Exile and the Kingdom*, the *Reflections on the Guillotine* (1957), the *Chroniques algériennes* (1958), the Stockholm sentence about his mother, preferred to justice, and *The First Man*, unfinished, published in 1994, close the work. Camus never ceased to engage in dialogue with Christianity, whose hope he rejects, without hostility, and his 1936 dissertation dealt with Plotinus and Augustine.

#### What the essay assumes

The essay takes as known the absurd, philosophical suicide, Sisyphus happy, revolt as cogito, the divinization of history, the thought of noon, the 1952 quarrel and the critique of Jaspers, an attested response. It ranks Camus among the exceptions of attitude, unstable like Nietzsche, sees in the Mediterranean an aesthetic myth, the most discreet form of refoundation, a reservation that does not remove him from the exceptions, and borrows from him its answer to the sigh without a heaven, on condition that the *we* remains horizontal.
