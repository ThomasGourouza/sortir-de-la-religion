In eighty years, from Constantine's victory at the Milvian Bridge (312) to the ban on pagan sacrifices by Theodosius (391-392), Christianity passes from the status of a persecuted religion to that of the religion of the Empire. This is not a single act but a series of imperial decisions, which the bishops inspired and from which they profited.

#### Constantine: favour, not obligation

Constantine, master of the West after the Milvian Bridge, attributes his victory to the God of the Christians. In 313, at Milan, he agrees with Licinius to grant freedom of worship to all and to restore to the Churches their confiscated property. He does not make Christianity the official religion, but he heaps favours on the Church: tax exemptions for the clergy, jurisdiction for bishops, the building of basilicas in Rome (the Lateran, St Peter's) and in Jerusalem, Sunday rest in 321. Sole emperor from 324, he founds Constantinople and in 325 convenes the Council of Nicaea to settle Arius's quarrel over the divinity of the Son: the first time an emperor assembles the Church and guarantees its decisions. He receives baptism only on his deathbed, in 337.

His sons continue the policy and harden it: Constantius II bans sacrifices (341) and orders the closing of the temples (356). The Church then divides as much as it grows, and the emperor becomes the arbiter of its quarrels.

#### Julian: the reverse attempt

Julian, Constantine's nephew, brought up a Christian and secretly converted to Neoplatonism, reigns from 361 to 363. He restores paganism, but in a new form copied from the adversary: a hierarchical clergy, a morality, works of charity, a theology borrowed from Iamblichus. He forbids Christians to teach classical letters (362) and writes an *Against the Galileans* whose arguments echo those of Celsus and Porphyry. His death in Persia, after twenty months of rule, ends the experiment; Christian tradition nicknamed him the Apostate.

#### Theodosius: compulsory religion

His successors return to Christianity. Gratian renounces the title of pontifex maximus and has the altar of Victory removed from the Senate (382); in 384 the prefect Symmachus will plead in vain for its restoration, and Ambrose of Milan will answer him. In 380, by the Edict of Thessalonica, Theodosius orders all the peoples of the Empire to follow the faith of Nicaea as professed by the bishops of Rome and Alexandria, and calls the others madmen and heretics, liable to divine and then imperial punishment. The Council of Constantinople (381) confirms Nicaea. In 391 and 392 a series of laws bans sacrifices, visits to temples and domestic worship; the Serapeum of Alexandria is destroyed in 391. After the massacre of Thessalonica (390), Ambrose imposes a public penance on Theodosius: the bishop now judges the emperor.

The Empire is Christian, but pagans remain numerous, especially in the countryside, hence the word *paganus* according to the most common etymology, and in the senatorial aristocracy; Christianization still takes centuries. Repression turns towards heretics and schismatics: it is against the Donatists of Africa that Augustine, c. 405-412, justifies recourse to the secular arm.

#### What the essay assumes

The essay sums up this period in one sentence, the Christian emperors, from Constantine to Theodosius, carrying out the strict refoundation Plato had dreamed of, a rational theology armed by the state; it mentions Lactantius as tutor to Constantine's son, Julian as the refounder of a restored paganism, and Augustine against the Donatists. It assumes the reader knows that the alliance of Church and Empire, and not the conversion of individuals alone, made Christianity the religion of Europe.

- 341 and 356: laws of Constantius II against sacrifices and temples.
- 361-363: reign of Julian.
