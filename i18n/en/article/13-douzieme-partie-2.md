### Why is there something rather than nothing? A constructed debate

We must carry the widening of religion into spirituality to its end, and ask the question that precedes all religions and outlives them all: why is there something? It belongs to no Church. A child can ask it, and so can a physicist, and most of those who say today that they “believe in something” without believing in God mean, by that vague word, the emotion it arouses. It therefore allows us to test the criterion: is the question of being the door to a spirituality without God, or the back door through which theology returns? The debate is constructed; I indicate which of its exchanges are attested.

The starting point is Greek, and it is not yet the question. It is wonder. Socrates tells the young Theaetetus: “μάλα γὰρ φιλοσόφου τοῦτο τὸ πάθος, τὸ θαυμάζειν· οὐ γὰρ ἄλλη ἀρχὴ φιλοσοφίας ἢ αὕτη”, this affection is very much a philosopher's, to wonder; for there is no other beginning of philosophy than this (*Theaetetus*, 155d). Aristotle takes up the word at the beginning of the *Metaphysics*: “διὰ γὰρ τὸ θαυμάζειν οἱ ἄνθρωποι καὶ νῦν καὶ τὸ πρῶτον ἤρξαντο φιλοσοφεῖν”, it is through wonder that men, now as at the beginning, began to philosophize (A 2, 982b12-13). And he adds a remark precious for our purpose: “διὸ καὶ ὁ φιλόμυθος φιλόσοφός πώς ἐστιν· ὁ γὰρ μῦθος σύγκειται ἐκ θαυμασίων”, this is why the lover of myths is also in some way a philosopher, for myth is made of wondrous things (982b18-19). Wonder is the common root of myth and of philosophy, and therefore of religion and of its critique. But Greek wonder bears on phenomena, the phases of the moon, the generation of the whole; it does not ask why there is a whole rather than nothing. The Greeks had no idea of an absolute nothingness from which being would have been drawn; it took creation *ex nihilo* for the question to take its modern form. A first clue: the question that seems the most secular of all was born in a biblical world.

The modern form, perfectly sharp, is Leibniz's. In the *Principles of Nature and Grace, Based on Reason*, written in 1714, Leibniz first lays down the principle of sufficient reason, then, in § 7, quoted in Part Three, the first question we have the right to ask: why is there something rather than nothing, since nothing is simpler and easier than something? The answer follows: the sufficient reason of the universe cannot be found in the series of contingent things, where each state refers back to the previous one without end; it must lie outside that series, in a necessary substance, “portant la raison de son existence avec soi”, bearing the reason of its existence within itself, and “cette dernière raison des choses est appelée Dieu”, this ultimate reason of things is called God (§ 8). Three things should be noted. The question is not a wonder but a demand: “on a droit”, we have the right, to ask it, because reason requires it. It assumes that nothingness is the default state, “plus simple et plus facile”, simpler and easier, and that being calls for an explanation. And its answer does not stop at an abstract first principle: three paragraphs further on, this God has chosen the best of all possible worlds (§ 10), and, in § 18, read in Part Three, he is the object of a love that gives a foretaste of future felicity. In Leibniz, the question of being leads without a break to a piety: this is the back door.

The sceptical answer comes from Hume, and it is attested in its form, if not in its addressee. In Part IX of the *Dialogues Concerning Natural Religion* (published in 1779), Demea defends the argument he calls “the common one”, which asks what determined the existence of this series of causes rather than any other: “What was it, then, which determined Something to exist rather than Nothing, and bestowed being on a particular possibility, exclusive of the rest?” This is Clarke's argument, which has the same structure as Leibniz's; Hume names Clarke in a note a few lines further on, he does not name Leibniz. Cleanthes answers him with two blows. The first bears on necessity: “Whatever we conceive as existent, we can also conceive as non-existent. There is no being, therefore, whose non-existence implies a contradiction.” The second bears on the whole: each part of the series has its cause in the preceding one, and to demand in addition a cause of the whole is to forget that the unity of this whole “is performed merely by an arbitrary act of the mind, and has no influence on the nature of things”. Hume does not say that the question is absurd, but that it does not lead where Leibniz wanted to go. The whole needs no reason outside itself, because “the whole” is only a manner of speaking.

Kant takes up the difficulty from within and gives it the tone no longer of demand but of vertigo. In the *Critique of Pure Reason*, as he refutes the cosmological proof, he writes: “Die unbedingte Nothwendigkeit, die wir als den letzten Träger aller Dinge so unentbehrlich bedürfen, ist der wahre Abgrund für die menschliche Vernunft”, unconditioned necessity, which we so indispensably need as the ultimate bearer of all things, is the true abyss for human reason. And he imagines the supreme being speaking to itself: “Ich bin von Ewigkeit zu Ewigkeit, außer mir ist nichts ohne das, was bloß durch meinen Willen etwas ist; aber woher bin ich denn?”, I am from eternity to eternity, outside me there is nothing except what is something merely through my will; but whence then am I? “Hier sinkt alles unter uns”, here everything gives way beneath us. By putting the question to God himself, Kant shows that Leibniz's answer merely pushes it back, and he adds that we can neither dismiss this thought nor bear it. With Kant, the question of being becomes an affect, an experience of the abyss, and no longer a problem.

Schelling, half a century later, drives it to despair, and the question then changes its source. In the lectures of the *Philosophie der Offenbarung* (*Philosophy of Revelation*, delivered in Munich from 1831-1832, then in Berlin from 1841, published in 1858 by his son), it is not the spectacle of the universe that prompts it, it is man. Everything moves restlessly without aim, he writes, and, “wie ein altes Buch sagt”, as an old book says, everything under the sun is full of toil and labour: this is Qoheleth. Man, far from making the world intelligible, is “selbst das Unbegreiflichste”, himself the most incomprehensible thing of all. And Schelling concludes: “Gerade Er, der Mensch, treibt mich zur letzten verzweiflungsvollen Frage: warum ist überhaupt etwas? warum ist nicht nichts?”, it is precisely he, man, who drives me to the last, the despairing question: why is there anything at all? why is there not nothing? The passage goes on to say so: that there should exist a science which answers these questions and tears us from this despair is a need of human nature itself. This science, for Schelling, is a positive philosophy that culminates in a philosophy of revelation, that is, in Christianity thought through. The despairing question opens, as in Leibniz, onto a theology; but it opens onto it through anguish and not through sufficient reason.

Bergson, in 1907, declares the question a false problem. Chapter IV of *Creative Evolution* begins by describing from within the very movement of Leibniz and Schelling: “L'existence m'apparaît comme une conquête sur le néant. Je me dis qu'il pourrait, qu'il devrait même ne rien y avoir, et je m'étonne alors qu'il y ait quelque chose.”, existence appears to me as a conquest over nothingness; I tell myself that there could, that there even should, be nothing, and I am then astonished that there is something. He adds: “je ne puis me défaire de l'idée que le plein est une broderie sur le canevas du vide, que l'être est superposé au néant, et que dans la représentation de ‘rien’ il y a moins que dans celle de ‘quelque chose’. De là tout le mystère.”, I cannot rid myself of the idea that the full is an embroidery on the canvas of the void, that being is superimposed on nothingness, and that there is less in the representation of “nothing” than in that of “something”; hence the whole mystery. This is Leibniz's premise, the nothing “plus simple et plus facile”, simpler and easier; Bergson does not name Leibniz in these two sentences, but he names him at the end of the following paragraph, together with Spinoza, as the culmination of this movement: the connection is therefore his own. To think the absence of one thing is always to represent the presence of another; to suppress is to substitute. The idea of an abolition of everything is therefore “une idée destructive d'elle-même, une pseudo-idée, un simple mot”, a self-destroying idea, a pseudo-idea, a mere word, as absurd “que celle d'un cercle carré”, as that of a square circle. And the conclusion falls: the question of knowing “pourquoi quelque chose existe”, why something exists, is “une question dépourvue de sens, un pseudo-problème soulevé autour d'une pseudo-idée”, a question devoid of meaning, a pseudo-problem raised around a pseudo-idea. Once nothingness is dispelled, one can posit an absolute that acts freely and endures, the one Bergson would find again, twenty-five years later, in the mystics of the *Two Sources*. He dissolved the question, but he did not dissolve wonder: he moved it from existence to creation.

In Heidegger, the reply is attested twice. In 1929, the Freiburg inaugural lecture, *Was ist Metaphysik?*, ends on the question; in 1935, the lecture course published in 1953 under the title *Einführung in die Metaphysik* (*Introduction to Metaphysics*) opens with it, and Heidegger declares it the first of all questions, the broadest, the deepest, the most originary. Above all, in 1949, in the introduction added to the fifth edition of *Was ist Metaphysik?*, he answers Leibniz explicitly: he acknowledges that the final question of his lecture, “Warum ist überhaupt Seiendes und nicht vielmehr Nichts?”, why are there beings at all and not rather nothing?, is “offenbar die Frage, die der Metaphysiker Leibniz in seinen Principes de la nature et de la grace gestellt hat”, evidently the question that the metaphysician Leibniz asked in his *Principles*, and he wonders why, if that is so, Leibniz's name was not mentioned. The question is asked there, he replies, “in einem ganz anderen Sinne”, in an entirely different sense. It does not ask for the first cause of beings; it asks how it comes about that beings everywhere take precedence, while nothingness, understood as being itself, remains forgotten. And Heidegger turns Leibniz's own premise against him: if being were not forgotten, Leibniz could not have written, by way of explanation, “Car le rien est plus simple et plus facile que quelque chose”, for nothing is simpler and easier than something. Leibniz's question is the question of metaphysics; the same question, asked otherwise, would be the exit from metaphysics.

One might side with Heidegger: he wrested the question from Leibnizian theology and gave it back to wonder. But one must read the postscript to the same small volume, written in 1943. The thinking of being, Heidegger says there, is a thanking, *Danken*, for the favour by which being has given itself to man, and this thanking is accomplished in sacrifice: “Das Opfer ist der Abschied vom Seienden auf dem Gang zur Wahrung der Gunst des Seins”, sacrifice is the farewell to beings on the way to safeguarding the favour of being. Sacrifice is the “Verschwendung des Menschenwesens”, the squandering of the human essence, for the guarding of the truth of being; and it “duldet keine Berechnung”, it tolerates no calculation of its usefulness or uselessness. I do not wish to load these lines with a meaning they do not have: they speak of the sacrifice of thought, not of soldiers. But they were written in 1943, and the vocabulary of farewell, of squandering and of the refusal of all calculation was not neutral that year. What matters for us is that the question of being, which Heidegger had wrested from Leibnizian piety, ends in his work in a still more demanding piety, one that calls in so many words for a sacrifice. The criterion is borne out here: the moment wonder turns into debt is the moment spirituality becomes religion again.

The most brutal reply came from Vienna. In 1931, in the journal *Erkenntnis*, Rudolf Carnap took the 1929 lecture as a model of metaphysical nonsense, and he ridiculed the question of being as a violation of the rules of logical syntax; Heidegger abandoned the word “metaphysics” shortly afterwards, partly, according to Taylor Carman, in reaction to this attack. For Carnap there is neither abyss nor mystery: there is a faulty use of the word “nothing”, treated as a noun when it is a negation. One might think one was hearing Bergson, with formal logic replacing psychology; but the convergence is deceptive. Bergson dissolves the question to set creative duration free; Carnap dissolves it to set science free. The first keeps wonder, the second dismisses it.

Wittgenstein, for his part, holds both theses at once. The *Tractatus* (1921) says on the one hand: “Das Rätsel gibt es nicht”, the riddle does not exist, and: “Wenn sich eine Frage überhaupt stellen lässt, so kann sie auch beantwortet werden”, if a question can be put at all, then it can also be answered (6.5). Leibniz's question is therefore not a question. But he says on the other hand, as we read in Part Ten, that the mystical is not how the world is, but that it is (6.44), and that the inexpressible shows itself (6.522); the 1929 *Lecture on Ethics* names the experience in view, wondering at the existence of the world, while admitting that language is then being misused. Wonder survives the destruction of the question: without interrogative form, hence without any possible answer, hence without God at the end, it is what Wittgenstein calls the mystical, and the section devoted to him in this essay has shown that he did not see a religion in it.

Jaspers, finally, replays the scene in the vocabulary of failure, with the phrase Camus borrows from him and which we read above: where reason fails to say why there is something, Jaspers sees not nothing but transcendence, through a leap that leads to no Church.

I believe the debate allows us to decide. The question “why is there something rather than nothing?” has received, from Plato to Wittgenstein, four treatments. The first takes it as a demand of reason and answers it with God: this is Leibniz, this is Demea, this is, by way of despair, Schelling. The second dissolves it as an ill-formed question: this is Hume, Kant in his own way, Bergson, Carnap, the Wittgenstein of 6.5. The third keeps it as a wonder without an answer: this is Plato and Aristotle before it, the Wittgenstein of 6.44, Jaspers. The fourth converts it into debt: this is Heidegger, in whom the question, after being wrested from theology, issues in thanking and sacrifice.

The question is therefore in itself neither religious nor secular; it becomes one or the other according to what one does with its affect. Whoever wants an answer meets God, Being or a first principle, and falls back into the onto-theology that Heidegger denounced in Leibniz before falling back into it himself in another way. If one dissolves the question, one may lose wonder along with it, like Carnap, or keep it, like Bergson and Wittgenstein. This last way seems to me the only outcome that does not turn into debt. Wonder before existence is perhaps the only religious affect that demands nothing: it founds no obligation, promises nothing, has neither an addressee to thank nor an altar on which to sacrifice. The Greeks saw in it the beginning of philosophy; Wittgenstein saw in it the mystical; one may see in it, without adding anything, the heart of a spirituality without God. The condition is strict, and Heidegger shows what happens when it is violated: one must not turn the fact that there is something into a gift, for a gift calls for gratitude, gratitude for a creditor, and the creditor, sooner or later, for a sacrifice.

Let us give the opposing thesis its best form. Leibniz would reply that wonder without an answer is a laziness of reason, and that to give up the principle of sufficient reason is to give up science itself. I answer with Hume and Kant that the principle holds within the world and does not hold for the world, and that science has never needed to apply it to the whole. Heidegger would reply that wonder without gratitude is still the attitude of a calculating subject, who receives being without thinking it. I answer that to think being is not to owe it something, and that this whole essay turns on that difference. Epicurus's sage looked at the world without debt; Spinoza loved God without expecting to be loved in return; Wittgenstein wondered that the world is without asking to whom he owed it. This, I believe, is what “believing in something” can mean today, in all rigour: not believing that there is someone behind the world, but never ceasing to wonder that there is a world.

### Iris Murdoch (1919-1999): the Good without God

Wonder asks nothing and founds nothing. Iris Murdoch asks more: she maintains that there is, at the heart of moral life, an absolute that is not God, and that we must keep from religion what was most demanding in it while abandoning what was most consoling. That is why she puts the criterion to a fine test: her Good is an absolute, but an absolute that calls for no sacrifice, only the dispossession of the self.

She is known above all as a novelist, an injustice that English philosophy took a long time to repair. She taught at Oxford from 1948 to 1963, in a milieu dominated by the analysis of moral language, and she brought there readings that her colleagues considered exotic: Sartre, to whom she devoted her first book, *Sartre: Romantic Rationalist* (1953), Simone Weil, Plato read as a living author, and Buddhist thought. In 1968 she called herself, according to a phrase reported by the entry devoted to her, “a kind of Platonist”. Her philosophy is essentially contained in a small book, *The Sovereignty of Good* (1970), which gathers three lectures: “The Idea of Perfection”, delivered in 1962 and published in 1964, “On ‘God’ and ‘Good’”, given in 1966 and published in 1969, and “The Sovereignty of Good over Other Concepts”, the Leslie Stephen Lecture at Cambridge, in 1967. She would take all this up again, complicating it, in *Metaphysics as a Guide to Morals* (1992), her last major book of philosophy, after *The Fire and the Sun* (1977), devoted to Plato and the arts.

The adversary of the first lecture is not a theologian; it is the modern subject as drawn both by existentialism and by the analytic philosophy of her day: a pure will, choosing freely among neutral facts, whose morality consists in its public acts. Murdoch had read Sartre closely, and she sees in this figure the secular descendant of the voluntarist God: a being who brings values into existence by choosing them. Murdoch sets against it another picture of moral life, in which the essential is played out before the choice, in the way we see. Our gaze is spontaneously distorted by the ego, by its fantasies, its resentments and its consolations; the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entry quotes her phrase about “the fat, relentless ego”. The moral task is therefore first of all one of perception: to see the other as he or she is, as real as oneself, distinct from oneself. It has a name: attention.

The word comes from Simone Weil, and the debt is attested. In 1976 Murdoch cited *Waiting for God* among the only three philosophical works that had marked her deeply, together with the *Symposium* and *Fear and Trembling*. In Weil, attention is a religious category, and she says so plainly: “L'attention, à son plus haut degré, est la même chose que la prière. Elle suppose la foi et l'amour”, attention, at its highest degree, is the same thing as prayer; it presupposes faith and love. And further on: “L'attention absolument sans mélange est prière”, absolutely unmixed attention is prayer. Murdoch takes up the word and withdraws faith from it: the supreme object of attention is no longer God, but reality, other people, beauty, the Good. She also takes from Weil the idea of “decreation”, but transposes it into a soberer vocabulary: the ego must fall silent, and she calls this silence “unselfing”. Her sharpest formula: “We cease to be in order to attend to the existence of something else”.

Her example is one of the most famous in the century's moral philosophy, because it is tiny. She is looking out of the window, absorbed in a wound to her pride: “I am looking out of my window in an anxious and resentful state of mind, oblivious to my surroundings… Then suddenly I observe a hovering kestrel”. And everything changes: “The brooding self with its hurt vanity has disappeared. There is nothing now but kestrel”. When she returns to her worry, it seems less important to her. The experience has nothing mystical about it in the sense James or Otto meant. Without revelation, without content, without consolation, it merely removes the ego from the centre of the picture, and that is why Murdoch holds it to be moral: natural beauty, art, the study of a foreign language or of a difficult science all do the same thing, they oblige us to submit to something that is not ourselves.

Towards what is this attention directed? On this point Murdoch meets our question. In “On ‘God’ and ‘Good’”, she proposes a definition of God that is at the same time a leave-taking: “I shall suggest that God was (or is) a single perfect transcendent non-representable and necessarily real object of attention”. And she adds that moral philosophy must try to keep a concept that has all these characteristics without being God: that concept is the Good. Murdoch's Good is the Good of the *Republic*, the principle that is beyond essence and that makes all things visible as the sun makes objects visible; the Stanford entry reports that she herself compares the Good to a light that enables us to see the good in particular things. Part One of this essay quoted Plato's Greek text on the Good “beyond essence”; Murdoch is a faithful and free reader of it, since she keeps the sun without keeping the world of Forms. The Good is not a person; it neither commands nor rewards. It is what orients, and it retains, she says, something irreducibly mysterious: “A genuine mysteriousness attaches to the idea of goodness and the Good”.

Prayer, then, does not disappear; it changes its nature. The Wikipedia article on the book reports the formula according to which prayer, properly understood, is not petition but an attention to God which is a form of love; I give it in paraphrase, not having read it in the text. What Murdoch proposes is a prayer without an addressee, or rather a prayer whose addressee is no longer someone: patient and loving attention to what is. The kinship with Hadot's spiritual exercises is real: for both, philosophy is a conversion of the gaze. But the difference is real too. Hadot describes ancient exercises that he does not claim have a transcendent object; Murdoch maintains that there is an object, the Good, and that it is real. She is more of a metaphysician than he is, and closer to Simone Weil.

She is also opposed, without having known it, to Comte-Sponville's spirituality of immanence, for which the universe suffices and which makes mystical experience a yes to everything that happens. Murdoch would not accept this identification of the Whole and the Good. Reality, for her, is not good because it is; it is the place where one can learn to see the good, which is not the same thing. Her Good judges the world, it does not bless it. The confrontation is constructed, since Murdoch died in 1999 and *L'Esprit de l'athéisme* dates from 2006; it sets against each other two ways of keeping the absolute without God, one that dissolves it in the totality, the other that maintains it above it.

Measured against the criterion, Murdoch is a limiting case, and that is what makes her precious. She unambiguously installs an absolute: the Good is sovereign, transcendent, necessary. One could therefore rank her among the refounders, alongside those who put Man, Society or History in the place of God. But this absolute demands no sacrifice: it does not ask that individuals be immolated to it, but that we cease to immolate ourselves to our own fantasies. The dispossession it requires is not submission to an authority that would speak in its name, since the Good does not speak and has neither Church nor priests; it is an unburdening of the gaze. Murdoch therefore belongs, I believe, to the family of exceptions, where she occupies a place that was missing: between Spinoza and Hadot, who think immanence and exercise, and Simone Weil, who keeps the faith, she proposes a transcendent absolute without God, which demands nothing but attention. Her weakness is the one Marx would have pointed out: a morality of vision risks leaving the world as it is, provided one sees it clearly. Murdoch was not unaware of this, and her novels are full of characters who see rightly and do not act. But she replied that we act badly because we see badly, and that most of the crimes committed in the name of an absolute were committed by men who no longer looked at anyone.
