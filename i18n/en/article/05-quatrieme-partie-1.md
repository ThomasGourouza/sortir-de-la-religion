## Part Four: the Enlightenment and religion within limits

The Enlightenment did not have one doctrine of religion but at least five, which fought one another as fiercely as they fought the Church. Scottish empiricism made religion an object of natural history. French deism kept it as a social restraint. Rousseau refounded it twice, as a religion of the heart and as a civil religion. The materialism of Baron d'Holbach sought to destroy it. Kant's critical philosophy reduced it to morality. These five positions are still ours: we explain religion, we use it, we refound it, we fight it, or we keep what seems reasonable in it. Almost none of them leaves religion without leaving a remainder, and that remainder almost always takes the form of a cult; Hume alone is the exception.

### Hume (1711-1776): a natural history of belief

David Hume published *The Natural History of Religion* in 1757, in a collection entitled *Four Dissertations*. His *Dialogues Concerning Natural Religion*, drafted from the 1750s onward and revised until his death, appeared only in 1779, posthumously, through the care of his nephew. The gap between the dates tells of the author's caution: the first book explains religion without judging it; the second judges it, but through the mouths of characters.

The *Natural History* changes the question: no longer whether God exists, but where, in human nature, belief in invisible powers comes from. And it starts from a fact that the theologians of his time regarded as a late corruption: “polytheism or idolatry was, and necessarily must have been, the first and most ancient religion of mankind” (section 1). Monotheism is not the origin but a late product, for religion was not born of the contemplation of the order of the world, as the deists believe with their argument from design, but of anxiety. The first religious ideas arose “not from a contemplation of the works of nature, but from a concern with regard to the events of life, and from the incessant hopes and fears, which actuate the human mind” (section 2).

One recognizes the thesis of Lucretius and Spinoza, fear at the origin of the gods; but Hume adds a dynamic to it. Religions oscillate: “the principles of religion have a kind of flux and reflux in the human mind”, and men have a natural tendency to rise from idolatry to theism, then to sink back from theism into idolatry (section 8). The one and infinite God is too abstract for popular devotion, which gives him intercessors, saints, images; and when these multiply, a reform recalls the divine unity. This law of oscillation is one of the most fertile ideas in the tradition: it anticipates Weber, for whom religious rationalization itself produces disenchantment, and Bergson, for whom dynamic religion always falls back into static religion.

He adds a comparison that Bayle had prepared and that all liberals would take up: polytheism is tolerant, monotheism intolerant, for a single god suffers no rivals. And he ends on a sceptical observation of great beauty. Having reviewed popular beliefs, which one might readily take for “sick men's dreams”, he concludes: “The whole is a riddle, an ænigma, an inexplicable mystery. Doubt, uncertainty, suspence of judgment appear the only result of our most accurate scrutiny, concerning this subject”. But this very doubt, he adds, would be hard to sustain without a stratagem: setting the superstitions against one another, making them quarrel, and during their fury “happily make our escape, into the calm, though obscure, regions of philosophy”.

This sentence defines the sceptical exit, and all those that follow are measured against it. Hume does not claim to have refuted religion, proposes nothing in its place, founds no cult. He escapes, while admitting that the region into which he escapes is obscure. He practises the regime of error without drawing a refoundation from it, and he is thus, with Spinoza and Nietzsche, one of the rare authors in this history who leave without refounding, by a path that Spinoza and Nietzsche did not take: not a spirituality, but a suspension. Is it livable? Not all the time, Hume answered, whose sceptical philosopher, in *A Treatise of Human Nature*, goes back to play backgammon with his friends: “I dine, I play a game of back-gammon, I converse, and am merry with my friends” (I, 4, 7, § 9).

The *Dialogues* stage three characters. Cleanthes defends experimental theism: the universe resembles a machine, so it has an author similar to the human mind. Demea defends orthodox a priori theology: God is incomprehensible, and his nature exceeds all analogy. Philo, the sceptic, allies himself by turns with one against the other and demolishes the argument from design: the analogy is weak, since one could just as well compare the universe to an animal or a vegetable, and an imperfect world suggests an imperfect god, or several, or an apprentice. At the heart of the book, in Part X, Philo takes up an ancient argument: “EPICURUS's old questions are yet unanswered. Is he willing to prevent evil, but not able? then is he impotent. Is he able, but not willing? then is he malevolent. Is he both able and willing? whence then is evil?”

This trilemma, which Hume attributes to Epicurus on the authority of Lactantius (*De ira Dei*, 13), an attribution now disputed, is the argument that Ivan Karamazov will take up in concrete form, with the tortured children. But Philo does not conclude in favour of atheism. In Part XII he even seems to rally to a minimal form of theism, and he lets fall this sentence, whose irony is undecidable: “To be a philosophical Sceptic is, in a man of letters, the first and most essential step towards being a sound, believing Christian”. A tribute to Pascal, who wished to humble reason to make room for faith, a precaution, or mockery? Critics disagree, and I believe the ambiguity must be left where Hume put it: the sceptic can no more affirm the non-existence of God than his existence, and his exit from religion is an exit *from certainty*, in one direction as in the other.

Hume's clearest successor is Kant, who would say that Hume had woken him from his dogmatic slumber (about causality, in the *Prolegomena* of 1783), and whose *Critique of Pure Reason* (1781) would take up the critique of the proofs of God's existence. But he would draw from it a conclusion Hume had not envisaged: if theoretical reason cannot prove God, practical reason can *postulate* him.

### Voltaire (1694-1778): “it would be necessary to invent him”

Voltaire is not an atheist, and he repeated it all his life with an insistence that is not mere caution. He is a deist: he believes in a Supreme Being, architect of the universe and guarantor of morality, and he fights the “Infâme”, the Infamous, that is, superstition, fanaticism and the Church that sustains them. His position is the mirror image of Spinoza's: Spinoza criticizes the vulgar image of God in order to keep an impersonal God; Voltaire criticizes the Church in order to keep a personal God who judges.

The central text is a verse epistle of 1769 to the anonymous author of the *Treatise of the Three Impostors*, a clandestine pamphlet that made three frauds of Moses, Jesus and Muhammad. Voltaire grows angry there with militant atheism: “Corrige le valet, mais respecte le maître. / Dieu ne doit point pâtir des sottises du prêtre”, correct the servant, but respect the master; God must not suffer for the follies of the priest. Then he sets out the function of belief in God in lines from which comes the most quoted text of the whole affair:

“Ce système sublime à l'homme est nécessaire. / C'est le sacré lien de la société, / Le premier fondement de la sainte équité, / Le frein du scélérat, l'espérance du juste.” This sublime system is necessary to man. It is the sacred bond of society, the first foundation of holy equity, the curb of the wicked, the hope of the just.

And a little further on, the line that became a proverb: “Si Dieu n'existait pas, il faudrait l'inventer. / Que le sage l'annonce, et que les rois le craignent.” If God did not exist, it would be necessary to invent him. Let the wise man proclaim him, and let kings fear him.

People always quote the first of these alexandrines, almost never the second, which nonetheless gives it its meaning. Voltaire does not merely say that God is useful to the people; he says that the wise man must *proclaim* him, and that *kings* must fear him. This God has a double political function: he restrains the wicked, but also the tyrant, and the rest of the epistle warns kings that if they oppress the innocent, God will judge them. The idea is therefore not, or not only, the cynicism of an aristocrat who would like his servants to believe in God so as not to be robbed, a cynicism Voltaire did formulate elsewhere, but in the mouth of a character, the Englishman A in *L'A, B, C* (1768): “Je veux que mon procureur, mon tailleur, mes valets, ma femme même, croient en Dieu ; et je m'imagine que j'en serai moins volé et moins cocu”, I want my lawyer, my tailor, my servants, even my wife, to believe in God; and I imagine I shall be less robbed and less cuckolded for it (seventeenth conversation); it is a theory of the moral contract guaranteed by a transcendence.

Read in the light of what was to come, the formula changes in scope. “If God did not exist, it would be necessary to invent him”: the line entirely separates the question of God's existence from that of his function. It matters little whether he exists; people must believe in him. This is the programme of Dostoevsky's Grand Inquisitor, who governs men by miracle, mystery and authority without himself believing in God, and who does so, like Voltaire, in the name of men's good. The novel confirms it: Ivan quotes this line two chapters before the poem (Part Seven).

Voltairean deism is an exit from religion, in the sense that it abandons dogmas, miracles, Scriptures, the Church. But it keeps what Durkheim will call the function of the sacred: a “sacré lien de la société”, a sacred bond of society. Voltaire emptied religion of its content in order to keep its form, and that form is precisely that of an absolute that binds. It is a refoundation that does not hide itself: it *invents* God, in full awareness, for his function. This line makes God a necessary artefact.

### Rousseau (1712-1778): the religion of the heart and the catechism of the citizen

Rousseau occupies in this debate a place that neither Voltaire nor d'Holbach can hold, because he makes two contrary gestures in the same year. In 1762 there appeared *Emile*, whose Book IV contains the *Profession of Faith of the Savoyard Vicar*, and *The Social Contract*, whose penultimate chapter is entitled “De la religion civile”, “On civil religion”. The first text is a radical exit from the Church written by a believer; the second, a precise project for a political religion, twenty-seven years before the Revolution. He leaves the Church and refounds a religion in two books issued by different publishers, which Geneva condemns together.

The *Profession of Faith* is a natural religion, but not that of the philosophers. The vicar does not demonstrate God by abstract reasoning; he starts from inner feeling. Before the order of the world, he does not deduce, he experiences: “Je crois donc que le monde est gouverné par une volonté puissante & sage ; je le vois, ou plutôt je le sens, & cela m'importe à savoir”, I believe, then, that the world is governed by a powerful and wise will; I see it, or rather I feel it, and that is what matters for me to know. The “ou plutôt je le sens”, “or rather I feel it”, is the whole difference from Voltaire. Voltaire's God is a clockmaker one infers; the vicar's is a presence one feels. And morality comes neither from a revealed law nor from a calculation of interest; it comes from a faculty that Rousseau hails as an inner divinity: “Conscience ! conscience ! instinct divin ; immortelle & céleste voix, guide assuré d'un être ignorant & borné, mais intelligent & libre ; juge infaillible du bien & du mal, qui rends l'homme semblable à Dieu”, conscience! conscience! divine instinct, immortal and heavenly voice, sure guide of a being ignorant and limited, but intelligent and free; infallible judge of good and evil, who makes man like God.

This felt God needs no intermediaries, and the *Profession of Faith* draws an explosive consequence from this. God has spoken, say the revealed religions; the vicar then asks questions all the stronger for being asked by a priest: “Comment cela ? Par des prodiges. Et où sont ces prodiges ? Dans des livres. Et qui a fait ces livres ? Des hommes. Et qui a vu ces prodiges ? Des hommes qui les attestent. Quoi ! toujours des témoignages humains ? toujours des hommes qui me rapportent ce que d'autres hommes ont rapporté ? Que d'hommes entre Dieu & moi !” How so? Through wonders. And where are these wonders? In books. And who made these books? Men. And who saw these wonders? Men who attest them. What! Always human testimony? Always men reporting to me what other men have reported? How many men between God and me! Revelation is a chain of testimony human from end to end. The vicar draws the conclusion: “J'ai donc refermé tous les livres. Il en est un seul ouvert à tous les yeux, c'est celui de la Nature”, so I have closed all the books. There is only one open to all eyes, the book of Nature. External cults, matters of climate, government and national genius, are all of equal worth when they are sincere, for “le culte essentiel est celui du cœur”, the essential worship is that of the heart.

One might think one was reading Spinoza, and the kinship is real on one point: like the *Tractatus*, the vicar reduces religion to the practice of justice and charity, and denies miracles any value as proof. But the spirit is the reverse. Spinoza rejects miracles because they would contradict the necessary order of nature, which is God, the vicar because they put men between God and himself: the one speaks in the name of reason, the other in the name of the heart. And the vicar's heart keeps what Spinoza's reason set aside: an immense reverence for the Gospel. This is the most famous page of the *Profession of Faith*, and it was enough to have it condemned by both camps. The vicar confesses that “la majesté des Écritures m'étonne, la sainteté de l'Évangile parle à mon cœur”, the majesty of the Scriptures astonishes me, the holiness of the Gospel speaks to my heart, and he compares the death of Socrates with that of Jesus: “Oui, si la vie & la mort de Socrate sont d'un Sage, la vie & la mort de Jesus sont d'un Dieu”, yes, if the life and death of Socrates are those of a sage, the life and death of Jesus are those of a God. But he adds at once that this same Gospel “est plein de choses incroyables, de choses qui répugnent à la raison, & qu'il est impossible à tout homme sensé de concevoir ni d'admettre”, is full of incredible things, things repugnant to reason, which no sensible man can conceive or accept, and he concludes with what he himself calls a “scepticisme involontaire”, an involuntary scepticism, which suspends judgment on everything that does not bear on conduct.

This position, a very coherent one, holds against both its adversaries. Against the philosophers, it maintains that a principled atheism is a presumption as dogmatic as faith, and that one does not live on demonstrations. Against the Churches, it maintains that no one can be damned for not having believed what he could not examine, and that God cannot require of us, as the vicar says, to be so learned. This is the position Kant would take up in 1793 by separating pure religious faith from ecclesiastical faith, and he never hid his debt. In personal notes from the years 1764-1765 he writes that Rousseau set him right and taught him to honour human beings. The response is attested: religion within the boundaries of mere reason is the *Profession of Faith* translated into the language of critique.

The judges, for their part, were not mistaken. The Parlement of Paris condemned *Emile* in June 1762 and issued a warrant for Rousseau's arrest; the book was torn up and burned. A few days later the Small Council of Geneva condemned *Emile* and *The Social Contract*. The Archbishop of Paris, Christophe de Beaumont, published a pastoral letter against *Emile*, to which Rousseau replied in 1763 with his *Letter to Christophe de Beaumont*. The citizen of Geneva, as he signed his books, was driven from his homeland for having written a religion without a Church.

Yet in the same year Rousseau wrote the chapter that makes him the great modern theorist of political religion. Chapter VIII of Book IV of *The Social Contract* begins with a typology. There is the religion of man, “sans Temples, sans autels, sans rites, bornée au culte purement intérieur du Dieu Suprême & aux devoirs éternels de la morale”, without temples, without altars, without rites, limited to the purely inward worship of the Supreme God and to the eternal duties of morality, which is “la pure & simple Religion de l'Evangile, le vrai Théïsme”, the pure and simple religion of the Gospel, true theism: this is the vicar's religion. There is the religion of the citizen, national, with its tutelary gods and its rites prescribed by law. And there is a third, the worst, which gives men two legislations and two homelands: “On peut appeller celui-ci la religion du Prêtre”, this one may be called the religion of the priest, and the example Rousseau gives of it is Roman Christianity.

The religion of man, however pure, is politically useless, and even harmful, because it detaches hearts from the earthly city. Rousseau goes as far as this sentence, always forgotten when he is made into a sentimental Christian: “Les vrais Chrétiens sont faits pour être esclaves ; ils le savent & ne s'en émeuvent gueres ; cette courte vie a trop peu de prix à leurs yeux”, true Christians are made to be slaves; they know it and are hardly moved by it; this short life has too little value in their eyes. A Christian republic is a contradiction in terms: “chacun de ces deux mots exclud l'autre”, each of these two words excludes the other. Something else is therefore needed, and Rousseau takes the step. “Il y a donc une profession de foi purement civile dont il appartient au Souverain de fixer les articles, non pas précisément comme dogmes de Religion, mais comme sentimens de sociabilité, sans lesquels il est impossible d'être bon Citoyen ni sujet fidele”, there is therefore a purely civil profession of faith whose articles it is for the Sovereign to fix, not exactly as dogmas of religion, but as sentiments of sociability, without which it is impossible to be a good citizen or a faithful subject.

The articles are listed with the precision of a catechism. The positive dogmas are “L'existence de la Divinité puissante, intelligente, bienfaisante, prévoyante & pourvoyante, la vie à venir, le bonheur des justes, le châtiment des méchans, la sainteté du Contract social & des Loix”, the existence of a powerful, intelligent, beneficent, foreseeing and providing Divinity, the life to come, the happiness of the just, the punishment of the wicked, the sanctity of the social contract and the laws; as for the negative dogmas, Rousseau limits them to one: “c'est l'intolérance”, it is intolerance. One might believe this a minimal and tolerant religion, were it not that the preceding sentence gives the sanction: “Sans pouvoir obliger personne à les croire, il peut bannir de l'Etat quiconque ne les croit pas ; il peut le bannir, non comme impie, mais comme insociable, comme incapable d'aimer sincerement les loix la justice, & d'immoler au besoin sa vie à son devoir. Que si quelqu'un, après avoir reconnu publiquement ces mêmes dogmes, se conduit comme ne les croyant pas, qu'il soit puni de mort ; il a commis le plus grand des crimes, il a menti devant les loix”, without being able to oblige anyone to believe them, it may banish from the State whoever does not believe them; it may banish him, not as impious, but as unsociable, as incapable of sincerely loving the laws and justice, and of sacrificing his life to his duty if need be. If anyone, after publicly acknowledging these same dogmas, behaves as though he did not believe them, let him be punished with death; he has committed the greatest of crimes, he has lied before the laws.

I know of no text that illustrates my criterion more exactly: one refounds a religion when one installs an absolute in whose name the sacrifice of the individual can be demanded, and criticism of which becomes sacrilege, and Rousseau writes it out in full. The unsociable man is the one who would be incapable of “immoler au besoin sa vie à son devoir”, sacrificing his life to his duty if need be, and the sanctity of the social contract is a dogma whose betrayal is paid for with one's life. The absolute is no longer God, who appears in the list only as a guarantor; the absolute is the city. Civil religion is a religion of the exit from religion: it takes from the priest the power to damn and gives it to the Sovereign, and replaces hell with exile and the scaffold. The parallel with Plato is constructed: Book X of the *Laws* had already made belief in the gods a matter of law and punished impiety, and Rousseau, an assiduous reader of Plato, rediscovers the same gesture at the very moment he believes he is founding freedom.

The project is, moreover, older. Rousseau had formulated it six years earlier, in a letter addressed to Voltaire, and had presented it as a remedy for intolerance. The *Letter to Voltaire on Providence*, of 18 August 1756, answers the *Poem on the Lisbon Disaster*, in which Voltaire had mocked optimism in the face of the dead of the 1755 earthquake. Rousseau defends Providence there with one argument that makes one smile and another that strikes home. The argument that makes one smile is that men are responsible for their own ills: nature, he writes, “n'avoit point rassemblé là vingt mille maisons de six à sept étages”, had not assembled twenty thousand houses of six or seven storeys there. The argument that strikes home is existential, and it is the vicar's cry before the vicar: “Non, j'ai trop souffert en cette vie pour n'en pas attendre une autre.[…] Je la sens, je la crois, je la veux, je l'espere, je la défendrai jusqu'a mon dernier soupir”, no, I have suffered too much in this life not to expect another. I feel it, I believe it, I will it, I hope for it, I shall defend it to my last breath. Shortly before comes the proposal that heralds *The Social Contract*: “Je voudrois donc qu'on eût dans chaque État un code moral, ou une espece de profession de foi civile qui contînt positivement les maximes sociales que chacun seroit tenu d'admettre, & négativement les maximes intolérantes qu'on seroit tenu de rejetter, non comme impies, mais comme séditieuses”, I would therefore wish that each State had a moral code, or a kind of civil profession of faith, containing positively the social maxims everyone would be bound to accept, and negatively the intolerant maxims one would be bound to reject, not as impious but as seditious. And Rousseau invites Voltaire to write it: “Vous nous avez donné dans votre Poëme sur la Religion naturelle le catéchisme de l'homme, donnez-nous maintenant dans celui que je vous propose le catéchisme du citoyen”, you have given us in your Poem on Natural Religion the catechism of man; give us now, in the one I propose to you, the catechism of the citizen. The same letter allows that one may “commencer par exterminer”, begin by exterminating, the religions that attack the foundations of society.

Voltaire's response is attested, and it is a flat refusal. On 12 September 1756, from Les Délices, he writes: “Mon cher Philosophe, nous pouvons vous & moi, dans les intervalles de nos maux, raisonner en vers & en prose”, my dear Philosopher, you and I can, in the intervals of our ills, reason in verse and in prose; but he begs to be excused for the moment from these “discussions philosophiques qui ne sont que des amusemens”, philosophical discussions that are mere amusements. The real answer came three years later: *Candide* (1759); Rousseau himself asserted, in Book IX of the *Confessions*, that the novel was the promised answer to his letter: “Elle n'est autre que le roman de Candide, dont je ne puis parler, parce que je ne l'ai pas lu”, it is none other than the novel *Candide*, of which I cannot speak, because I have not read it. The quarrel turned to hatred: in 1764 Voltaire anonymously published the *Sentiment des citoyens*, which revealed to the public that Rousseau had placed his children in the foundling hospital [a known fact]. On the substance, the irony is complete. Voltaire, who wanted God invented to restrain the wicked and the tyrant, and Rousseau, who wanted a catechism of the citizen sanctioned by death, both want a social sacred, and differ only in tone. Voltaire's line and Rousseau's chapter are the two faces of the same refoundation.

History took care of the implementation, and the confrontation, though constructed, here touches on fact. Robespierre, who venerated Rousseau, had the Convention vote, on 18 Floréal Year II (7 May 1794), a decree by which the French people recognized the existence of the Supreme Being and the immortality of the soul, and instituted festivals every tenth day; the Festival of the Supreme Being was celebrated on 20 Prairial (8 June 1794), a few days before the law of 22 Prairial that accelerated the Terror. I do not believe Rousseau can be held responsible for what others made of him. But one cannot say that the cult of the Supreme Being betrayed the chapter on civil religion: it reproduces its positive dogmas, and the death penalty for civic hypocrisy was not Robespierre's invention.

Where to place Rousseau? I believe the two texts must be held together, and that one must refuse to choose the “good” Rousseau against the “bad” one. The Savoyard vicar is an admirable exit from the Church: a felt God, without priests or miracles, a morality of conscience, a peaceful scepticism about everything else. The legislator of *The Social Contract* is an unscrupulous refounder, who knows what he is doing and says so. The link between the two is rigorous: because the religion of man is purely inward, it can found no city; and because no city holds together without the sacred, one must manufacture a sacred that is no longer the priests'. Rousseau saw before Durkheim that society needs to sacralize itself. He was not content to see it, he willed it, and that is why he is an instructive counter-example: a man who leaves the Church through the heart and re-enters religion through the city.

> **En littérature.** Victor Hugo, *Ninety-Three* (1874) ; Anatole France, *Les dieux ont soif* (1912)
>
> In *Ninety-Three*, Cimourdain, a former priest turned delegate of the Committee of Public Safety, explains to his pupil Gauvain, on the eve of having him guillotined, what the Republic must be: “le droit et le devoir parallèles, l'impôt proportionnel et progressif, le service militaire obligatoire, le nivellement, aucune déviation, et, au-dessus de tous et de tout, cette ligne droite, la loi. La république de l'absolu.” Right and duty side by side, proportional and progressive taxation, compulsory military service, levelling, no deviation, and, above all men and all things, that straight line, the law. The republic of the absolute. Gauvain replies that he prefers “la république de l'idéal”, the republic of the ideal: “Au-dessus de la balance il y a la lyre”, above the scales there is the lyre. At the moment Gauvain's head falls, Cimourdain shoots himself through the heart.
>
> Why here: Cimourdain is the civil religion of *The Social Contract* made man. He left the Church for the city, and the city has become for him an absolute that demands the sacrifice of the only being he loves, because sparing him would be sacrilege. That he dies at the same stroke changes nothing in the criterion: it is not self-offering that refounds, it is the authority in whose name the offering of others is demanded.
>
> In Anatole France, the painter Évariste Gamelin, a juror of the Revolutionary Tribunal, professes the faith that Rousseau prescribed for the citizen: “La croyance en un Dieu bon est nécessaire à la morale. L'Être suprême est la source de toutes les vertus, et l'on n'est pas républicain si l'on ne croit en Dieu”, belief in a good God is necessary to morality. The Supreme Being is the source of all virtues, and one is not a republican if one does not believe in God. The atheist Brotteaux, whom the Tribunal will send to the guillotine, had just answered him that “l'humanité copie ses dieux sur ses tyrans”, humanity models its gods on its tyrants: this is Rousseau's single negative dogma, intolerance, applied to the letter.
>
> Sources: Victor Hugo, *Quatrevingt-treize*, part three, book VII, ch. 5 and 6 (1874) ; Anatole France, *Les dieux ont soif*, ch. VI (1912).
