### Rudolf Otto (1869-1937): the numinous and the wholly other

Rudolf Otto's *Das Heilige* (*The Idea of the Holy*) appeared in 1917, the same year as Weber's lecture on science. The two texts answer each other without knowing it: Weber records disenchantment, Otto describes what, in the sacred, resists all rationalization. The book's subtitle, *Über das Irrationale in der Idee des Göttlichen und sein Verhältnis zum Rationalen*, on the irrational in the idea of the divine and its relation to the rational, says what matters.

Otto, a Lutheran theologian at Marburg, begins with an operation on vocabulary. The word “heilig”, holy, has become a moral term in German: the holy is the perfectly good. Yet this moral sense overlays an older one, which religious languages preserve and which must be isolated. For this Otto coins a word: “Ich bilde hierfür zunächst das Wort: das Numinöse, (wenn man von omen ominös bilden kann, dann auch von numen numinös)”, for this I first form the word: the numinous (if one can form ominös from omen, one can also form numinös from numen). And he adds that since this category is “vollkommen sui generis”, entirely sui generis, it cannot be defined in the strict sense, but only “erörterbar”, open to being discussed and suggested.

Hence a singular method, which makes *Das Heilige* a book of spirituality as much as of the history of religions. At the beginning of the third chapter, Otto invites his reader to recall a moment of strong religious emotion, and warns: “Wer das nicht kann oder solche Momente überhaupt nicht hat, ist gebeten, nicht weiter zu lesen”, whoever cannot do so, or has never had such moments at all, is asked to read no further. The sentence, famous and often mocked, is nonetheless consistent with the thesis: if the numinous is a primary datum, one can speak of it only to someone who has felt it, as one cannot speak of colours to the blind. The consequence is considerable. If Otto is right, every critique of religion that treats it as a theory or a function misses its object: the critic who has never felt the numinous does not know what he is talking about.

The numinous is described through its effects on the soul. Otto reduces them to a formula that has become classic, “das mysterium tremendum”, the mystery that makes one tremble, to which is added the “fascinans”, what attracts and enraptures. The divine is at once terrifying and fascinating; it repels and it attracts. And above all it is the “ganz Andere”, the wholly other, something that does not belong to the circle of our reality. The numinous is not a being greater than the others; it is of another order.

Otto answers Schleiermacher at the centre of chapter 3, and the response is attested. Otto acknowledges his predecessor's discovery: “Ein sehr bemerkenswertes Element solchen Erlebnisses hat Schleiermacher glücklich herausgegriffen: das Gefühl der „Abhängigkeit"”, Schleiermacher happily singled out a very remarkable element of such experience: the feeling of “dependence”. But he levels two reproaches at him. The first is that this feeling, as Schleiermacher describes it, is not sufficiently distinguished from other feelings of natural dependence, of powerlessness or inadequacy. Otto therefore proposes another name: “Ich suche nach einem Namen für die Sache und nenne es Kreaturgefühl, das Gefühl der Kreatur, die in ihrem eigenen Nichts versinkt und vergeht gegenüber dem, was über aller Kreatur ist”, I look for a name for the thing and call it creature-feeling, the feeling of the creature that sinks and vanishes into its own nothingness before that which is above all creatures. The second reproach goes deeper: Schleiermacher started from the feeling of self and inferred God from it, as a cause; Otto holds that the feeling of dependence is a secondary effect, which presupposes the primary experience of an external object. “Das Gefühl einer „schlechthinnigen Abhängigkeit" meiner hat zur Voraussetzung ein Gefühl einer „schlechthinnigen Unnahbarkeit" seiner”, the feeling of an “absolute dependence” of myself presupposes a feeling of an “absolute unapproachability” of him.

The correction reaches far. Schleiermacher, by grounding religion in the feeling of the subject, had unwittingly opened the way to Feuerbach: if religion is a feeling of man, it can be traced back to man. Otto closes this way. Religious feeling is not a feeling of self; it is the response to an object that is not the self. The Feuerbachian critique of projection is thus circumvented, not by an argument but by a description: the numinous gives itself as what precedes the subject. I am not entirely convinced; but the question of leaving religion changes its nature. If the numinous is an a priori category of the mind, as the chapters that the book's table of contents entitles “Das Heilige als Kategorie a priori” assert, then one cannot leave it, any more than one can leave the category of causality. One can only let it lie fallow or cultivate it.

Otto also answers, in an attested way, William James, whom he cites several times. He borrows testimonies of religious experience from James's book, but reproaches him for having collected them “ohne selber auf das „Irrationale", das in ihnen zittert, acht zu haben”, without himself paying attention to the “irrational” that trembles in them. James, a pragmatist, judges experience by its fruits; Otto, a phenomenologist before the term, wants to grasp it in its own content. And he targets Kant and religious humanism when he evokes, with visible irony, those who seek to build themselves a “Religion innerhalb der Grenzen der reinen Vernunft” or of “Humanität”. Kant's title and the humanism of *Humanität*, in which one may also hear Comte's religion of Humanity, which Otto does not name, are dismissed together to the shelf of religions without the numinous, that is, for Otto, of religions that are not religions.

Otto's book is one of the strongest defences of the irreducibility of the religious that the twentieth century produced, and it is also, paradoxically, one of the main sources of spirituality without a Church. For if the numinous is a universal experience, prior to dogmas, it can be felt outside any confession, in nature, art, music, solitude. Otto did not want this, since he thought Christianity the highest form of the sacred; but his category escaped his theology. It nourished Eliade as much as contemporary spiritualities, and one may wonder whether Romain Rolland's “oceanic feeling”, to which Freud would reply, is not a variant of it. I believe so: it is a numinous without tremendum, a pure fascinans.

### Freud (1856-1939): illusion and the oceanic feeling

*Die Zukunft einer Illusion* (*The Future of an Illusion*) appeared in 1927. It is the text that fulfils, in our series, the regime of illusion, and its concept must first be made precise, for Freud is subtler than his readers. An illusion is not an error. “Eine Illusion ist nicht dasselbe wie ein Irrtum, sie ist auch nicht notwendig ein Irrtum”, an illusion is not the same thing as an error, nor is it necessarily an error. What defines illusion is its motivation: “Wir heißen also einen Glauben eine Illusion, wenn sich in seiner Motivierung die Wunscherfüllung vordrängt, und sehen dabei von seinem Verhältnis zur Wirklichkeit ab”, we therefore call a belief an illusion when wish-fulfilment comes to the fore in its motivation, and in doing so we disregard its relation to reality.

The distinction answers, without naming it, the reproach James addressed to medical materialism. Freud does not claim to have shown that God does not exist; he shows that belief in God would be held even if it were false, because it answers a wish. This is a new regime in our series, distinct from Spinozist error and Feuerbachian projection: religion is not false knowledge, it is a belief whose strength does not come from knowledge.

What wish? That of the child in distress. Freud reconstructs the genesis: the adult, facing the overwhelming forces of nature and fate, finds himself again in a situation he has already known, that of the child before his parents, and above all before the father, “dessen Schutzes man aber auch sicher war”, of whose protection one was also sure. The man who discovers that he will always remain a child before the world “verleiht diesen die Züge der Vatergestalt, er schafft sich die Götter”, lends these powers the features of the father figure, he creates gods for himself. *Das Unbehagen in der Kultur* would restate the thesis in one sentence: “Ein ähnlich starkes Bedürfnis aus der Kindheit wie das nach dem Vaterschutz wüßte ich nicht anzugeben”, I could not point to any need arising from childhood as strong as the need for a father's protection. Religion then becomes, in a formula that caused a scandal, “die allgemein menschliche Zwangsneurose”, the universal obsessional neurosis of humanity, arising, like the child's, from the Oedipus complex.

Like Feuerbach, Freud makes God a human production; but where Feuerbach saw in it the projected essence of man, to be reappropriated, Freud sees a remnant of childhood, to be outgrown. Freud does not cite Feuerbach in these two books; the confrontation, which is constructed, measures a reversal of value: Feuerbach's God was man writ large, Freud's is man writ small.

The objection Freud raises against himself is the classic objection of the defenders of useful religion: can man live without religious consolation? Will the masses, deprived of God, not believe that everything is permitted? Freud answers with a pedagogy. Infantilism exists to be outgrown: “Der Mensch kann nicht ewig Kind bleiben, er muß endlich hinaus ins „feindliche Leben"”, man cannot remain a child for ever, he must at last go out into “hostile life”. And against consolation he sets another power, weaker but more tenacious: “die Stimme des Intellekts ist leise, aber sie ruht nicht, ehe sie sich Gehör geschafft hat”, the voice of the intellect is soft, but it does not rest until it has gained a hearing.

To designate the power that is to replace religion, Freud writes: “Unser Gott Λόγος”, our god Logos; and he specifies in a note that he borrows the divine pair “Λόγος-᾽Ανάγκη”, Logos and Ananke, from the Dutch writer Multatuli. Further on, he concedes: “unser Gott Λόγος ist vielleicht nicht sehr allmächtig, kann nur einen kleinen Teil von dem erfüllen, was seine Vorgänger versprochen haben”, our god Logos is perhaps not very almighty, he can fulfil only a small part of what his predecessors promised. The vocabulary is deliberate, and Freud knows it to be ironic; it is no less symptomatic for that. To say what replaces God, Freud finds no other word than god: a more modest god, a more honest one, but a god all the same, with his “predecessors”. This is the thesis of our essay, pronounced by the man who most resolutely wanted to refute it.

The book ends with a famous sentence, which is at once an act of faith and a profession of humility: “Nein, unsere Wissenschaft ist keine Illusion. Eine Illusion aber wäre es zu glauben, daß wir anderswoher bekommen könnten, was sie uns nicht geben kann”, no, our science is no illusion. But it would be an illusion to believe that we could get elsewhere what it cannot give us. Freud's own criterion can be turned against him. Is this confidence in science not itself a belief in which wish-fulfilment comes to the fore? Freud had asked himself the question in the book, asking whether our conviction that we can know reality through science has “eine bessere Begründung”, a better foundation. He answered yes, because science corrects itself; but Nietzsche, in § 344 of *The Gay Science*, had already shown that this will to truth was a faith: Freud's god Logos is the latest avatar of the God-as-truth of Plato and of Christianity.

The response that matters here came from a friend, not a philosopher. Romain Rolland (1866-1944), who had received *The Future of an Illusion*, answered Freud in a letter of 5 December 1927. He reproached him for not having grasped the true source of religiosity, which according to him was neither dogma nor consolation, but a particular feeling, that of eternity, without perceptible bounds and as it were oceanic. Rolland was then preparing his books on Ramakrishna and Vivekananda, and he spoke as a connoisseur of Indian mysticism. Freud answered publicly, in the first chapter of *Das Unbehagen in der Kultur* (*Civilization and Its Discontents*, 1930). There he summarizes his friend's letter, which describes “ein Gefühl wie von etwas Unbegrenztem, Schrankenlosem, gleichsam „Ozeanischem"”, a feeling as of something unlimited, unbounded, as it were “oceanic”, and which he glosses: “Also ein Gefühl der unauflösbaren Verbundenheit, der Zusammengehörigkeit mit dem Ganzen der Außenwelt”, a feeling, then, of an indissoluble bond, of belonging to the whole of the external world. A note, added later, lifts the anonymity: since the publication of *The Life of Ramakrishna* and *The Life of Vivekananda* (1930), Freud no longer needs to conceal that the friend in question is Romain Rolland.

Then Freud makes an admission of disarming honesty: “Ich selbst kann dies „ozeanische" Gefühl nicht in mir entdecken”, I myself cannot discover this “oceanic” feeling in myself. He does not deny that it exists in others; he explains it by a return to primary narcissism: the infant does not distinguish its ego from the world, and the oceanic feeling would be the survival, in certain adults, of this “primäre Ichgefühl” (I reproduce the inflected form of the text), primary ego-feeling, whose ideational contents would be precisely “die der Unbegrenztheit und der Verbundenheit mit dem All”, those of limitlessness and of a bond with the All. But he refuses to see in it the source of religion. The source remains, for him, infantile helplessness and longing for the father: “Für die religiösen Bedürfnisse scheint mir die Ableitung von der infantilen Hilflosigkeit und der durch sie geweckten Vatersehnsucht unabweisbar”, for religious needs, their derivation from infantile helplessness and the longing for the father it arouses seems to me irrefutable.

With this exchange, the question of religion becomes that of spirituality. Rolland defends a religiosity without a personal God, without dogma, without a Church: a feeling of unity with the Whole, which he holds to be universal and finds as much in Spinoza as in the Indian mystics. Freud opposes to it a religion that is always that of the father, of protection and of guilt. Both are right, and that is why the debate matters. Freud is right about the instituted religions, whose infantile mainspring he describes accurately. Rolland is right to say that there is something else, an experience Spinoza would have recognized. But Freud is perhaps right a second time, and that is his real strength: the oceanic feeling, if it is a remnant of primary narcissism, is still a form of childhood, a way of denying the separation of the ego and the world. Spirituality without God would then be only a religion without a father, but not without a mother.

Freud, moreover, encountered Dostoevsky, which closes a loop. In 1928 he contributed, as a preface to a German edition of the drafts of *The Brothers Karamazov*, an essay, “Dostojewski und die Vatertötung”, Dostoevsky and parricide, in which he reads the novel in the light of the murder of the father. The novel whose centre is a parricide, and whose question is the death of God, is thus read by the man who makes God the figure of the father. Freud saw what Dostoevsky had staged: that the death of God and the death of the father are one and the same affair, and that guilt does not die with them.

### Bergson (1859-1941): static religion and dynamic religion

*The Two Sources of Morality and Religion*, published in 1932, is Bergson's last great book, and probably the work of interwar French philosophy that poses our question most directly. It presents itself as a response both to Durkheim, whom it discusses explicitly, and to James, whom it cites and whose intuition it extends. It attempts to hold the two families together, the sociological and the mystical, by tracing them to two distinct sources.

Bergson begins, in the second chapter, with an observation that could be Spinoza's or Onfray's: “Le spectacle de ce que furent les religions, et de ce que certaines sont encore, est bien humiliant pour l'intelligence humaine. Quel tissu d'aberrations !”, the spectacle of what religions were, and of what some still are, is truly humiliating for human intelligence. What a tissue of aberrations! But he draws from it a question that neither Spinoza nor Onfray asks with the same rigour: how could an intelligent being have believed such things, and why has he never been cured of them? The answer is not ignorance, since humanity clings to them all the more as experience and reasoning contradict them. It is a function. “La vérité est que la religion, étant coextensive à notre espèce, doit tenir à notre structure”, the truth is that religion, being coextensive with our species, must pertain to our structure.

Bergson calls this function fabulatory. Intelligence, which is proper to man, has a dissolving power: it shows the individual that he could prefer his own interest to that of the group, and it also reveals to him that he will die. Nature, which produced intelligence, had to produce a counterweight at the same time. This counterweight is religious fables: “la religion est donc une réaction défensive de la nature contre le pouvoir dissolvant de l'intelligence”, religion is thus a defensive reaction of nature against the dissolving power of intelligence. And the fabulating function did not first serve the novel or the theatre: “la religion était la raison d'être de la fonction fabulatrice”, religion was the raison d'être of the fabulating function. The “static religion” thus produced “attache l'homme à la vie, et par conséquent l'individu à la société, en lui racontant des histoires comparables à celles dont on berce les enfants”, binds man to life, and consequently the individual to society, by telling him stories comparable to those with which children are lulled to sleep.

The kinship with Durkheim is manifest, and Bergson acknowledges it. He cites the Durkheimian thesis that religious representations are the work of the collective mind, and he accepts it in part: “Nous admettrons volontiers, quant à nous, l'existence de représentations collectives, déposées dans les institutions, le langage et les mœurs”, for our part we shall readily admit the existence of collective representations, deposited in institutions, language and customs. But he rejects its consequence. For Durkheim, society is the sole source of the sacred. For Bergson, it is only one source, and the least lofty. Static religion is that of closed societies, which preserve themselves by defending themselves; it is infra-intellectual, natural, and it turns on the spot. The book's first chapter had already contrasted the closed soul, which turns in a circle with its society, and the open soul, whose love extends to the whole of humanity, “aux animaux, aux plantes, à toute la nature”, to animals, to plants, to all of nature. Between the nation and humanity, Bergson writes, lies the whole distance from the finite to the indefinite: no gradual extension of social solidarity leads from one to the other. A leap is needed.

This leap, the mystics accomplished. “Dynamic religion”, inward and supra-intellectual, is born of a few exceptional individuals who regained contact with the creative impetus of life. Bergson defines mysticism precisely: “l'aboutissement du mysticisme est une prise de contact, et par conséquent une coïncidence partielle, avec l'effort créateur que manifeste la vie. Cet effort est de Dieu, si ce n'est pas Dieu lui-même”, the culmination of mysticism is a contact, and consequently a partial coincidence, with the creative effort that life manifests. This effort is of God, if it is not God himself. And what the mystics say, when one tries to put it in a formula, is simple: “Dieu est amour, et il est objet d'amour : tout l'apport du mysticisme est là”, God is love, and he is the object of love: the whole contribution of mysticism lies there. Bergson adds that the mystics unanimously testify “que Dieu a besoin de nous, comme nous avons besoin de Dieu”, that God needs us, as we need God, and he draws from this a vision of creation as “une entreprise de Dieu pour créer des créateurs”, an undertaking of God to create creators.

Bergson's thesis matters because it separates two things that most of our authors confused. There is static religion, produced by society to defend itself; it is fabulating, and it deserves all the criticisms that have been addressed to it, from Spinoza to Freud. And there is mysticism, which is not a religion but the living source of which religions are the cooling. Bergson says it in an image: “Nous nous représentons donc la religion comme la cristallisation, opérée par un refroidissement savant, de ce que le mysticisme vint déposer, brûlant, dans l'âme de l'humanité”, we therefore picture religion as the crystallization, brought about by a skilful cooling, of what mysticism deposited, burning hot, in the soul of humanity. One can then leave static religion, and one must, without leaving mysticism. Leaving religion does not call for another religion; it calls for a return to the source.

Bergson here answers, in a constructed way, all the authors of our series. To Durkheim he answers that society explains only static religion, and does not account for the love of humanity. To Freud, whom he does not cite but knows, he answers that the fabulating function is indeed a defence, but does not exhaust the religious. To Nietzsche he answers that the “overman” already exists, and that his name is the mystic: Bergson cites the sages of Greece, the prophets of Israel, the Arahants of Buddhism and the saints of Christianity as the exceptional men in whom open morality has been embodied. To William James, finally, he pays explicit tribute, quoting at length his testimony on the San Francisco earthquake, and he extends his intuition by giving the “wider self” a name, the creative effort of life.

The last pages carry the question onto the terrain of technical civilization, with a strange topicality. Bergson asserts there that “la mystique appelle la mécanique”, mysticism calls for mechanics, because man will detach himself from matter only by leaning on it; but he adds that the converse is true: “le corps agrandi attend un supplément d'âme, et que la mécanique exigerait une mystique”, the enlarged body awaits a supplement of soul, and mechanics would require a mysticism. The book ends with this sentence: “L'humanité gémit, à demi écrasée sous le poids des progrès qu'elle a faits. Elle ne sait pas assez que son avenir dépend d'elle”, humanity groans, half crushed beneath the weight of the progress it has made. It does not know well enough that its future depends on itself; and it is for humanity to decide whether it wants to make the effort required “pour que s'accomplisse, jusque sur notre planète réfractaire, la fonction essentielle de l'univers, qui est une machine à faire des dieux”, so that the essential function of the universe, which is a machine for making gods, may be fulfilled, even on our refractory planet.

This ambiguous sentence sums up, in my view, the whole difficulty of this part. A machine for making gods: Bergson means that the universe tends to produce beings capable of creation and love, mystics, divine men. But one can also hear the echo of the question of Nietzsche's madman, “must we not ourselves become gods?”, and the echo of Kirillov. Bergson believes he escapes the divinization of man because the god that is made in us is the creative impetus and not the self. I believe he does escape it, but narrowly, and at the price of a metaphysics of life that few readers today are prepared to follow. The fact remains that Bergson gives systematic form to the distinction on which the rest of this essay will live: closed religion, which one can and must leave, and open spirituality, which one does not leave because it is not an institution but a movement.
