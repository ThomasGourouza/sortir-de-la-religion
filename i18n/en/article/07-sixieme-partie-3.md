### Auguste Comte (1798-1857): the proof by fact

If every exit from religion tends to refound one, there must be a case in which refoundation is not a hidden symptom but an avowed programme. That case exists. Auguste Comte, founder of positivism, the man who had decreed the end of the theological age and of the metaphysical age, founded a religion.

The starting doctrine is well known. The *Cours de philosophie positive* (1830-1842) sets out the law of the three states: the human mind passes through the theological state, in which it explains phenomena by supernatural wills, then through the metaphysical state, in which it explains them by abstract entities, and finally through the positive state, in which it gives up the search for first causes and confines itself to discovering laws; each branch of our knowledge, says the first lesson, “passe successivement par trois états théoriques différents : l'état théologique, ou fictif ; l'état métaphysique, ou abstrait ; l'état scientifique, ou positif”, passes successively through three different theoretical states: the theological or fictitious state, the metaphysical or abstract state, the scientific or positive state. Religion therefore belongs to the childhood of humanity, and one would expect from Comte a pure and simple exit from religion.

Yet from 1845 onwards, after his meeting with Clotilde de Vaux (October 1844), who died in April 1846, Comte carried out what he himself called a subjective turn, and he founded the Religion of Humanity. The *Système de politique positive* (1851-1854) gives its doctrine, the *Catéchisme positiviste* (1852) its popular exposition, in the form of a dialogue between a priest of Humanity and a woman. The text, perfectly clear, carries out point by point what Feuerbach had announced and what Stirner had denounced.

Religion, first of all, is redefined without God: “La religion consiste donc à régler chaque nature individuelle et à rallier toutes les individualités”, religion therefore consists in regulating each individual nature and in rallying all individualities. We recognize the double etymology examined in the prologue in connection with Cicero and Lactantius: to regulate and to bind. Religion is no longer a relation to a transcendent being, but the function that unifies the individual and society, and one is needed, since this function is permanent. Comte quotes on this point a maxim that sums up what is at stake: “On ne détruit que ce qu'on remplace”, one destroys only what one replaces.

The object of this religion is then named. Comte condenses the whole of positive conceptions “dans la seule notion d'un être immense et éternel, l'Humanité”, in the single notion of an immense and eternal being, Humanity. He goes on: “Autour de ce vrai Grand-Être, moteur immédiat de chaque existence individuelle ou collective, nos affections se concentrent aussi spontanément que nos pensées et nos actions. Sa seule idée inspire directement la formule sacrée du positivisme : L'Amour pour principe, l'Ordre pour base, et le Progrès pour but”, around this true Great Being, immediate mover of every individual or collective existence, our affections gather as spontaneously as our thoughts and our actions; its mere idea directly inspires the sacred formula of positivism: Love as principle, Order as basis, and Progress as end. Note the expression “formule sacrée”, sacred formula: Comte founds a sacred, and makes no secret of it. The Great Being is Humanity conceived as the whole of past, future and present beings who freely contribute to perfecting the universal order, and it is composed first of all of the dead: “Les vivants sont toujours, et de plus en plus, gouvernés nécessairement par les morts : telle est la loi fondamentale de l'ordre humain”, the living are always, and ever more, necessarily governed by the dead: such is the fundamental law of the human order.

Religion, finally, has all its organs: a dogma, positive philosophy; a cult, with its social sacraments that punctuate life from presentation to posthumous incorporation; a calendar, in which the months and days bear the names of the great men of humanity; a priesthood, made up of scientists, which exercises spiritual power; a private cult, in which woman is the guardian angel, and in which Comte devotes an intimate cult to Clotilde. Dogma, cult and regime, Comte writes, form the whole that religion must direct. Morality is condensed into a formula that would become the positivist motto: “vivre pour autrui”, to live for others. For the occasion Comte coins the word “altruisme”, altruism.

One passage of the *Catéchisme* deserves attention. The priest's interlocutor, the woman, accepts the conception of the Great Being, but she confesses a worry: “je m'effraye de ma nullité personnelle envers une telle existence, dont l'immensité m'efface davantage que ne le fit jadis la majesté d'un Dieu avec lequel, quoique chétive, je me sentais une relation propre et directe”, I am frightened by my personal nullity before such an existence, whose immensity effaces me more than did once the majesty of a God with whom, puny as I was, I felt a relation of my own, a direct one. Stirner's objection is formulated in the very text of the new religion, by its own catechumen: the Great Being crushes the individual more than the old God did, who had a personal relation with each soul, whereas Humanity has one only with the species. Comte answers it with the cult of the dead and with the affection of women; but he saw the objection.

Comte founds his Church and proclaims himself High Priest of Humanity. After his death his disciples open positivist temples: some survive in Brazil, notably the Capela Positivista in Porto Alegre, and the Temple of Humanity in Paris, rue Payenne, set up in 1903 by South American positivists, has been listed as a historic monument since 1982. The motto of the Brazilian flag, *Ordem e Progresso*, comes from the sacred formula of positivism.

Let us set Comte against Engels's historical argument (a constructed comparison: Engels is not aiming at Comte at this point). From those years 1793-1798 in which, according to Engels, France lived without Christianity and without any need for a substitute, Comte, born in 1798 and raised under the Empire, draws the opposite conclusion. He has before his eyes the revolutionary attempts at replacement: the cult of Reason in 1793, Robespierre's cult of the Supreme Being in 1794, theophilanthropy under the Directory. Their failure does not prove to him that the need does not exist, but that these cults were still metaphysical, that is to say abstract, and that a cult founded on science was required. In the same years the two men read two contrary truths: for Engels, religion can disappear without leaving a void; for Comte, the void is so real that it must be organized. The twentieth century, with its political religions, would rather prove Comte right on the diagnosis, and Engels right on the danger of wanting to fill this void by decree.

One may smile at this religion. Mill, who in 1844 had found English patrons for Comte, Grote, Molesworth and Raikes Currie, turned away from it in embarrassment and, in *Auguste Comte and Positivism* (1865), while judging him justified in wanting to make his philosophy a religion, reproached him with a complete error from the very starting point. But it would be wrong to see in it nothing more than an oddity of old age. On this point Comte is an author consistent to the end: he saw that religion fulfils a social and moral function that does not disappear with belief in God, and will therefore be fulfilled in one way or another. He preferred that it be fulfilled by a rational religion, conscious of itself, rather than by hidden idols. This is Durkheim's analysis sixty years later, except that Durkheim confines himself to describing. Comte proves by fact what Stirner asserted by argument: atheist humanism, if it goes all the way, becomes a cult. He also proves, through the reaction of his contemporaries, that this cult does not take: the Religion of Humanity remained a sect. One does not found a religion by decree, even a rational one; and what Comte lacks is precisely what Schleiermacher placed at the heart of religion, the lived experience of the infinite, which no catechism can produce.

This is the subjective immortality of the *Catéchisme positiviste*, the dead living in the living, and the poem was sung in the English positivist churches; it shows what is purest in a religion of Humanity, and that it still needs a heaven, if only to deny it.

### Arthur Schopenhauer (1788-1860): a mysticism without God

Schopenhauer belongs to Hegel's generation, not to that of the Young Hegelians; his great book, *Die Welt als Wille und Vorstellung* (*The World as Will and Representation*), appeared at the end of 1818 dated 1819, and went almost unnoticed. Fame came only with the *Parerga und Paralipomena* (1851), at the moment when the failure of 1848 had ruined Hegelian and revolutionary hopes. I place him here because his reception is contemporary with the crisis of the Hegelian left, and because, at the moment when Comte was founding a religion without God turned towards action and progress, he offers a spirituality without God turned towards renunciation.

Schopenhauer's metaphysics is well known: the world is representation for the subject who knows it, but in itself it is will, a blind will, without end and without reason, which objectifies itself in all beings and devours itself. Life is therefore essentially suffering, and the world is not the work of a good being, but the expression of an absurd will to live, without God or providence.

And yet the fourth book of *The World* culminates in a doctrine of salvation. There are two possible attitudes before the knowledge of the essence of the world: the *Bejahung*, the affirmation of the will to live, and the *Verneinung des Willens zum Leben*, the denial of the will to live. Denial occurs when knowledge of universal suffering becomes a “quietive” of the will: the subject ceases to will, and in this renunciation finds peace. Schopenhauer calls this resignation, “welche das letzte Ziel, ja, das innerste Wesen aller Tugend und Heiligkeit, und die Erlösung von der Welt ist”, which is the final goal, indeed the innermost essence of all virtue and holiness, and redemption from the world.

Paradox: an atheist speaks of holiness and redemption, and not metaphorically. Schopenhauer recognizes in the Christian saints, the Hindu ascetics and the Buddhists the witnesses of one and the same experience, the denial of the will to live, and he makes the mystics the true practical philosophers. Christianity, he writes, developed its ascetic germ “in den Schriften der Christlichen Heiligen und Mystiker”, in the writings of the Christian saints and mystics, and nowhere is the spirit of Christianity expressed as perfectly as in the German mystics. And he names “ein noch größerer Mystiker, Meister Eckhard”, a still greater mystic, Meister Eckhart, whose writings had just been made accessible by Franz Pfeiffer's edition (1857). Here we meet again the Eckhart of Part One.

The book ends on a famous sentence. For those in whom the will has turned and denied itself, “diese unsere so sehr reale Welt mit allen ihren Sonnen und Milchstraßen” is “Nichts”, this world of ours, so very real, with all its suns and milky ways, is nothing. This nothing is not Stirner's, the creative nothing of an Ego that consumes everything; it is the nothing of the extinction of the Ego. Two atheists of the same decade arrive at two opposite nothings.

On positive religion Schopenhauer holds a subtle position, which he sets out in a dialogue in the *Parerga*, chapter XV of the second volume, “Über Religion”, between Demopheles, the friend of the people, and Philalethes, the friend of truth. I have been able to consult only T. Bailey Saunders's English translation. Demopheles maintains that religion is necessary for the people, who cannot be philosophers: “Religion is the metaphysics of the masses; by all means let them keep it”. Humanity needs an interpretation of life adapted to popular understanding; religion is therefore “an allegorical investiture of the truth”, which, as a rule of action and as a consolation in suffering and death, perhaps accomplishes as much as truth itself could if we possessed it. Philalethes protests in the name of truth, and the dialogue ends without a conclusion.

This dialogue is a centrepiece of our debate. Demopheles is already the Grand Inquisitor without the tragedy: truth is for the few, religion for the many, and it would be cruel to take their allegory away from the people. But Schopenhauer, unlike Hegel, is not sure that the concept does better than the allegory, and, unlike the Inquisitor, he leaves the last word to the friend of truth without proving him right. This dialogue without a conclusion seems to me more honest than most treatises: it shows that the question of religion for others has no theoretical solution.

Nietzsche was Schopenhauer's pupil before becoming his adversary, and the response is attested. In 1874, in the third *Unzeitgemäße Betrachtung*, *Schopenhauer als Erzieher* (*Schopenhauer as Educator*), he writes: “Ich gehöre zu den Lesern Schopenhauers, welche, nachdem sie die erste Seite von ihm gelesen haben, mit Bestimmtheit wissen, daß sie alle Seiten lesen und auf jedes Wort hören werden”, I am one of those readers of Schopenhauer who, after reading his first page, know with certainty that they will read every page and listen to every word. The third essay of *On the Genealogy of Morality* (1887) would deal him the psychological blow, as we shall see in Part Seven: the great denier of the will lived on his hatred, that is to say on his will, and his mysticism without God was a disguised affirmation.

The objection is only partly right. Schopenhauer really did make the denial of life a supreme value, and Nietzsche is right to see in it the deepest Christian inheritance, that of asceticism. In the terms of our question, Schopenhauer left God without leaving the religion of salvation: he keeps holiness, redemption, renunciation, and explicitly so. But Nietzsche does not see, or does not want to see, what is new in Schopenhauer: he is the first modern philosopher to have taken the spiritualities of India seriously and to have read the Christian mystics outside theology. He opened the way to the comparative spiritualities of the twentieth century, from William James to Aldous Huxley, and posed with unprecedented rigour the question that will occupy Part Twelve: can a spirituality exist without God, without dogma and without a Church, as a pure relation to oneself and to the world? Schopenhauer answers yes, but his spirituality is one of extinction, and it is against it that Nietzsche will seek a spirituality of affirmation.

### Transition: from treatise to novel

The landscape is complete. Religion has been criticized as projection (Feuerbach), as spectre (Stirner), as symptom (Marx), as imposture (the Enlightenment tradition, which Strauss and Engels reject), as betrayal of faith (Kierkegaard), as a social function to be fulfilled otherwise (Comte), as allegory for the people (Schopenhauer). And each in turn has been accused of refounding what it criticized: Feuerbach sacralizes human relations, Stirner installs the Ego in the place of God, Marx keeps the form of salvation, Comte founds a Church, Schopenhauer keeps holiness.

Yet this picture lacks a dimension that treatises cannot give, that of the person who lives these positions: what becomes of a man who believes that God does not exist and draws the consequences, and what is the atheism of Feuerbach or of Stirner, no longer as a thesis but as an existence? This is the question asked by Dostoevsky, who in his youth knew Belinsky's circle, where Feuerbach and Strauss were read with fervour, and who puts them to the test in characters. It is also the question taken up by Nietzsche, the pupil turned adversary of Schopenhauer, who announces the death of God without promising Hegel's resurrection. Part Seven brings them together, because both understood before anyone else that the exit from religion is not a matter of theory, but an event that engages one's whole life.
