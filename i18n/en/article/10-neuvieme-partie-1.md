## Part Nine: why do people believe? Need, function, cognition

Until now, the essay has asked how one leaves religion, almost never why one enters it; and yet this is the question the reader asks first: why does the human being need religion, or, when he no longer wants it, spirituality? The word “need” already contains the answer. To speak of need is to suppose a constant lack, inscribed in human nature, which religion fills and which would come back to claim its due if religion were abolished: that is Durkheim's thesis, and it is what the thesis of this essay calls into question. I shall therefore speak rather of a **disposition**: something in the mind, in existence and in common life makes religious belief easy, frequent, sometimes almost irresistible, and the task is to take its springs apart without deciding in advance that they call for a god. The philosophers we have met drew a first map of them: fear, desire, lament, helplessness, society, the fabulating function, the need for meaning. Two formulas set the tone. Feuerbach: “Der Wunsch ist der Ursprung, ist das Wesen selbst der Religion”, the wish is the origin, it is the very essence of religion. Freud: religious ideas were born “aus dem Bedürfnis, die menschliche Hilflosigkeit erträglich zu machen”, from the need to make human helplessness bearable. The human sciences of the twentieth century put the question again with fieldwork, experiments and figures: Tylor and Frazer looked for the origin of religion in an error of reasoning, Malinowski in anxiety before what one does not master, Jung in a natural function of the psyche, Girard in violence and sacrifice; then will come the psychology of death, the sociology of meaning and of memory, the cognitive sciences and the comparative surveys, which a constructed debate will bring face to face.

### Tylor (1832-1917) and Frazer (1854-1941): religion as primitive philosophy

The anthropology of religion was born in England, in the last third of the nineteenth century, from an ambition that resembles that of the Scottish Enlightenment: to make belief an object of natural history, going back from its most complex forms to its simplest. Edward Burnett Tylor, a Quaker by upbringing, without a university degree, a traveller in Mexico and then the first holder of a chair of anthropology at Oxford, published in 1871 the two volumes of *Primitive Culture*. The book bears as its epigraph a French sentence by Président de Brosses, the inventor of the word “fetishism”: “ce n'est pas dans les possibilités, c'est dans l'homme même qu'il faut étudier l'homme”, it is not in possibilities, it is in man himself that man must be studied. A whole programme: not to speculate on what man ought to have believed, but to look at what he believes.

Tylor starts from a difficulty of definition, and his solution would weigh on the whole of the following century. To require, before speaking of religion, belief in a supreme god, in a judgement after death, in idols or sacrifices, is to exclude many peoples from it and to confuse it with some of its developments. One must therefore, says Tylor, posit “as a minimum definition of Religion, the belief in Spiritual Beings”. This belief he calls **animism**, and he reconstructs its genesis by a line of reasoning that Durkheim would summarize. The man of the origins dreams; at night he sees himself travelling far away, conversing with the absent or the dead; he concludes that there is in him a double, a soul, capable of leaving the body. From the human soul one passes to the souls of animals and things, from these to spirits, from spirits to gods. Religion is first of all a philosophy: a theory, false but rational, worked out by a savage philosopher to explain sleep, dreams, sickness and death.

Tylor accepts both consequences of this thesis. The first is that there is no people without religion, if one adopts the minimum definition: belief in spirits is universal, which makes religion a trait of humanity and not an invention of priests. The second is that this primitive philosophy is an error, and that its remains live on in modern societies in the form of “survivals” which must be identified and rooted out. Tylor says so bluntly on the last page of his book: ethnography has the office, harsher and at times painful, of exposing “the remains of crude old culture which have passed into harmful superstition” and marking them out for destruction; that is why “the science of culture is essentially a reformer's science”. One recognizes the first regime of this essay, the regime of error, in its evolutionist version: religion is true as a universal fact, false as a theory, and progress dissolves it.

James George Frazer, more than twenty years younger, gave this school its monument and its public. *The Golden Bough* appeared in two volumes in 1890 and swelled to twelve in the third edition (1906-1915), before the abridgement of 1922 that would make it a bedside book for generations of writers. Frazer starts there from a riddle of detail, the priest of Diana at Nemi who attains his office only by killing his predecessor, and draws from it a fresco of human thought in three ages. In the first, man believes he can act on nature through magic, which rests on two laws of the association of ideas, similarity and contiguity. Frazer gives its most famous formula: legitimately applied, these laws produce science; illegitimately applied, they produce magic, “the bastard sister of science”. In the second age, man discovers that nature does not obey him, and he turns to powers that must be prayed to. Frazer then defines religion as “a propitiation or conciliation of powers superior to man which are believed to direct and control the course of nature and of human life”. In the third age, science recovers, against religion, the magical idea of a regular order of nature, but this time founded on observation. The book closes on this movement of thought, which goes, “on the whole”, “from magic through religion to science”.

Two features concern this essay directly. Frazer is, first, cautious about the last word. The laws of nature are themselves only hypotheses, and “in the last analysis magic, religion, and science are nothing but theories of thought”, so that science may one day be superseded by a more perfect hypothesis. Next, and above all, *The Golden Bough* is an immense repertory of sacrificed kings, of gods who die and are reborn, and of public scapegoats, human or animal, laden with the ills of the community before being driven out or killed. Frazer does not say in so many words that the Passion of Christ belongs to this series, but the whole book suggests it, and his readers understood it so: it is one of the sources of the mythicist thesis examined in connection with the birth of Christianity. Girard would take up this repertory and reverse its meaning.

This anthropology was refuted from three sides, and each refutation opens a path that what follows will take. Durkheim, first, sets against it an argument that I believe is decisive against intellectualism. If the animist theory were true, he writes, one would have to admit that religious beliefs are “représentations hallucinatoires, sans aucun fondement objectif”, hallucinatory representations without any objective foundation, born of “une sorte de délire qui le saisit régulièrement chaque jour”, a sort of delirium that seizes him regularly every day, and one could not understand how such an aberration has run through the whole of human history. An institution so universal and so lasting cannot rest on a mere error; it must answer to something real, which Durkheim finds in society. Wittgenstein, next, reproaches Frazer for making his “savages” into bad physicists (Part Ten). Malinowski, finally, would reproach the two Englishmen for having studied religion in their libraries, and would show in the field that magic is not a false science but a technique for dealing with anxiety.

Tylor and Frazer's answer is the simplest: people believe because they are mistaken, and they will stop believing when they know. It is the answer of the Enlightenment carried into ethnography, with the same weakness as in d'Holbach: it explains why a belief is born, not why it lasts when one knows it to be false. Tylor nevertheless left a gain that no one has undone: belief in invisible beings is universal. The cognitive sciences, at the end of the twentieth century, would take up his question almost in his own terms, asking no longer whether this belief is true, but why a human mind produces it so easily.

### Malinowski (1884-1942): magic begins where mastery ends

Bronisław Malinowski, born in Kraków, trained in physics and philosophy before coming to anthropology through reading *The Golden Bough*, spent most of the years 1915-1918 in the Trobriand Islands, off New Guinea, living among those he studied. On this experience he founded the method of participant observation, and from it he drew, on our question, a short essay, “Magic, Science and Religion”, which appeared in 1925 in a collective volume edited by Joseph Needham, *Science, Religion and Reality*.

Malinowski first ruins Frazer's thesis with a fact. The Trobrianders do not confuse magic with technique. They know perfectly well that a garden grows because it has been dug, weeded and fenced, and they do all this with the care of good farmers; magic is added to work, it does not replace it. But it is not added everywhere, and here observation becomes experiment, in the example that every textbook has cited since. In the villages on the inner lagoon, fishing is done by poisoning, in an easy and perfectly reliable way; on the shores of the open sea, it is dangerous, and its yield depends on the uncertain passage of shoals of fish. Now, “in the Lagoon fishing, where man can rely completely upon his knowledge and skill, magic does not exist, while in the open-sea fishing, full of danger and uncertainty, there is extensive magical ritual to secure safety and good results”.

The conclusion shifts the whole question. Magic is not an erroneous science, born of a badly conducted association of ideas; it is man's answer to the zones where his knowledge no longer suffices, where chance, danger and death decide. Its function, writes Malinowski, is “to ritualise man's optimism, to enhance his faith in the victory of hope over fear”. We find again the fear and hope that Hume placed at the origin of religion, but their sign is reversed. For Hume, fear produced terrible gods, and religion was a sickness of the mind; for Malinowski, the rite produces confidence, and this confidence has a vital value. The sailor who recites his formula before leaving the channel is not mistaken about the sea; he is giving himself the courage to go out on it.

Religion, which Malinowski distinguishes from magic, is born of the most extreme of these zones. “Of all sources of religion, the supreme and final crisis of life, death, is of the greatest importance”. Before the death of someone close, man is torn between attachment and terror, the wish to keep the dead and the horror of the corpse; funeral rites give form to this ambivalence, and belief in immortality chooses, between the two answers suggested by the instinct of self-preservation, the answer of hope. “Religion saves man from a surrender to death and destruction”. And Malinowski concludes against Tylor: the true kernel of animism is not the observation of dreams, it is “the deepest emotional fact of human nature, the desire for life”. The soul is not a savage philosopher's hypothesis; it is a refusal.

Malinowski finally opposes Durkheim, and his criticism is all the more interesting in that he does him justice. He rejects the idea of a “collective soul” and maintains that, in the societies he observed, religion arises to a large extent from purely individual sources, that crowds do not always produce religion, and that their effervescence is often entirely profane. He acknowledges, however, that Durkheim's school asked the right questions: why are religious acts almost always collective, and why are beliefs obligatory for all members of the tribe? His answer: religion, born of individual anxiety, is taken up by the group because the group, too, needs to survive the death of its members: funerals are the moment when society reconstitutes itself around a loss. We thus have, in 1925, a psychology and a sociology of religion reconciled around death, which announces Ernest Becker's psychology of death.

A year later, in *Myth in Primitive Psychology* (1926), Malinowski extended the same analysis to myth. Myth, he says in substance, is not among the Trobrianders an explanation of natural phenomena, as the mythologists of the nineteenth century believed, nor a learned allegory; it is a “charter”, a narrative that founds and guarantees a present order, a prerogative, a rite, a rule of marriage or of property. Myth does not answer curiosity, it answers contestation: it says why things must be as they are. Religion therefore answers not only the anxiety of the individual before chance and death; it also answers a group's need to justify its order, which joins by another route the civil religion of Plato and of Rousseau, and announces the sociology of Peter Berger.

Malinowski's position seems to me solid, and work he could not have known has confirmed it. It explains what neither Tylor nor Frazer explained: why belief does not retreat as knowledge advances, but only as uncertainty retreats. It makes religion not an error but an answer to a situation, and this answer varies with the situation. This is the hypothesis that Pippa Norris and Ronald Inglehart would put to the test on a world scale. But it leaves open the question that concerns this essay: if religion is the ritualized optimism of those who face danger, does it still have a function when danger is mastered, and what then remains, if not death itself, which nothing masters?

### Jung (1875-1961): the religious function of the psyche

Carl Gustav Jung is the most famous of Freud's disciples to have broken with him, and religion lies at the centre of this break. The son of a pastor, brought up in a parsonage in the canton of Basel and nourished on a keen aversion to his father's theology, he had been designated by Freud as his heir, the “crown prince” of psychoanalysis, according to a formula found in their correspondence. The break was consummated in 1912-1913, after the publication of *Wandlungen und Symbole der Libido* (*Transformations and Symbols of the Libido*), in which Jung extended the libido beyond sexuality and read myths as expressions of a general psychic energy. Freud could not accept this, because the extension took the edge off his theory of religion: if the libido is any psychic energy whatever, religious desire is no longer the repetition of an infantile desire, it is a desire in its own right.

Jung's thesis is simple to state and difficult to assess. The human psyche possesses by nature a religious function, which is not the residue of a neurosis but a normal activity of the soul, as natural as sexuality or aggressiveness. This function expresses itself in images that recur, with striking constancy, in the myths, dreams, visions and delusions of the most distant peoples and ages: these are the **archetypes**, empty forms of the collective unconscious that the particular representations of each culture fill. The most important of them is the **Self**, the image of psychic totality, which Jung recognizes in Christ, the Buddha, the mandala, the philosophers' stone of the alchemists. The process by which the individual enters into relation with this totality, which he calls **individuation**, is for him the psychological equivalent of what religions called salvation. He set it out for the general public in the Terry Lectures at Yale, in 1937, published the following year under the title *Psychology and Religion*.

The Yale lectures contain a definition that concerns this essay directly, because it chooses, between the two etymologies of *religio* examined in the prologue, Cicero's against Lactantius's. Religion, says Jung, is the careful and scrupulous observation of what Rudolf Otto called the *numinosum*, a power that seizes the subject rather than being produced by him. The choice is not innocent: Cicero's *religio* was a scruple, an attentiveness, a rereading; it presupposed neither dogma nor a personal God. In taking it up, Jung makes religion an attitude of consciousness before what exceeds it, and he can say, without contradiction from his point of view, that a man with no denomination is religious if he takes his dreams and symbols seriously. Otto's *Wholly Other* becomes, in Jung, a region of the psyche.

The gap with Freud is clear. For Freud, religion is an illusion, which humanity must outgrow as the child outgrows its dependence; for Jung, it is a function, and the man who represses it pays the price. He formulated this conviction in a clinical observation for which he has often been reproached: among his patients over thirty-five, he says in substance in an essay of 1932, there is not one whose ultimate problem was not that of finding a religious attitude towards life again, which has nothing to do, he specifies, with membership of a denomination or a Church. The neurosis of modern man would be a sickness of the loss of meaning, and psychotherapy would take over from spiritual direction.

This function is not a belief. Jung repeats that he speaks as an empiricist, of what the soul produces, and not as a theologian, of what exists outside it; but the empiricism he claims constantly overflows its own limits.

One formula has fixed this position in people's memories, and it also shows what is disquieting about it. In 1959, the BBC broadcast a long interview of Jung by John Freeman, recorded in his house at Küsnacht. Freeman asks him whether he believes in God. Jung hesitates, then answers: “I know. I don't need to believe. I know”. The sentence caused a scandal on both sides: believers heard in it a gnostic pride that replaces faith with knowledge; unbelievers, a profession of faith disguised as science. Jung himself had to specify in letters that he was speaking of the experience of a psychic reality, not of a metaphysical demonstration.

Jung's boldest book on this ground is also his latest. In *Antwort auf Hiob* (*Answer to Job*, 1952), he reads the book of Job as the moment when man, just and suffering, becomes morally superior to a God who crushes him to win a wager; the Incarnation would be this God's answer to his own injustice, the necessity for him to become man in order to attain the consciousness that his creature has revealed to him. The book scandalized theologians, and with reason: it makes God a developing psychic image that needs human consciousness in order to know itself. But it touches, unexpectedly, the question that the conclusion of this essay leaves open, that of the lament of the oppressed creature. Jung takes Job's side against God, as Ivan Karamazov took the child's side against universal harmony; only, instead of concluding in favour of revolt, he concludes in favour of a transformation of the divine. This is yet another way of keeping religion, by making it absorb its own critique.

The main objection came from a believer, and the response is attested. In 1952, Martin Buber published in the review *Merkur* an essay, “Religion und modernes Denken”, taken up in *Eclipse of God* (New York, Harper, 1952), then in the German edition, *Gottesfinsternis* (*Eclipse of God*, Zurich, Manesse, 1953). In it he accuses Jung of two things: of psychologism, since God is there no more than an image of the soul, and of gnosis, since the relation to the living God, which for Buber is an encounter with an external Thou, is replaced by the knowledge of an inner content. Jung replied in the same review with an “Antwort an Martin Buber”, to which Buber responded. The debate is, at bottom, that of mysticism and gnosis, of faith and knowledge. Jung claims to assert nothing about God in himself, but only about the image of God in the psyche; Buber answers that this reservation is an evasion, and that a god who exists only as a psychic function is not a god but an instrument.

I believe Buber is right, but for a reason that is not exactly his. The danger of Jung, in the light of this essay's criterion, is not that he denies God; it is that he makes religion **necessary** without making it true. If the soul needs religious symbols as the body needs food, one cannot leave religion, only change symbols, and the therapist becomes the guardian of this necessity. It is Durkheim's position transposed from society to the psyche, and it has the same effect: it makes the question of this essay idle. It nevertheless has a consequence that Jung did not see. A religious function detached from all truth and all institution can be filled with anything at all, including what Jung called, in 1936, in an essay on Wotan, the awakening of an archaic god in the German soul, which he described, in my view, more than he fought it. A religion of the unconscious demands no explicit sacrifice; nor does it offer any criterion for refusing the sacrifice that an awakened archetype would demand. Jung was right against Freud on one point: the religious disposition is not merely infantile. He was wrong to believe that it was, in itself, a wisdom.
