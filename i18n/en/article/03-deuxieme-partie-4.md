### Transition: from the Cross to the God of the philosophers

The religion that the Moderns will seek to leave has now received its form: the faith of a messianic Jewish community, thought through with the instruments of Greek philosophy. The God of the Bible, who speaks, chooses, grows angry and repents, has become in Philo a Being of whom we know only that he exists, and whose Logos secures the link with the world. The Christ of the first communities, a crucified prophet whom his disciples saw alive, has become in Justin the Logos itself, which the philosophers had glimpsed, and in Augustine the Word that the books of the Platonists announced without knowing it incarnate. Celsus was right, against the Christians, to say that their theology owes much to Plato, and wrong to see plagiarism in it: it was a translation, perhaps the most fruitful in the whole intellectual history of Europe.

This translation has a price, which the following centuries will pay. In receiving the God of the philosophers, Christianity also received his demands: a God who must be demonstrated, who must be coherent, who must account for the world and its evil. As long as the Church guaranteed the agreement of faith and reason, the demand remained under control; scholasticism had settled it by distinguishing what reason can prove from what revelation alone teaches. The classical age will set it free, and when Pascal sets the God of Abraham, of Isaac and of Jacob against the God of the philosophers, it is precisely to the God from before the Greek translation that he will appeal.
