## Part One: the Ancients and the monotheisms before reason

The critique of religion, which is usually made to begin with Spinoza or the Enlightenment, has an ancient and medieval prehistory, which supplied it with its most durable arguments and its most radical gestures. Plato and Aristotle wanted a rational theology, which Plato armed with a penal law. The Epicureans invented the idea that religion is an error that causes suffering. Plotinus showed that one could seek union with the principle without temple or priest. Averroes and Maimonides, the one a Muslim, the other a Jew, asked what becomes of the revealed Law when a philosopher reads it. Eckhart, finally, carried the critique of the image of God into the heart of Christian mysticism. All of them already raise our question: some leave religion without leaving the gods, others criticize the representation of God in the name of God himself.

### Plato (428/427-348/347 BCE): the first theology, and the first prison for the impious

We must begin with Plato, and not only for reasons of chronology. It was he who invented the word theology in the sense in which we understand it, speech about the gods subjected to rules (the term θεολογία, *theologia*, appears in Book II of the *Republic*, when Socrates lays down the “τύποι περὶ θεολογίας”, the models to which the poets' stories about the gods must conform, 379a); it was he who gave the first philosophical demonstration of the existence of the gods; and it was he, finally, who wrote the first penal law against unbelief. The three gestures are linked, and they are linked in Book X of the *Laws* (*Νόμοι*, *Nomoi*), his last work, unfinished at his death and published, according to tradition, by his disciple Philip of Opus. Anyone who wants to know what it means to “found a religion” through philosophy must read these thirty pages: they are the original of which almost all later refoundations are copies.

The book opens on a problem of criminal law. The Athenian, who leads the dialogue with the Cretan Clinias and the Lacedaemonian Megillus, has finished legislating on acts of violence and comes to outrages against the gods. Before fixing the penalties, he makes a diagnosis that is the founding text of every typology of unbelief: “θεοὺς ἡγούμενος εἶναι κατὰ νόμους οὐδεὶς πώποτε οὔτε ἔργον ἀσεβὲς ἠργάσατο ἑκὼν οὔτε λόγον ἀφῆκεν ἄνομον, ἀλλὰ ἓν δή τι τῶν τριῶν πάσχων, ἢ τοῦτο, ὅπερ εἶπον, οὐχ ἡγούμενος, ἢ τὸ δεύτερον ὄντας οὐ φροντίζειν ἀνθρώπων, ἢ τρίτον εὐπαραμυθήτους εἶναι θυσίαις τε καὶ εὐχαῖς παραγομένους”, no one who believes, as the laws prescribe, that the gods exist has ever willingly committed an impious act or let slip a lawless word; rather, one of three things has befallen him: either he does not believe that they exist, or, secondly, he believes that they exist but take no care of men, or, thirdly, that they are easily appeased, won over by sacrifices and prayers (885b). Three impieties, then: atheism properly so called, indifferent deism, and what might be called mercantile religion, the kind that thinks it can buy the gods.

This table already contains half of the essay. The first impiety is the one Plato attributes to the “learned men” of his time, natural philosophers and sophists, who explain the world by nature and chance and make the gods conventions of the law (889e-890a): it is, twenty-three centuries before d'Holbach, the regime of error. The second is the position that Epicurus will maintain a generation later: gods who exist but concern themselves with nothing. The third, finally, is the target of the whole moral critique of religion, from Spinoza to the Enlightenment and Kant. Plato therefore fights on two fronts. Against the unbeliever, he defends the existence and the providence of the gods; against the devout, their incorruptibility. His religion is at once an apologetics and a critique of popular religion, and that is what makes it formidable: it has already absorbed the objection.

Then comes the proof, the first in history to present itself as a demonstration. It starts not, as Aristotle's will, from the motion of the heavens, but from the soul. Every communicated motion presupposes a first motion that is not communicated; this first motion is “τὴν δυναμένην αὐτὴν αὑτὴν κινεῖν κίνησιν”, the motion capable of moving itself (896a). Now this self-moving motion is the very definition of the soul; Cousin translates: “Une substance qui a la faculté de se mouvoir elle-même”, a substance that has the faculty of moving itself. The soul is therefore prior to the body, and the good souls that move the stars are gods. The Athenian concludes with a rhetorical question in which one recognizes the formula that tradition attributed to Thales: “ἔσθʼ ὅστις ταῦτα ὁμολογῶν ὑπομενεῖ μὴ θεῶν εἶναι πλήρη πάντα;”, is there anyone who, granting this, will bear to deny that all things are full of gods? (899b). I leave aside the worth of the argument, which modern criticism, from Hume to Kant, has taken apart thoroughly enough; what matters to me is its status: for the first time, belief in the gods is no longer handed down by the poets and the ancestors but demonstrated by a philosopher, and, once demonstrated, it can be required.

After the proof comes the law, and it is terrible. Plato provides for three prisons in his city; one of them, near the place where the nocturnal council meets, bears the name “σωφρονιστήριον”, *sôphronistêrion*, the house where one is made wise (908a). The impious man in good faith, the one who has gone astray through want of judgement and not through vice, will spend at least five years there, with no visitors other than the members of the nocturnal council, who will come to converse with him “ἐπὶ νουθετήσει τε καὶ τῇ τῆς ψυχῆς σωτηρίᾳ”, for admonition and for the salvation of his soul; on his release, if he seems to have grown wise, he will live among the wise; if not, should he be convicted a second time, “θανάτῳ ζημιούσθω”, let him be punished with death (909a). Those who join impiety to trickery, the charlatans who claim to summon the dead and to sway the gods, will be shut up for life in the prison in the wilderness, and their corpse will be cast beyond the borders, unburied; any free man who buried it could be prosecuted for impiety (909c). To cut the root of the evil, a last law forbids private worship: “Ἱερὰ μηδὲ εἷς ἐν ἰδίαις οἰκίαις ἐκτήσθω”, let no one possess a shrine in his private house; whoever wishes to sacrifice will go to the public temples, into the hands of the priests (909d).

I have proposed that a religious refoundation be recognized by the presence of an absolute in whose name the sacrifice of the individual can be demanded. Book X of the *Laws* is the pure case of it, and a philosophical case: it is not priests persecuting philosophers, it is a philosopher who founds a state religion, who demonstrates its articles, and who entrusts to a council of the wise the task of re-educating, and then of killing, those who do not adhere to it. Dostoevsky's Grand Inquisitor, who wants the happiness of men and to that end takes from them the freedom to believe, has his ancestor here; and one understands why Karl Popper, in 1945, made Plato the first enemy of the “open society” (I summarize the general thesis of *The Open Society and Its Enemies*, vol. I, without citing a page). Yet Plato must be given his best form. The *sôphronistêrion* is a compulsory school, not a place of torture; the impious man in good faith is treated there as sick in soul, not as a criminal; and the religion imposed on him is not that of the poets, whom Plato despises, but a purified theology in which the gods are good, do not lie, and cannot be bought. The violence of the law serves a religion more moral than the one it replaces. That is the argument of all later refoundations, and what makes them dangerous: Rousseau's civil religion, Comte's cult of Humanity, the education of the new man by the party, all plead that they are replacing a superstition with a truth.

There is, however, another Plato, who nourished the critique of religion rather than state religion: the Plato of the *Euthyphro*, an early dialogue in which Socrates, a few days before his trial for impiety, questions a soothsayer very sure of his own piety. His question has become philosophy's most durable weapon against revealed morality: “ἆρα τὸ ὅσιον ὅτι ὅσιόν ἐστιν φιλεῖται ὑπὸ τῶν θεῶν, ἢ ὅτι φιλεῖται ὅσιόν ἐστιν;”, is the pious loved by the gods because it is pious, or is it pious because it is loved by the gods? (10a). If the pious is pious because the gods love it, morality is merely the arbitrariness of a sovereign will; if they love it because it is pious, piety has its reason in itself, and the gods are merely the witnesses of an order they did not make. The dilemma does not deny the gods, it makes them useless for grounding the good. We shall meet it again, without Plato's name, in the quarrel that closes Part Four: Bayle maintaining that a society of atheists can have morals, Kant making religion the recognition of our duties as if they were divine commands, Dostoevsky putting into Ivan's mouth the claim that there is no virtue without immortality. It is a constructed confrontation, since none of them replies expressly to the *Euthyphro*; but all of them inhabit the question that Socrates opened.

Between the Socrates of the *Euthyphro* and the Athenian of the *Laws* there is thus a tension that Plato did not resolve, and which I take to be the very tension of this essay: on one side, the philosopher who questions religion to the point of dissolving it into morality; on the other, the legislator who protects religion to the point of imprisoning those who question it. The trial of Socrates, accused of not recognizing the gods of the city (*Apology*, 24b-c, which I paraphrase), is the one that the law of the *Laws* makes possible: Plato watched his master die for impiety and wrote, forty years later, a law against impiety. One could not say more brutally that the exit from religion, when philosophy leads it, can end in a religion more demanding than the one it was leaving.

There remains a third Plato, the Plato of the cave, whom the widening to spirituality obliges us to take seriously. In Book VII of the *Republic*, education is not the filling of an empty soul, it is a conversion of the gaze: just as an eye could turn towards the light only together with the whole body, one must, says Socrates, turn the whole soul, “σὺν ὅλῃ τῇ ψυχῇ ἐκ τοῦ γιγνομένου περιακτέον εἶναι”, it must be turned round, with the whole soul, away from what comes to be (518c), and the art of education is “τῆς περιαγωγῆς”, the art of this turning round (518d). The περιαγωγή, *periagôgê*, is the first philosophical name for what Foucault will call spirituality: a transformation of the subject necessary for access to the truth. And the goal of the conversion, the Good, is placed by Plato beyond all that is: it is not an essence, but “ἐπέκεινα τῆς οὐσίας”, beyond essence, which it surpasses in dignity and in power (VI, 509b). This Good beyond being has neither temple, nor priest, nor penal law. Plotinus will make it the One, Dionysius and Maimonides the God of whom nothing can be affirmed, Levinas the Other who escapes ontology.

Plato thus holds both branches of our alternative: the civil religion demonstrated and imposed, with its nocturnal council and its prison for the impious; the spirituality of conversion, which subjects the soul to nothing external but turns it towards a Good it does not possess. The history that follows could be read as the struggle of these two Platos. Lucretius rises up against the first; the Neoplatonists develop the second; and Christianity, which inherits both, will make the *Euthyphro* a theological question (does God will the good because he is good?) and the *Laws* an institution.

### Aristotle (384-322 BCE): a god who thinks only himself

Aristotle wrote no law against the impious, and tradition even relates that he had to leave Athens in 323 under threat of a trial for impiety, after the death of Alexander (I report a late biographical tradition, whose details are uncertain). He nonetheless supplied Western theology with its most durable concept and, without meaning to, supplied its critique with its two strongest arguments: the idea of a god indifferent to human beings, and the idea that religious myths were invented in order to govern.

The first is contained in Book Λ (XII) of the *Metaphysics* (*τὰ μετὰ τὰ φυσικά*, *ta meta ta phusika*), a brief, dense treatise, probably independent of the rest of the collection, which tradition traces back to the editor Andronicus of Rhodes (1st century BCE), though the origin of the title is not certain. The eternal motion of the heavens requires a first mover that is not itself moved, otherwise one would go on to infinity. Now what moves without being moved is the object of desire: “κινεῖ δὴ ὡς ἐρώμενον, κινούμενα δὲ τἆλλα κινεῖ”, it moves, then, as that which is loved, while the other things move by being moved (Λ 7, 1072b3). The world hangs upon it, “ἐκ τοιαύτης ἄρα ἀρχῆς ἤρτηται ὁ οὐρανὸς καὶ ἡ φύσις”, it is on such a principle, then, that the heavens and nature depend (1072b13-14); but it does nothing for the world. It does not create it, since the world is eternal; it does not govern it, since it does not act; it attracts it, as the beloved attracts the lover, without knowing it.

What, then, does it do? It thinks, and, being what is best, it can think only what is best, that is to say itself. Chapter 9 draws the conclusion with admirable dryness: to think anything other than itself would be a fall, for “μὴ ὁρᾶν ἔνια κρεῖττον ἢ ὁρᾶν”, it is better not to see some things than to see them; therefore “αὑτὸν ἄρα νοεῖ, εἴπερ ἐστὶ τὸ κράτιστον, καὶ ἔστιν ἡ νόησις νοήσεως νόησις”, it is itself, then, that it thinks, if indeed it is the most excellent of things, and its thinking is a thinking of thinking (Λ 9, 1074b32-35). Aristotle's god knows neither Socrates, nor the city, nor the prayers addressed to it; it can neither reward nor punish. It is, in the formula that comes earlier, “ζῷον ἀΐδιον ἄριστον”, an eternal and most excellent living being (1072b29), whose life is the activity of the intellect, and this life we share only “μικρὸν χρόνον”, for a short time (1072b15).

Hence the paradox. By the table of the *Laws*, Aristotle falls under the second impiety, that of gods who take no care of human beings; Plato's Athenian would have sent him to the *sôphronistêrion*. Yet this indifferent god became, through Averroes, Maimonides and Thomas Aquinas, the God of the philosophers of the three monotheisms: the first mover of the Thomist “five ways” is a direct reprise of it. Everything Aristotle denied it had to be added: creation, knowledge of particulars, providence; and the tension between the Aristotelian god and the biblical God has never ceased to work on theology. I hold that Pascal was right in substance (Part Three): Aristotle's god is an object of contemplation, not of religion, and one does not pray to the thinking of thinking. But that is precisely what makes it an ancestor of the spiritualities without God: an absolute that demands nothing.

Aristotle is not entirely consistent on this point, and this must be said so as not to modernize him. In the *Nicomachean Ethics*, he writes that the man who lives according to the intellect is “θεοφιλέστατος”, the most loved by the gods, and he immediately adds a conditional: “εἰ γάρ τις ἐπιμέλεια τῶν ἀνθρωπίνων ὑπὸ θεῶν γίνεται, ὥσπερ δοκεῖ”, for if the gods take some care of human affairs, as people think (X, 8, 1179a22-24). The “as people think”, ὥσπερ δοκεῖ, marks a calculated prudence: Aristotle does not deny popular providence, he lodges it in opinion without integrating it into his theology.

The second argument is more discreet, and more important. In chapter 8 of the same Book Λ, after calculating the number of the celestial movers, Aristotle makes a remark worthy of a historian of religions before the term existed: the ancients, he says, handed down in the form of myth that the stars are gods and that the divine encompasses the whole of nature; and he goes on: “τὰ δὲ λοιπὰ μυθικῶς ἤδη προσῆκται πρὸς τὴν πειθὼ τῶν πολλῶν καὶ πρὸς τὴν εἰς τοὺς νόμους καὶ τὸ συμφέρον χρῆσιν”, the rest has since been added in mythical form, to persuade the many and for its usefulness with a view to the laws and the common interest (1074b3-5). Gods in human form, genealogies, metamorphoses into animals: all this is superadded. Only the kernel, the idea that the first substances are divine, deserves to be said “θείως”, divinely, and it is a relic of a lost wisdom that the revolutions of the arts have several times destroyed and recovered.

These three lines reach far. They contain the thesis of religion as social function, which Durkheim will systematize in 1912, and that of religion as a political instrument, which the libertines and the anonymous author of the *Treatise of the Three Impostors* will harden into the accusation of fraud. But in a subtler form than in his heirs: Aristotle does not say that the legislators lied, he says that myth was added to a truth, to make it effective among those who cannot contemplate it. Popular religion is a useful translation of a philosophical truth. Averroes in *The Decisive Treatise*, Spinoza in making Scripture a pedagogy of obedience, Hegel in making religion the truth in the form of representation, will give successive versions of it. It is a lineage, not a series of attested responses; but it is clear enough for me to hold Aristotle, and not Lucretius, to be the true ancestor of the “elitist” reading of religion, which does not deny its content but relegates its form.

To him, finally, we owe the expression that will occupy Heidegger. In Book E of the *Metaphysics*, he classifies the theoretical sciences: “ὥστε τρεῖς ἂν εἶεν φιλοσοφίαι θεωρητικαί, μαθηματική, φυσική, θεολογική”, so there would be three theoretical philosophies, mathematics, physics, theology (E 1, 1026a18-19). First philosophy is thus at once the science of being qua being and the science of the highest being, the divine. It is this double vocation, ontology and theology fused, that Heidegger will call the onto-theo-logical constitution of metaphysics, and that he will make the common source of Western philosophy and of the Christian God of the theologians (we shall meet it again in Part Ten). If Heidegger is right, philosophy cannot lead the critique of religion, since it is already, and has been ever since Aristotle, a theology. This is the most radical of the accusations in this essay, *you think you have left religion, but you are still in it*, addressed not to this or that author but to metaphysics as a whole; it has its origin in an adjective of Aristotle's.

There remains a spiritual Aristotle, an unexpected one. In Book X of the *Nicomachean Ethics*, the happiest life is the contemplative life, and Aristotle describes it in terms that border on mysticism: such a life “κρείττων ἢ κατʼ ἄνθρωπον”, would be higher than the human level, for it is not insofar as he is a man that one will live it, “ἀλλʼ ᾗ θεῖόν τι ἐν αὐτῷ ὑπάρχει”, but insofar as there is something divine in him; and we must not listen to those who advise man to think human thoughts and the mortal to think mortal thoughts, but must on the contrary, “ἐφʼ ὅσον ἐνδέχεται ἀθανατίζειν”, as far as is possible, make ourselves immortal (X, 7, 1177b26-33). The verb ἀθανατίζειν, *athanatizein*, is one of the most astonishing in the Greek philosophical language. It promises no survival; it designates a way of living, here and now, at the height of what does not die, without worship, without prayer, without community, through the sole exercise of the intellect.

Aristotle's best lesson perhaps lies there. He supplied the religion of the philosophers with its god and its critique with its sociological suspicion; but above all he showed that one could have a relation to the divine that is neither a belief, nor an obedience, nor a hope. The god who thinks only himself demands no sacrifice, since he does not know that we exist; the man who makes himself immortal by thinking submits to nothing, he rises. It is, in a form that is still theological, the spirituality of exercise which I have hypothesized to be the only exit that does not refound. Spinoza, who did not like Aristotle, will give its version without transcendence with the intellectual love of God, and the eternity that he says is felt and experienced (Part Three) is very close to the Aristotelian ἀθανατίζειν.

### Epicurus (341-270 BCE) and Lucretius (c. 98-c. 55 BCE): terror and peace

Let us first correct an image: Epicurus is not an atheist. The *Letter to Menoeceus*, preserved by Diogenes Laertius in Book X of the *Lives of the Eminent Philosophers*, affirms the existence of the gods and even makes it the first article of wisdom. In Octave Hamelin's translation (1910): “Commence par te persuader qu'un dieu est un animal immortel et bienheureux, te conformant en cela à l'anticipation du dieu qui est gravée en toi”, begin by persuading yourself that a god is an immortal and blessed living being, in keeping with the common notion of god engraved in you; then: “Car les dieux existent, attendu que nous connaissons leur existence par une intuition évidente. Mais, quant à leur nature, ils ne sont pas tels que la foule le croit”, for the gods exist, since our knowledge of them is a matter of clear vision; but as to their nature, they are not such as the many believe. The Epicurean critique bears not on the existence of the gods, but on their character. The gods of the crowd grow angry, reward, punish, demand sacrifices; those of Epicurus, perfectly happy, concern themselves with nothing, for any care would disturb their blessedness.

Hence the sentence that overturns the very notion of impiety: “Et celui-là n'est pas impie qui nie les dieux de la foule, c'est celui qui attribue aux dieux ce que leur prêtent les opinions de la foule”, the impious man is not the one who denies the gods of the many, but the one who attaches to the gods what the opinions of the many ascribe to them. The impious man is no longer the unbeliever, but the devout man who forms an unworthy idea of God. This reversal Spinoza will take up, and Simone Weil will push it to its limit in speaking of a purifying atheism. Religious error, for Epicurus, is first of all a lapse of metaphysical taste: we lend the gods our passions.

This error is serious because it causes suffering. Epicurean philosophy is a therapeutics, and the religion of the crowd is, along with the fear of death, one of the two great causes of the soul's disturbance. Against the latter, the *Letter* gives the famous formula: “Prends l'habitude de penser que la mort n'est rien relativement à nous”, accustom yourself to thinking that death is nothing to us. The two fears are linked, for one fears the gods for what they will do with us after death: remove the one, and the other falls.

Lucretius gives this therapeutics its epic form and its violence. The *De rerum natura* (*On the Nature of Things*), a didactic poem in six books whose date of composition is uncertain and whose author is almost unknown, opens with a eulogy of Epicurus that is a battle painting. Human life lay on the ground, shamefully, “humana ante oculos foede cum vita iaceret / in terris oppressa gravi sub religione”, crushed beneath the weight of religion (I, 62-63); and it was a Greek, a mortal, who first dared to raise his eyes against it, “primum Graius homo mortalis tollere contra / est oculos ausus” (I, 66-67). The victory is total and it has a precise meaning: “quare religio pedibus subiecta vicissim / opteritur, nos exaequat victoria caelo”, therefore religion in its turn is cast beneath our feet and trampled, and the victory makes us equal to heaven (I, 78-79).

This last line announces our whole problem. Victory over religion *makes us equal to heaven*. Lucretius does not say merely that man is liberated, but that he takes the place of the gods, or at least rises to their height. The end of the *Letter to Menoeceus* promises it to the disciple: “vivras comme un dieu parmi les hommes”, you will live like a god among men. The exit from religion is accomplished as a divinization of man. Feuerbach, nineteen centuries later, will say nothing else, and Stirner will reproach him for it.

Then comes the example, the most famous passage of the poem. So that no one should think that philosophy leads to impiety, Lucretius recalls that it is religion that has given birth to crimes, “religio peperit scelerosa atque impia facta” (I, 83), and he tells of the sacrifice of Iphigenia at Aulis: the girl led to the altar not for her wedding but to be slaughtered by her father, so that the Greek fleet might obtain a favourable wind. The account closes on the line that the whole libertine tradition engraved on the pediment of its books: “tantum religio potuit suadere malorum”, so great the evils religion could prompt (I, 101).

The word *religio* in Lucretius does not have its Christian sense. It designates the scrupulous fear of the gods, the feeling of being bound by sacred obligations, and it is this feeling, more than any dogmas, that Lucretius combats. His critique belongs entirely to the regime of error: the fear of the gods comes from ignorance of natural causes, and atomist physics, by explaining lightning, earthquakes and disease, dispels the terror. But it already contains a spiritual dimension that the moderns will forget: knowledge of nature is not an end in itself, it is an exercise that procures ataraxia, the absence of disturbance.

Michel Onfray will claim Epicurus and Lucretius as his ancestors. That is half right. He inherits from them the regime of error and the hatred of priests, not their piety: Epicurus and Lucretius, who mock the religion of the crowd, keep gods, and these gods are a model of life. The Epicureans leave religion without leaving the divine: it is the first form, and perhaps the wisest, of my paradox.

> Horace's ode I, 34 opens with the confession of a disciple of Epicurus: “Parcus deorum cultor et infrequens, / insanientis dum sapientiae / consultus erro”, a sparing and infrequent worshipper of the gods, while I strayed, versed in a wisdom that was folly; then a clap of thunder from a clear sky, “per purum tonantis”, brings him back to the gods, for Jupiter has driven his chariot without the help of a cloud, which the Epicurean physics of lightning does not foresee.
>
> Why here: the poem shows, from within and with irony, the fragility of the regime of error. Lucretius explains lightning to dispel fear; an unexplained bolt is enough for fear to make a god again. Hume will make a law of it: flux and reflux.
>
> Eighteen centuries later, Leopardi, who had read Lucretius, writes *La ginestra* at the foot of Vesuvius: the broom that grows on the lava asks nothing of heaven, does not think itself the centre of the world, and bends without pride beneath the flow that will destroy it; the poem mocks the centuries that paint on these slopes “dell'umana gente / "le magnifiche sorti e progressive"”, the magnificent and progressive destinies of the human race, and says, before the human anthill crushed by the lava, “Non so se il riso o la pietà prevale”, I do not know whether laughter or pity prevails; the broom will bow “sotto il fascio mortal non renitente / il tuo capo innocente”, unresisting beneath the mortal burden, its innocent head, “ma non piegato insino allora indarno / codardamente supplicando innanzi / al futuro oppressor”, but not having bowed it until then in vain, cravenly pleading before its future oppressor. It is Lucretius' ataraxia without his happy gods, and without the hope that knowledge will heal. Yourcenar, finally, lends her Hadrian the position of the Epicurean emperor who maintains the cults of the Empire without believing in them and composes his *Animula vagula blandula* in the face of death: religion kept as a form, the divine kept as a question.
>
> Sources: Horace, *Odes* I, 34 (23 BCE) ; Giacomo Leopardi, “La ginestra, o il fiore del deserto”, *Canti* XXXIV (1836) ; Marguerite Yourcenar, *Memoirs of Hadrian* (1951).
