### Barth (1886-1968): religion is the affair of godless man

Karl Barth is the greatest Protestant theologian of the twentieth century, and the first theologian of that century, in this essay, to maintain that the critique of religion is not a weapon against faith but a demand of faith. His position is all the more singular in that he formulates it in Feuerbach's own terms, having read Feuerbach closely and taken him seriously as almost no theologian before him had.

Trained in German liberal theology, the heir of Schleiermacher, Barth broke with it during the First World War, when he saw his teachers, Harnack and Herrmann among them, sign the “Aufruf an die Kulturwelt” of 4 October 1914, known as the Manifesto of the Ninety-Three, by which German intellectuals declared their solidarity with the war; Barth would say that he had discovered there, “with horror”, the names of almost all his German teachers. The theology that had made religion an expression of human consciousness, of feeling, of culture, seemed to him to have handed God over to man. His *Römerbrief* (*The Epistle to the Romans*, 1919, recast in 1922) proclaims, on the contrary, the infinite qualitative difference between God and man. The expression comes from Kierkegaard, and Barth says so himself in the preface to the second edition: if he has a “system”, it consists in keeping in view what Kierkegaard called the “unendlichen qualitativen Unterschied” of time and eternity; the commentary itself speaks of the “qualitativen Unterschieds von Gott und Mensch, Gott und Welt”, the qualitative difference between God and man, God and world. God is not the extension of our aspirations; he is the Wholly Other, who can be known only if he reveals himself, and who reveals himself in Jesus Christ, not in religion.

It is in this context that paragraph 17 of the *Kirchliche Dogmatik* (*Church Dogmatics*) must be read, in volume I/2, published in 1938. Its title is already a thesis: “Gottes Offenbarung als Aufhebung der Religion”, the revelation of God as the *Aufhebung* of religion. The word is Hegel's, and Barth plays on it deliberately: it means at once abolition and elevation, suppression and preservation at a higher level. The central sentence of the paragraph is this: “Religion ist Unglaube; Religion ist eine Angelegenheit, man muß geradezu sagen: die Angelegenheit des gottlosen Menschen”, religion is unbelief; religion is an affair, one must say it outright: the affair of godless man.

By “religion”, Barth means the human attempt to grasp God by one's own means: to represent him, to win him over, to justify oneself before him through works, rites, thoughts. Now this attempt is, in the eyes of revelation, the contrary of faith. Faith receives; religion takes. Faith listens to a word that comes from elsewhere; religion manufactures a god to its own measure. Barth makes no exception for Christianity: the Christian religion is a religion like the others, and as such it is unbelief. It is “true” only by grace, as a sinner is righteous only when justified. Barth uses an image: revelation illuminates the Christian religion as the sun illuminates a region of the earth, without changing its nature.

The kinship with Feuerbach is not fortuitous. Barth devoted an essay to him in 1927, “Ludwig Feuerbach”, published in the journal *Zwischen den Zeiten* and then collected in *Die Theologie und die Kirche* (1928); it is this text which, translated into English, serves as the introductory essay to the 1957 American reissue of George Eliot's translation of *The Essence of Christianity*. In it Barth grants Feuerbach a victory that the apologists of the nineteenth century had always refused him: Feuerbach is “mit seiner Religionsdeutung auf der ganzen Linie Recht”, right all along the line with his interpretation of religion, at least “sofern sie sich auf die Religion als Erfahrung des bösen und sterblichen Menschen bezieht”, insofar as it bears on religion as the experience of evil and mortal man. This is an attested reading: a theologian concedes the point to theology's most feared critic.

But he concedes it on *religion*, and therein lies the whole subtlety of the manoeuvre. Yes, religion is projection; yes, the God whom man represents to himself is only his own essence enlarged; yes, the theology that starts from man, from his feeling or his consciousness, like Schleiermacher's, necessarily ends in anthropology. Barth even draws from this an argument against the whole of liberal theology: Feuerbach is the truth of Schleiermacher. Only, revelation is not religion. It is not a product of consciousness; it comes from elsewhere, and it is precisely for this reason that it judges religion. Feuerbach's critique, turned around, becomes an instrument for purifying faith.

The boldness of the move is considerable. Feuerbach believed he had dissolved theology into anthropology. Barth replies: you have dissolved religion, and you were right to do so; faith was not in what you dissolved. It is the reverse of Stirner's operation: Stirner showed that the atheist remained pious; Barth shows that the pious man, as religious, is an atheist, “gottlos”, without God.

The objection is well known, and it was formulated as early as the 1960s: Barth removes revelation from critique by declaring it out of reach, but this removal holds only for someone who already believes. John Glasse, in “Barth on Feuerbach” (*Harvard Theological Review* 57, 1964), and others after him have argued that the Barthian answer is intelligible only from within faith. Feuerbach could reply that “revelation” is the most refined projection of all: the one that declares itself unprojected. And a reader of Nietzsche would add that the distinction between religion and faith is itself a religious gesture, the one by which the priest reserves for himself the truth he denies to others.

I believe, however, that Barth scores a point that this objection does not erase. He establishes that the critique of religion is not, by nature, a critique of transcendence; it can be a critique of the *appropriation* of transcendence. What he condemns in religion is man making use of God, and this condemnation meets, by an entirely different road, what Spinoza said of the vulgar who imagine a God in their own image, what Marx said of opium, what Nietzsche said of the priest. Our question then takes a new form: leaving religion without founding another might perhaps be possible if one agrees not to leave faith. It is a wager that most of our authors would refuse, and one that an unruly pupil of Barth was to push to its most extreme consequences.

### Bonhoeffer (1906-1945): living before God without God

Dietrich Bonhoeffer, a Lutheran pastor trained in Berlin, close to Barth without being his disciple, joined the Confessing Church against the Nazi takeover of German Protestantism, then the resistance circles preparing the elimination of Hitler. Arrested in April 1943, he was executed at the Flossenbürg camp on 9 April 1945, a few weeks before the end of the war. The letters he wrote from Tegel prison to his friend Eberhard Bethge, published by Bethge in 1951 under the title *Widerstand und Ergebung* (*Resistance and Submission*), contain some of the most debated pages in the theology of the century.

The starting point is an observation, formulated in the letter of 30 April 1944: “Wir gehen einer völlig religionslosen Zeit entgegen; die Menschen können einfach, so wie sie nun einmal sind, nicht mehr religiös sein”, we are moving towards a completely religionless time; people as they now are simply cannot be religious any more. This observation is not a lament: Bonhoeffer does not regret the religious age and does not wish to restore it. He asks what becomes of Christ in a world that no longer needs religion.

By “religion” he means neither faith nor Christianity as such, but a certain way of believing, settled in the Christian West, which rests on three features: metaphysics, which places God in a world behind the world; inwardness, which lodges him in the secret of the individual soul; and above all recourse to God at the limits, where human knowledge and power fail. It is this last feature that he designates by the word *Lückenbüßer*, the stopgap. The God of religion is the one called upon when we no longer know, when we no longer can, when we die. Now, as science, technology, medicine and politics push back these limits, this God retreats: a God of the gaps, he loses a province with every human advance.

In the letter of 16 July 1944, the most famous, Bonhoeffer retraces the history of modern autonomy, from Herbert of Cherbury to Grotius, from Descartes to Kant, and concludes: “Gott als moralische, politische, naturwissenschaftliche Arbeitshypothese ist abgeschafft, überwunden; ebenso aber als philosophische und religiöse Arbeitshypothese (Feuerbach!)”, God as a moral, political, scientific working hypothesis has been abolished, overcome; but so too as a philosophical and religious working hypothesis (Feuerbach!). Feuerbach's name, in parentheses and followed by an exclamation mark, is an attested mention. Bonhoeffer does not refute Feuerbach: he records him as an irreversible stage in the history of consciousness, behind which there is no going back.

In the same letter, Bonhoeffer takes up Grotius's formula, “etsi deus non daretur”, as if God were not given. Grotius had used it to say that natural law would hold even if God did not exist. Bonhoeffer turns it into a maxim of Christian life: one must live in the world as if God were not given. And he adds the sentence that caused a scandal: “Vor und mit Gott leben wir ohne Gott. Gott läßt sich aus der Welt herausdrängen ans Kreuz, Gott ist ohnmächtig und schwach in der Welt und gerade und nur so ist er bei uns und hilft uns”, before God and with God we live without God. God lets himself be pushed out of the world and onto the cross; God is powerless and weak in the world, and precisely thus, and only thus, is he with us and does he help us.

This thought overturns, term by term, the critique of the nineteenth century. Feuerbach said: God is the projection of human power; man strips himself of his strengths in order to adore them in an all-powerful being. Bonhoeffer replies: the God of the cross is not all-powerful; he is weak, he lets himself be driven out, and it is this very weakness that makes him a God and not a projection. Marx said: religion consoles for powerlessness. Bonhoeffer replies: faith is not the recourse of the powerless; it is the life of the adult, who no longer needs God to plug the holes in his knowledge. Nietzsche said: Christianity is a religion of pity, which preserves the weak. Bonhoeffer replies: Christianity is participation in the suffering of God in the world, not an escape from the world.

Two days later, on 18 July 1944, Bonhoeffer returns to the idea of a world come of age, “mündig gewordene Welt”, an expression that echoes the Kantian definition of the Enlightenment as emergence from the state of immaturity. This world come of age is, he writes, “gottloser und darum vielleicht gerade Gott-näher als die unmündige Welt”, more godless, and for that very reason perhaps closer to God than the world not yet come of age.

A misreading must first be set aside. Bonhoeffer did not preach an atheist Christianity, nor a “death of God” in the sense of the American theologians of the 1960s who would claim him as their own. He believes in God, he prays, he dies a Christian. What he rejects is religion as a historical form of faith, and he seeks a “non-religious interpretation” of biblical concepts. I have not verified that the exact phrase “religionsloses Christentum”, religionless Christianity, appears in the letters, although it circulates everywhere under his name; I therefore give it as a received summary, not as a quotation.

Bonhoeffer also distinguishes himself from Barth, and the distinction is attested. In the letters of May and June 1944, he reproaches his elder with an “Offenbarungspositivismus”, a positivism of revelation: Barth was right to criticize religion, but he put in its place a revelation to be taken or left, a block of dogmas that must be swallowed whole. This is, in theological terms, the very objection that Feuerbach could have made to Barth: revelation declared out of reach becomes a new positivity, a new law. Bonhoeffer is no gentler with Bultmann, who wanted to demythologize the New Testament: “Bultmanns Ansatz ist eben im Grund doch liberal”, Bultmann's approach is, at bottom, liberal after all. To demythologize is still to reduce faith to what modern man can accept: it is to do Schleiermacher over again.

Bonhoeffer thus attempts a narrow path between two failures: that of liberal theology, which dissolves faith into culture, and that of Barth, which freezes it into positive revelation. The dogmaticians who followed him doubted that it was practicable, and history seemed to prove him wrong on one point at least: the forecast of a “completely religionless” time turned out to be false, since the last third of the twentieth century saw religion return everywhere in forms Bonhoeffer had not foreseen. But this empirical refutation does not touch the heart of his thought, which is not a sociological prophecy: faith does not need religion, and the fading of religion can be an opportunity for it.

Here we have the radical form of the Christian answer to our question. Can one leave religion without founding another? Yes, Bonhoeffer answers, on condition of not leaving Christ. The exit from religion is not the end of faith but its purification, and this purification passes through the acceptance of a weak, absent, crucified God. It will be objected, and I object, that this weak God looks very much like a projection of the condition of the prisoner of Tegel: a man reduced to powerlessness, who turns his powerlessness into a place of divine presence. But the objection turns back on itself: if projection produces a powerless God, without glory, who does not console and guarantees nothing, it is no longer the projection Feuerbach described, the one that gave God all the perfections man denied himself. It is, on the contrary, a dispossession. And it was this dispossession that a French philosopher, who died in the same year as Bonhoeffer's arrest, had pushed further.

### Simone Weil (1909-1943): atheism as purification

Simone Weil died at Ashford, in England, on 24 August 1943, at the age of thirty-four. An *agrégée* in philosophy, a pupil of Alain, a factory worker at Alsthom and at Renault, a short-lived combatant in the Spanish Civil War, she had, from 1937 and 1938 onwards, mystical experiences that she describes with extreme sobriety, without ever asking for baptism. She remains on the threshold of the Church, out of fidelity, she says, to everything the Church has excluded. She published no book in her lifetime. *Gravity and Grace* (1947) is a compilation of fragments drawn from her notebooks, chosen, ordered and titled by Gustave Thibon, to whom she had entrusted her notebooks in 1942; the chapter titles and their order are his. What one quotes, then, are fragments, not a composed doctrine.

One of these chapters is entitled “L'athéisme purificateur”, purifying atheism. It opens with a paradox that Weil accepts without trying to resolve it: “Cas de contradictoires vrais. Dieu existe, Dieu n'existe pas. Où est le problème ? Je suis tout à fait sûre qu'il y a un Dieu, en ce sens que je suis tout à fait sûre que mon amour n'est pas illusoire. Je suis tout à fait sûre qu'il n'y a pas de Dieu, en ce sens que je suis tout à fait sûre que rien de réel ne ressemble à ce que je peux concevoir quand je prononce ce nom. Mais cela que je ne puis concevoir n'est pas une illusion”, a case of contradictories that are both true. God exists, God does not exist. Where is the problem? I am quite sure that there is a God, in the sense that I am quite sure my love is not illusory. I am quite sure that there is no God, in the sense that I am quite sure nothing real resembles what I can conceive when I pronounce that name. But that which I cannot conceive is not an illusion.

Weil does not say that God exists in one way and does not exist in another, according to a convenient scholastic distinction. She says that both propositions are true, and true together. God exists, in the sense that the love that turns towards him is not an illusion. God does not exist, in the sense that everything I can represent to myself under that name is false. The negation bears on the representation, the affirmation on the orientation. And this double truth leads to the most quoted sentence of the chapter: “Il y a deux athéismes dont l'un est une purification de la notion de Dieu”, there are two atheisms, one of which is a purification of the notion of God.

What is the other? Weil does not name it, but the context points to it: the atheism that denies God in order to put man in his place, the humanist atheism of Feuerbach and Comte. That one is not a purification; it is one more idolatry. Weil says so elsewhere in the same chapter, with a severity that meets Stirner by an entirely different road: “Les erreurs de notre époque sont du christianisme sans surnaturel. Le laïcisme en est la cause”, the errors of our age are Christianity without the supernatural. Secularism is the cause of them. Christianity without the supernatural is the religion of Humanity, Christian morality kept without God, which Nietzsche mocked in the English “atheists” and which Stirner denounced in Feuerbach. Weil rejects it, but in the name of the supernatural, not in the name of the Unique One.

Purifying atheism, by contrast, denies the false God, and Weil specifies what it is: “La religion en tant que source de consolation est un obstacle à la véritable foi : en ce sens l'athéisme est une purification. Je dois être athée avec la partie de moi-même qui n'est pas faite pour Dieu. Parmi les hommes chez qui la partie surnaturelle d'eux-mêmes n'est pas éveillée, les athées ont raison et les croyants ont tort”, religion as a source of consolation is an obstacle to true faith: in this sense atheism is a purification. I must be an atheist with the part of myself that is not made for God. Among those in whom the supernatural part of themselves has not been awakened, the atheists are right and the believers wrong.

This fragment matters to our question, for it answers Marx directly. Marx said that religion is the opium of the people, the consolation of the oppressed creature. Weil adopts this as her own, and draws from it a conclusion he had not envisaged: what Marx criticized as illusion, Weil criticizes as idolatry. And among those who have no experience of the supernatural, the atheist is right against the believer, because the believer without experience adores an image. In the same chapter: “Entre deux hommes qui n'ont pas l'expérience de Dieu, celui qui le nie en est peut-être le plus près”, between two men who have no experience of God, the one who denies him is perhaps the closer to him. The confrontation is constructed, and the biography warrants it: Weil read Marx, she was a trade-union and revolutionary activist, and her critique of religious consolation is a Marxist critique turned towards the supernatural.

Weil also proposes a practice: “Un mode de purification : prier Dieu, non seulement en secret par rapport aux hommes, mais en pensant que Dieu n'existe pas”, one mode of purification: to pray to God, not only in secret with regard to men, but thinking that God does not exist. The formula meets, across six centuries, Eckhart's prayer quoted in Part One, to ask God to be freed of God. It also meets Bonhoeffer, living before God as if God were not given, and Heidegger, for whom godless thinking is perhaps closer to the divine God. These convergences are constructed confrontations, with no established reading of one by another; but their concordance, within the space of fifteen years, from 1942 to 1957, says something about the age: the critique of religion had ceased to be the monopoly of unbelievers.

The metaphysical heart of this thought is the notion of *decreation*. The chapter bearing that title opens thus: “Décréation : faire passer du créé dans l'incréé. Destruction : faire passer du créé dans le néant. Ersatz coupable de la décréation”, decreation: to make something created pass into the uncreated. Destruction: to make something created pass into nothingness. A guilty substitute for decreation. Then: “La création est un acte d'amour et elle est perpétuelle. À chaque instant notre existence est amour de Dieu pour nous. Mais Dieu ne peut aimer que soi-même. Son amour pour nous est amour pour soi à travers nous. Ainsi, lui qui nous donne l'être, il aime en nous le consentement à ne pas être”, creation is an act of love and it is perpetual. At every instant our existence is God's love for us. But God can love only himself. His love for us is love for himself through us. Thus he who gives us being loves in us the consent not to be.

God, in creating, withdrew: he renounced being everything so that something other than himself could exist. Weil writes it in a single line: “Dieu renonce, en un sens, à être tout. Nous devons renoncer à être quelque chose. C'est le seul bien pour nous”, God renounces, in a sense, being everything. We must renounce being something. That is the only good for us. Spirituality is then neither the flourishing of the self nor its divinization, but the imitation of the divine withdrawal: the self must consent not to be, so that God may pass through it.

The gap with Part Six is total. Feuerbach wanted to give back to man the predicates he had alienated in God; Weil wants man to renounce even those he believes he possesses. Stirner founded his cause on nothing, and this nothing was the creative self, “das schöpferische Nichts”. Weil founds hers on a nothing of another nature: the consent of the self to efface itself. Both start from nothingness, but in opposite directions: Stirner's nothingness is a power that consumes, Weil's an absence that lets through.

This thought provides, in my view, a fine instrument for distinguishing a spirituality that serves as opium from one that does not: *consolation*. It is not a second criterion of refoundation, which remains the absolute in whose name the sacrifice of the individual can be demanded; it is an indicator. Every thought that consoles, promises, guarantees a reward, whether heavenly, as with Nietzsche's priests, or historical, as with the Marxists, keeps religion's function as opium. A spirituality that does not console, that accepts misfortune without justifying it and consents without hoping for compensation, escapes it. Weil says so in another fragment of the collection: “Il ne faut pas avoir de consolation”, one must not have consolation.

The objection is serious. This spirituality of consent, of renunciation, of decreation, resembles feature for feature what Nietzsche called the ascetic ideal: the will turned against itself, which would rather will nothingness than not will. I believe the objection is partly right, and it would be dishonest to set it aside in the name of the admiration Weil inspires. But it misses the essential point. The ascetic ideal, according to Nietzsche, gives a meaning to suffering; it makes it bearable by interpreting it as punishment or as trial. Weil refuses precisely that: she gives no meaning to misfortune, she refuses to let one be given. Misfortune is neither a punishment nor a means; it is the condition of the creature subject to necessity, and the only right attitude is to look at it without disguising it. In this sense Weil is closer to Spinoza than to Saint Paul: she wants to see necessity as it is.

I therefore hold Weil to be the principal believing candidate for the rank of exception, alongside Spinoza, Hume, Nietzsche and Wittgenstein. She does not leave religion, since she believes. But she leaves consoling religion, the religion that founds and guarantees, and she does so while keeping the name of God and denying every representation of God. She is, with Eckhart, the proof that a spirituality can be a critique of religion from within.
