### Do we have the right to believe? Clifford, James and analytic philosophy

Another question has accompanied this one since the nineteenth century, more unsettling because it bears no longer on causes but on rights: do we have the right to believe what we believe? Explaining a belief by fear, desire, society or the structure of the mind leaves wholly open the question whether the person who holds it is at fault. Hume had given this second question its soberest maxim, at the beginning of the section on miracles in the *Enquiry concerning Human Understanding* (1748, under the title *Philosophical Essays concerning Human Understanding*, which became *An Enquiry* in 1758): “A wise man, therefore, proportions his belief to the evidence.” But Hume was speaking of prudence, not of duty. It was an English mathematician who, in 1877, turned this maxim into a moral obligation, and an American psychologist who answered him, by name, twenty years later. The exchange is an attested response, and it founded what analytic philosophy now calls the ethics of belief.

William Kingdon Clifford (1845-1879) was a geometer of the first rank, who died of tuberculosis at thirty-three. His article “The Ethics of Belief” appeared in the *Contemporary Review* in January 1877. It opens with a parable that has remained famous. A shipowner is about to send an emigrant ship to sea; he knows that the vessel is old, that it has often needed repairs, and doubts about its soundness have been put to him. These doubts make him unhappy; yet he rids himself of them: the ship has come through so many storms, Providence will protect these poor families, and it would be ungenerous to suspect the builders. He thus acquires, Clifford writes, “a sincere and comfortable conviction that his vessel was thoroughly safe and seaworthy”; he watches her depart with a light heart, and he collects the insurance money when she goes down in mid-ocean and tells no tales. Clifford adds that even if the ship had reached port safely, the owner would be no less guilty, because he had no right to believe on such evidence. The fault lies not in the outcome but in the way the belief was acquired.

The thesis that follows concedes nothing. Belief is not a private matter. It is, Clifford writes, “ours not for ourselves, but for humanity”, because no belief, however humble, is without effect on others: each prepares actions, each sustains a climate. It is therefore sacred, and it can be profaned: “It is desecrated when given to unproved and unquestioned statements, for the solace and private pleasure of the believer.” Whoever wishes to deserve well of his fellows “will guard the purity of his belief with a very fanaticism of jealous care”. And if a belief has been accepted on insufficient evidence, even if it turns out to be true, the pleasure it gives is stolen, and guilty, “because it is stolen in defiance of our duty to mankind”, a duty that consists in guarding ourselves from such beliefs “as from a pestilence”, which may shortly master our own body and then spread to the rest of the town. The first section sums itself up in a formula that has become the coat of arms of what would be called evidentialism: “it is wrong always, everywhere, and for anyone, to believe anything upon insufficient evidence.”

Clifford does not soften the consequences of this principle. Whoever sets aside his doubts, avoids the books and the people who might feed them, and regards as impious the questions that disturb his belief, leads in his view a life that is one long fault: the sentence ends on “one long sin against mankind”. To the man too busy to examine, he answers in a word: “Then he should have no time to believe.” The second section, on the weight of authority, bears directly on our subject. Clifford allows that one may take a chemist at his word, because one could in principle verify what he says; he does not allow that one should believe a prophet, because a man's moral greatness does not guarantee that he knows what he asserts. He takes the example of the founders of religions: the Prophet teaches that there is one God, whereas “The Buddha says that there is no God”, and that we shall be annihilated if we are good enough; they cannot both be infallibly inspired. The argument from the diversity of revelations, which Hume had used against miracles, becomes in Clifford a rule of hygiene: where the authorities contradict one another, none has any standing. He ends the article with a sentence that hardens the maxim further still: “It is wrong in all cases to believe on insufficient evidence; and where it is presumption to doubt and to investigate, there it is worse than presumption to believe.”

Read in the light of this essay's criterion, Clifford is instructive. His vocabulary is religious through and through: belief is a “sacred faculty”, one profanes it, one sins, one steals, one guards its purity with fanaticism, and the duty that binds the believer is a duty towards humanity, in whose name, he writes, we shall be strong, “not for ourselves, but in the name of Man and in his strength”. One recognizes Comte's religion of Humanity and Feuerbach's human species, and one may wonder whether Clifford's evidentialism is not one more cult, that of scientific Truth, with its priests and its sins. I believe, however, that Clifford stays short of the line, which makes his case precious. His absolute demands a renunciation, but of oneself alone: one renounces a consolation, one demands the sacrifice of no one. The heart of his parable is even an argument against any refoundation. The shipowner is guilty because his comfortable belief endangered the lives of others. Clifford saw what most of his heirs have forgotten: the scandal of belief without evidence is not that it is false, it is that it is paid for, often, with the blood of others.

The answer came in 1896. William James, whose *Varieties of Religious Experience* we have read, delivered before the philosophical clubs of Yale and Brown a lecture entitled “The Will to Believe”, published in the journal *The New World* in June 1896, then at the head of a collection of the same title whose preface is dated December 1896. James quotes Clifford at length, with affectionate irony: he calls him “that delicious enfant terrible Clifford”, and reproduces the maxim with a slight alteration, since he writes “for every one” where Clifford had written “for anyone”. He also quotes Huxley, who saw in the habit of not pretending to believe what one has no reason to believe the last dyke against the worst immorality. He announces his thesis at the outset: to defend “the lawfulness of voluntarily adopted faith”.

The argument rests on a few distinctions. A hypothesis is live or dead according to whether or not it presents itself as a real possibility to the person to whom it is proposed: belief in the Mahdi is a dead hypothesis for a Yale student, a live one for an Arab, and the liveness of a hypothesis is measured by the willingness to act. An option, that is, a choice between two hypotheses, may be live or dead, forced or avoidable, momentous or trivial; James calls genuine an option that is “of the forced, living, and momentous kind”. And his thesis fits in one sentence, which he puts in italics: “Our passional nature not only lawfully may, but must, decide an option between propositions, whenever it is a genuine option that cannot by its nature be decided on intellectual grounds.” For to say in such a case “do not decide, leave the question open”, he adds, is still a passional decision, which runs the same risk of missing the truth.

Pascal's wager comes in at the heart of the argument, and James treats it with a severity surprising in a defender of faith. He gives a faithful paraphrase of it, down to “Cela vous fera croire et vous abêtira”, this will make you believe and will dull you, and he judges that religious faith, when it expresses itself in the language of the gaming table, is down to its last trumps. The wager is valid, in his view, only for someone who already has a tendency to believe: “Certainly no Turk ever took to masses and holy water on its account.” Yet he quotes Pascal a second time, to agree with him: “Le coeur a ses raisons”, he writes, “que la raison ne connaît pas”, the heart has its reasons which reason does not know. The small slip in the quotation says everything. James takes from Pascal the right of the heart; he leaves him the calculation.

The answer to Clifford starts from a distinction. There are, James says, two commandments in the pursuit of truth, and they are not the same: “Believe truth! Shun error!” Clifford has chosen to sacrifice the first to the second, to prefer ignorance to any risk of error. This choice is not that of pure reason; it is that of a particular passion, the horror of being duped: “He may be critical of many of his desires and fears, but this fear he slavishly obeys.” James too has a horror of being duped; but he can believe that worse things than that may befall a man, and Clifford's exhortation has for him “a thoroughly fantastic sound”: it is like a general telling his soldiers that it is better never to go into battle than to risk a single wound. He then applies these distinctions to the religious hypothesis, which he reduces to two affirmations: the best things are the more eternal things, and we are better off even now if we believe the first. For someone to whom this hypothesis is live, the option is momentous and it is forced, for scepticism does not avoid it: it misses the good as surely as a refusal would. The sceptic is therefore not a man without passion; he is a gambler betting against religion, and James puts the question to him: “Dupery for dupery, what proof is there that dupery through hope is so much worse than dupery through fear?” He concludes that a rule of thinking which would absolutely prevent him from acknowledging certain truths if they existed would be an irrational rule, and he claims for each of us the right to believe, “at our own risk”, any hypothesis live enough to tempt our will.

James has often been reproached with opening the door to every credulity, not without grounds: the right to believe what tempts our will can cover the fanatic as well as the mystic. But the end of the essay changes its meaning. The right he claims is not a right to impose: “No one of us ought to issue vetoes to the other, nor should we bandy words of abuse”; we ought, on the contrary, to respect one another's freedom of thought delicately and profoundly, and only on that condition, he says, shall we have “that spirit of inner tolerance without which all our outer tolerance is soulless”. With regard to the criterion, James's position is clear: it proposes no absolute in whose name the sacrifice of the individual could be demanded; it defends each person's right to take a risk for himself, and denies anyone the right to take it for others. James is one of the few authors in the series to defend faith without ever asking the unbeliever to share it.

The comparison with Kierkegaard brings out what is his own. The Dane had defined faith as the contradiction between the infinite passion of inwardness and objective uncertainty (see the section on Kierkegaard). James says much the same thing about the structure of faith: a passion that decides where the intellect cannot. But he removes from it the paradox, the offence and the fear. In Kierkegaard, faith is a leap that reason can only find absurd; in James, it is a reasonable risk, comparable to that of a man who asks a woman to marry him without being sure she will be an angel, an image James himself uses. Kant, for his part, had abolished knowledge to make room for faith (B XXX, see the section on Kant). James abolishes nothing. He does not limit reason to save faith; he observes that reason leaves open options that life obliges us to decide. It is an empiricist's faith, without dogma, which each person can revise; and that is why it lends itself so well to modern spirituality, whose charter he wrote in the *Varieties*.

Twentieth-century analytic philosophy took up the quarrel, and at first settled it in Clifford's favour. Bertrand Russell (1872-1970) is his great heir, and admits it. In the 1927 lecture that gives its title to *Why I Am Not a Christian*, he examines the traditional proofs of the existence of God and finds them all wanting; but he knows that arguments are not the driving force of belief, which rests in his view on fear and ignorance: fear of the mysterious, ignorance of natural causes, fear of death. He says so, in another essay in the same collection, “Has Religion Made Useful Contributions to Civilization?”, in a formula that ties English analytic philosophy directly to the first regime of this essay: “My own view on religion is that of Lucretius. I regard it as a disease born of fear and as a source of untold misery to the human race.” His *Sceptical Essays* (1928) open with a doctrine he presents, ironically, as wildly paradoxical: “that it is undesirable to believe a proposition when there is no ground whatever for supposing it true”. This is Clifford in a more modest key.

Russell's most famous image comes from an article written in 1952 at the request of the London magazine *Illustrated*, “Is There a God?”, which the magazine did not publish and which first appeared in 1997, in vol. 11 of the *Collected Papers*. If I maintained, he writes, that a china teapot revolves about the Sun between the Earth and Mars, too small to be seen even by the most powerful telescopes, no one could refute me; but if I concluded that it is presumptuous to doubt it, I would be thought absurd. If, on the other hand, the existence of this teapot were affirmed in ancient books, taught every Sunday as sacred truth and instilled into children at school, doubt would become “a mark of eccentricity”, earning the doubter the attentions of the psychiatrist in an enlightened age “or of the Inquisitor in an earlier time”. The argument bears on the burden of proof, and it strikes true against those who think they have won because they cannot be refuted. But it meets the limit James had marked in advance. The teapot is a trivial hypothesis: nothing depends on it, and one can wait indefinitely without losing anything. The religious hypothesis, for someone to whom it is live, is momentous and forced. Russell answers Clifford, not James. And his final sentence says something other than he thought: the teapot becomes a serious matter only when an institution makes it sacred and punishes those who doubt it. The problem is not belief; it is the Inquisitor.

The most serious critique of religious belief that analytic philosophy has produced bears, moreover, on evil, and returns to the quarrel of Leibniz, Bayle and Voltaire. John Leslie Mackie (1917-1981), in “Evil and Omnipotence” (1955), argues that the problem of evil shows not only that faith lacks support but that it contradicts itself: “Here it can be shown, not that religious beliefs lack rational support, but that they are positively irrational, that several parts of the essential theological doctrine are inconsistent with one another.” An omnipotent and wholly good God could not allow evil to exist; evil exists; one of the attributes must give way. This is the argument of Epicurus that Bayle had put back into circulation. Alvin Plantinga answered it in the 1970s with the “free will defence”: to remove the contradiction, it suffices that a world containing free creatures and moral evil could be better than a world without freedom. Mackie conceded this in *The Miracle of Theism* (1982), a posthumous book: “we can concede that the problem of evil does not, after all, show that the central doctrines of theism are logically inconsistent with one another”; whether this is a real solution, he adds, is another question. The debate then shifted to probabilistic versions, which no longer claim to demonstrate inconsistency but improbability. The result is that of the classical quarrel: the coherence of faith can be saved; its plausibility cannot be demonstrated. The objection that remains is not logical; it is that of Ivan Karamazov, who does not hold the final harmony to be impossible, but refuses to consent to it.

Plantinga did more: with Nicholas Wolterstorff and William Alston, he challenged Clifford's very principle in *Faith and Rationality* (1983), the founding collection of Reformed epistemology. Evidentialism presupposes classical foundationalism: a belief is rational only if it is self-evident, evident to the senses, incorrigible, or inferred from beliefs that are. Yet this criterion condemns most of our most reasonable beliefs, that the world has existed for more than five minutes, that other people have minds, that our memories do not deceive us, for which we have no evidence in Clifford's sense; and it does not itself satisfy its own conditions, since it is neither self-evident nor demonstrated. Plantinga concludes that belief in God can be, like belief in the existence of other minds, a “properly basic” belief, one that need not be inferred from other beliefs in order to be rational. He finds its source in a theme of Calvin: there is said to be in man “a kind of faculty or cognitive mechanism, what Calvin calls sensus divinitatis or a sense of divinity, which in a wide variety of circumstances produces in us beliefs about God”. Wolterstorff gave this position its legal formula, which reverses Clifford's: our beliefs, he writes, “are innocent until proved guilty, not guilty until proved innocent”. And Plantinga, in *Warranted Christian Belief* (2000), pushes the argument to the point of maintaining that one cannot reasonably hold religious belief to be without warrant without first having shown that the model he proposes for it, that of a *sensus divinitatis* created by God, is false.

The classic objection to Reformed epistemology is that of the Great Pumpkin, borrowed from a comic strip in which a child believes that a giant pumpkin visits the most deserving pumpkin patch every Halloween: if belief in God can be properly basic, why not that one? Plantinga replies that one can rule out certain candidates for the status of basic belief without possessing a general criterion, just as one recognizes that Lewis Carroll's line “T'was brillig; and the slithy toves did gyre and gymble in the wabe” is meaningless without possessing a general criterion of meaning. But the objection has a more serious form, which Clifford had already formulated in setting the Prophet against the Buddha. If belief in God is properly basic for the Reformed Christian, the absence of God can be so for the Buddhist, the Qur'an for the Muslim, and each finds himself equally justified. The parity argument defends the believer against the charge of irrationality; it gives him no reason to prefer his faith to his neighbour's. Plantinga saved the rationality of belief at the price of its universality.

This debate first of all shifts the question of this essay. The authors of our series asked whether one can leave religion without founding another; Clifford, James and Plantinga ask by what title one holds what one holds, whether it be a religion, an unbelief or a wisdom. The question is broader and fairer, because it is addressed to atheists too: did Russell, who reproached believers with believing without reason, have reason to believe that religion is a disease born of fear? Above all he believed Lucretius. Next, the three positions are not of equal worth with regard to the sacrifice criterion, and their ranking is not the one we might expect. Clifford's evidentialism, which passes for the enemy of faith, is in reality the enemy of refoundations, even if its vocabulary of purity, profanation and fanaticism shows the slope that lies in wait for every morality of belief: making Truth an absolute in whose name souls are judged. James's position is, of all of them, the closest to what this essay calls an exit without refoundation: it makes neither faith nor doubt sacred. Reformed epistemology, finally, renders a service its adversaries ought to acknowledge: it shows that evidentialism is not a neutral position but one option among others, with its own commitments. Yet it fails where James succeeds, because it defends a particular tradition instead of defending a common right.

I therefore propose to combine the first two, which translates the criterion of this essay into the order of knowledge: we have the right to believe, at our own risk, where reason leaves the question open, and that is James; this right ceases where belief engages the lives of others without their having chosen it, and that is Clifford, with the lesson of the shipowner. James, the man who defended the right to believe, had understood that one leaves religion only by renouncing the imposition of anything at all, unbelief included.

### Transition: from explanation to ordeal

The human sciences have explained religion; they have not dissolved it. They have shown that belief has universal springs, cognitive, existential and social, that its intensity varies with the security of existence, and that the need it satisfied, for meaning, for bonds and for protection, survives God. But explanation leaves untouched the question that the twentieth century poses with a new urgency. If religion is not an error that science corrects, but a human response to a human condition, what becomes of that response when God falls silent? The thinkers of the next part are no longer content to explain: they put religion to the test, from within and from without, believers and unbelievers together. All of them had read, directly or not, the explainers of religion. None of them believed that explanation was enough.
