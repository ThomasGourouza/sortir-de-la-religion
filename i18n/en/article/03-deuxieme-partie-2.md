### Celsus and Origen: the first critique of Christianity, and its answer

Around 178, under Marcus Aurelius, a Platonist philosopher of whom we know almost nothing, Celsus, wrote against the Christians a treatise he entitled *Alêthês logos*, *The True Word*. The book is lost. We know it because Origen, around 248, at the request of his patron Ambrose, refuted it sentence by sentence in the eight books of *Against Celsus*, quoting his adversary at length. Origen says as much in the very preface: he is answering the treatise that was sent to him, which he does not think anyone, however little advanced in philosophy, will accept as a “true word”, as Celsus entitled it (*Against Celsus*, preface, 4). A complete paradox: the first great anti-Christian pamphlet has reached us only through the piety of a theologian who wanted to destroy it. It is an attested response, the oldest in this essay.

Celsus's accusations fall into four families. The first targets the person of Jesus: Celsus has a Jew speak, who reproaches Jesus for having invented his virgin birth, for having been born in a village of Judea to a poor spinner driven out by her husband, a carpenter, for adultery; Jesus is said to have hired himself out as a servant in Egypt, to have learned marvellous powers there, and to have come back to proclaim himself a god (*Against Celsus* I, 28). The father is said to be a soldier named Panthera (I, 32). Celsus then compares the miracles of Jesus to the tricks of the charlatans who, in the marketplaces, for a few obols, drive out demons and cure diseases (I, 68). This is the magician thesis, which denies the divinity of Jesus, not his existence.

The second family targets faith itself. Celsus reproaches the Christians for wanting neither to give nor to receive a reason for what they believe, and for repeating: do not examine, believe, your faith will save you; and he compares them to the begging priests of Cybele and to soothsayers (I, 9). The third targets their audience. According to Celsus, the Christians say: let no educated, wise or prudent man come to us, but if anyone is ignorant, foolish, uneducated, let him come with confidence; which shows, he concludes, that they neither want nor are able to win over anyone but the simple, the nobodies, slaves, women and children (III, 44). The fourth, the most interesting for us, accuses the Christians of having misunderstood Plato: whatever is good in Scripture, Plato said better, without bombast and without promises (VI, 1), and the Christians who speak of a “supercelestial” God have merely misread the *Phaedrus* (VI, 19).

Celsus thus anticipated almost the whole of the modern critique. The thesis of credulity, which demands reasons, is that of Voltaire and d'Holbach. The social thesis, which makes Christianity a religion of slaves and the ignorant, will be Nietzsche's, turned into an accusation of ressentiment, and Engels's, turned into a eulogy of the oppressed. The thesis of borrowing, which makes Christianity a degraded Platonism, announces Nietzsche's formula quoted at the head of this part. And the scandalous genealogy of Jesus, son of a soldier and a repudiated woman, will be taken up by medieval Jewish polemic, then by the libertines. A single argument is missing, and its absence is remarkable: Celsus never claims that Jesus did not exist; on the contrary, he despises him because he lived and was crucified. The first adversary of Christianity, who lived a century and a half after the crucifixion and had every interest in denying whatever he could deny, does not deny that.

Origen's answer ranks among the great texts of Christian thought because it accepts the adversary's ground. On credulity, Origen answers that, if it were possible for all men to leave the business of life and devote themselves to philosophy, no other method should be followed but that one; but that this is impossible, and that faith is the path of those who cannot philosophize, without Christianity thereby renouncing inquiry and interpretation (I, 9). On the audience, he answers that the ambassadors of truth must seek to win over the greatest number, the simple as well as the intelligent, barbarians as well as Greeks, and that those who turn away from the ignorant as from slaves incapable of following a polished discourse strangely restrict the public good (VI, 1).

The climax of the dispute is an exchange about the *Timaeus*. Celsus sends the Christians back to Plato as a better teacher of theology, and quotes the sentence Justin had already quoted: it is hard to find the maker and father of this universe, and having found him, impossible to make him known to all; see, he adds, how divine men seek the way of truth, and how well Plato knew that it was impossible for all to walk in it (VII, 42). Origen answers that these words of Plato are noble and admirable, but that Scripture shows a still greater love of humankind in the Logos of God, who was in the beginning with God and who became flesh, in order to reveal to all what, according to Plato, it was impossible to make known to all (*ibid.*). The whole difference between Platonism and Christianity lies there. For Celsus, the knowledge of God is the privilege of an elite, and the multitude must keep to the cults of the city. For Origen, it is offered to all, because God himself came down to them.

Still, one must measure what Origen grants his adversary. Origen is himself a philosopher trained in Alexandria, a pupil, according to Porphyry, of the same Ammonius who was Plotinus's teacher. He reads Scripture according to Philo's method, on three levels, body, soul and spirit, and does not shrink from theses that the Church would later judge unacceptable: the pre-existence of souls, or the hope that all beings, perhaps even the devil, will in the end be restored in God. These doctrines, or those attributed to him, were condemned in the sixth century, long after his death, and most of his Greek work was lost for that reason. The first great Christian theologian, who had answered the first great critique, thus ended up a posthumous heretic. The lesson is twofold: Christian theology was built by borrowing from Greek philosophy its weapons against Greek philosophy, as Celsus accused it of doing; and the institution that came out of it ended up condemning the man who had best defended it, for having thought too much. Eckhart, also condemned after his death, would meet the same fate: the critique of religion has often begun inside theology, among those whom the Church afterwards rejected.

Celsus, moreover, is not a sceptic. He believes in a supreme God, inaccessible, whom only the wise approach, and in a host of lesser divinities, demons in the Greek sense, who govern peoples and places and to whom it is right to render the honours that custom prescribes. This is therefore not the critique of an unbeliever, but that of a man who cares about the hierarchy of the divine and about traditional piety, and who sees in a religion claiming universality a threat to the order of the world. That is why it seems to me wrong to make him the ancestor of modern atheists. He is the ancestor of those who defend religion for the order it secures, from Voltaire to Maurras and down to the later Onfray, who defends a Christian civilization without sharing its faith. The first adversary of Christianity was a religious conservative.

The dispute also concerns the city, and in that way it bears directly on our question. At the end of his treatise, Celsus adjures the Christians to help the emperor with all their strength, to work with him to uphold justice, to fight for him and, if need be, under his command; and he urges them to accept the public offices required to uphold the laws and religion (*Against Celsus* VIII, 73 and 75). Celsus does not ask the Christians to believe in the gods; he asks them to be citizens, that is, to take part in the civil religion without which, he thinks, the Empire dissolves. Origen answers that the Christians help the emperor by their prayers better than soldiers by their arms, and above all that the Christians recognize, in each city, the existence of another homeland, founded by the Logos of God, the Church, whose offices they prefer to serve (VIII, 73 and 75).

This answer gives birth to an idea that would govern the whole political history of the West: the separation of the religious community from the political community. For Celsus as for Plato, religion is a matter for the city; for Origen, there are two cities, and the higher is not the emperor's. Augustine would give this idea its finished form in the *City of God*. It has two faces. It frees conscience from the grip of the State, and Bayle, Locke and the Enlightenment would be its distant heirs. But it also creates a second power, the Church, which would in turn be able to demand what the city used to demand.

Who is right? Justice must first be done to Celsus, whom the Christian tradition has treated as a slanderer. His arguments on credulity and on the borrowings from Platonism are strong, his information on the Jewish polemic of his time precious. But his position rests on the Platonic presupposition par excellence, the division between the philosophers who know and the multitude who believe what the city prescribes for them: Celsus is the last heir of Plato the legislator. Origen, for his part, opens the door to spiritual equality: the God of the philosophers becomes everyone's God, and the slave can know as much as Plato. But he closes, at least in germ, the door to freedom of thought: if the truth is given to all by revelation, the one who refuses it is no longer an ignorant person to be enlightened, he becomes a rebel. The dispute is therefore symmetrical. Celsus defends a political absolute, the gods of the city, to which the individual must sacrifice; Origen defends a revealed absolute, which Christian power would soon impose with the same rigour. Neither of them leaves religion. But from their dispute are born the very idea of a critique of religion by reason and the idea, just as modern, that it must be possible to tell the truth to all.

### Porphyry and Augustine: the last adversary and the first synthesizer

The dispute between Celsus and Origen has a sequel, and it takes place in the same school. Porphyry of Tyre (c. 234-c. 305), disciple and editor of Plotinus, knew Origen in his youth and judged him severely. In a fragment preserved by Eusebius, he relates that Origen, a pupil of Ammonius, the most learned philosopher of his time, had drawn from his teacher a great knowledge of the sciences, but that, in the conduct of his life, he had taken the opposite road, passing from Greek culture to “barbarian audacity” (Eusebius, *Ecclesiastical History* VI, 19). For a third-century Platonist, Christianity is not a philosophy; it is a barbarism that stole philosophy.

Of his *Against the Christians*, written around 270 according to the most common dating (others place it around 300), only fragments quoted by his refuters remain. The most famous concerns the book of Daniel. Jerome reports, in the prologue to his commentary, that Porphyry devoted his twelfth book to denying that Daniel was written by the prophet whose name it bears, and to maintaining that it was composed in Judea in the time of Antiochus Epiphanes, so that Daniel did not predict the future but recounted the past (Jerome, *Commentary on Daniel*, prologue). This is also the conclusion of modern historical-critical exegesis, which dates the composition of Daniel to the years 167-164 BCE. Porphyry was right fifteen centuries before Spinoza and before David Friedrich Strauss, and his method is already theirs: whatever looks too much like a fulfilled prophecy was written after the event.

Porphyry is nevertheless neither an atheist nor even an enemy of Jesus, and that is what makes his position instructive. Augustine quotes, in the *City of God*, a passage from his *Philosophy from Oracles*: the gods, Porphyry writes, have proclaimed Christ most pious and made immortal, and they remember him with praise; but they say that the Christians are polluted, contaminated and entangled in error (“Christum enim dii piissimum pronuntiauerunt et inmortalem factum[…] Christianos uero pollutos[…] et contaminatos et errore implicatos esse dicunt”) (Augustine, *De civitate Dei* XIX, 23). Jesus is a sage whom his disciples betrayed by making him a god: the thesis that Renan would take up and that Nietzsche would radicalize by setting Jesus against Paul was first formulated by a Neoplatonist.

Augustine (354-430) answers Porphyry, and his answer settles the form Western theology would take for a thousand years. We have seen that he found among the Platonists the equivalent of the prologue of John, the Word with God, but not the Incarnation (Part One). His whole dialogue with them lies in this lack: they saw the goal without finding the way; they know the Logos, they refuse the Incarnation.

Book VIII of the *City of God* draws from this encounter a hierarchy of philosophies. Augustine takes up there Varro's distinction between three theologies, the fabulous theology of the poets, the civil theology of cities and the natural theology of the philosophers, and it is with the philosophers alone that he wants to argue, because if wisdom is God, “uerus philosophus est amator Dei”, the true philosopher is the one who loves God (*De civitate Dei* VIII, 1). Among them, the Platonists prevail: “Nulli nobis quam isti propius accesserunt”, none have come closer to us than they (VIII, 5). And Augustine adds that he argues with the Platonists because their books are the best known, without ruling out that other sages, of any nation whatever, may have thought the same about God (VIII, 10). One recognizes Justin's seminal Logos, become a victor's courtesy.

Augustine does more, and it is perhaps his most lasting legacy for spirituality: he shifts the place where God is sought. Plotinus sought the One by climbing above the soul; Augustine seeks him within the soul, and he finds a formula that expresses both movements at once: “tu autem eras interior intimo meo et superior summo meo”, you were more inward than my innermost self, and higher than my highest (*Confessions* III, 6, 11). The *Confessions* are the first great book in which a man tells his own life as the path of this inner search. It has been said that modern inwardness, that of Descartes seeking certainty in the *cogito* and that of Rousseau seeking religion in the heart, is its heir. If so, the spirituality of exercise that Part Twelve will defend as an exit from religion also has a Christian genealogy, and Hadot knew it, recalling what Christian spiritual exercises owed to the philosophical exercises of antiquity.

Augustine also fixes the relation between faith and reason that the whole of scholasticism would take up. He reads in the Latin translation of Isaiah that unless one believes, one will not understand, and he draws a method from it: believe in order to understand, then understand what one believes. Anselm of Canterbury would make it, in the eleventh century, the motto of a faith seeking understanding, *fides quaerens intellectum*, and it is within this framework that he would formulate the argument Descartes takes up in the fifth *Meditation*. The God of the philosophers of the classical age was therefore not born with Descartes: he is the God of Augustine and Anselm, from whom Descartes removes the prior faith so as to keep only the proof.

The Augustinian synthesis produces the God of whom this whole essay has been speaking from the beginning. It is the God of Plato and Plotinus, immutable, simple, source of being, of truth and of happiness, but endowed with what the Platonists denied him: a will, a history, a love, a grace, a wrath. The Good beyond being becomes a Father who judges and saves. This fusion of the God of the philosophers and the God of the Bible is the major fact of the intellectual history of the Christian West, and the moderns would undo it from two sides. Pascal would undo it from above, by setting the God of Abraham against the God of the philosophers; Heidegger would undo it from below, by showing that the God of metaphysics, cause of itself and ground of beings, has nothing divine about him. Between the two, Spinoza would keep the God of the philosophers while removing from him everything the Bible had given him, and Feuerbach would give back to man everything Augustine had given to God.

Augustine is also the author who, against the Donatists, justified recourse to the secular arm to bring schismatics back into the Church, commenting on the “compel them to come in” of the parable (Lc 14, 23); this is a fact established by his correspondence, which I do not quote here for lack of having reread it. The God of the philosophers, become a personal God and the God of the Empire, can henceforth demand. Celsus defended the gods of the city against the religion of slaves; two centuries later, the religion of slaves has become the religion of the city, and it has made the law of the *Laws* its own. The birth of Christianity thus ends in a refoundation in the strict sense, the one Plato had dreamed of and that the Christian emperors, from Constantine to Theodosius, carried out: a rational theology, armed by the State. The whole history of the modern critique of religion would be, in many respects, the history of the defeat of this alliance.
