## Part Five: feeling and concept

Kant had believed he could hold religion at bay by lodging it within the boundaries of mere reason: a moral faith, without dogma claiming to know, without worship claiming to please God. Religion was saved because it was reduced, and it is this reduction that the next generation finds intolerable, from two opposite sides. Some will say that Kant missed religion because he folded it back onto morality, whereas it is first of all a matter of sensibility, of intuition, of a lived relation to the infinite: this is Schleiermacher. Others will say that Kant missed it because he forbade reason to know the absolute, whereas religion already says, in the form of images, what philosophy can think in the form of concepts: this is Hegel. The first opens the way to the modern spiritualities of experience, the second to the philosophies that claim to preserve religion by surpassing it. From their clash, and then from Strauss's application of the Hegelian method to the Gospel narratives, the Hegelian left will emerge, and everything that follows, from Feuerbach to Comte, answers their question: is religion first a feeling or first a truth?

### Friedrich Schleiermacher (1768-1834): religion as sense and taste for the infinite

In 1799, in Berlin, the *Über die Religion. Reden an die Gebildeten unter ihren Verächtern* (*On Religion: Speeches to Its Cultured Despisers*) appeared without the author's name. Their author was a young pastor at the Charité hospital, a familiar of Friedrich Schlegel's Romantic circle, and the title already states the whole design: the book is not addressed to believers but to those who despise religion in the name of culture. It does not seek to prove God to them, but to show them that what they despise is something other than religion: a failed metaphysics and a catechism morality, which they are right to despise, but which are not religion.

The fundamental thought fits into one line of the second speech, the most famous in the book: “Praxis ist Kunst, Spekulation ist Wissenschaft, Religion ist Sinn und Geschmack fürs Unendliche”, practice is an art, speculation is a science, religion is sense and taste for the infinite. And a little earlier: “Ihr Wesen ist weder Denken noch Handeln, sondern Anschauung und Gefühl”, its essence is neither thinking nor acting, but intuition and feeling. Kant had brought religion back to action (morality); rationalist metaphysics had brought it back to thought (the proofs of God). Schleiermacher gives it a third province, irreducible to the other two: experience. “Religion haben, heißt das Universum anschauen”, to have religion is to intuit the universe.

The word *Anschauung* does not designate a mystical vision in the sense of an ecstasy cut off from ordinary life. It designates the subject's receptivity to what exceeds it, the moment when, in any perception whatever, the finite lets itself be grasped as an expression of the infinite. Schleiermacher describes it with the precision of a phenomenologist before the term existed: it is the first instant of every perception, before intuition and feeling separate, when sense and its object are, so to speak, fused into each other. He calls it “Jener erste geheimnisvolle Augenblick”, that first and mysterious instant, and he adds that it is indescribable and passes very quickly. Religion consists in recapturing this instant and recognizing it in the religious activity of the soul.

Three consequences follow.

The first concerns God. If religion is intuition of the universe, the idea of God is only one modality of it among others, one way of representing to oneself the unity of what one intuits. In 1799 Schleiermacher draws from this a conclusion that caused scandal and that he softened in later editions: “so müßt Ihr auch zugeben, daß eine Religion ohne Gott besser sein kann, als ein andre mit Gott”, you must also admit that a religion without God can be better than another with God. One could hardly announce more plainly what the twentieth century will call spirituality without God. The hero of this religion without God is named: Spinoza. The second speech pays him a tribute that has remained famous: “Opfert mit mir ehrerbietig eine Locke den Manen des heiligen verstoßenen Spinoza!”, offer with me, reverently, a lock of hair to the shades of the holy, outcast Spinoza! And further on: “voller Religion war Er und voll heiligen Geistes”, he was full of religion and full of holy spirit. The atheist par excellence in the eyes of Europe becomes, from a pastor's pen, the saint of true religion: what is impiety in the light of dogma becomes piety in the light of experience.

The second consequence concerns Scripture and the institution. If religion is a living experience, the sacred text is only its trace. Schleiermacher has an admirable sentence here: “Jede heilige Schrift ist nur ein Mausoleum der Religion ein Denkmal, daß ein großer Geist da war, der nicht mehr da ist”, every holy scripture is only a mausoleum of religion, a monument attesting that a great spirit was there who is there no longer. And he concludes: “Nicht der hat Religion, der an eine heilige Schrift glaubt, sondern der welcher keiner bedarf, und wohl selbst eine machen könnte”, the one who has religion is not the one who believes in a holy scripture, but the one who needs none and could well write one himself. The miracle undergoes the same displacement: it is no longer a departure from the laws of nature, but the religious name for any event whatever, once it is perceived in its relation to the infinite. Everything is a miracle for whoever has the religious sense, and nothing for whoever lacks it.

The third consequence concerns eternity. Religion does not promise a life after death; it gives, here, an eternity that is not a duration. Schleiermacher says so in a formula that Spinoza could have signed and that Wittgenstein will rediscover in the *Tractatus* (6.4311; the comparison is mine): “Mitten in der Endlichkeit Eins werden mit dem Unendlichen und ewig sein in einem Augenblick, das ist die Unsterblichkeit der Religion”, in the midst of finitude to become one with the infinite and to be eternal in a moment, that is the immortality of religion.

The *Reden* are not a mere Romantic effusion. Schleiermacher is a rigorous theologian, and his great dogmatics, *Der christliche Glaube* (*The Christian Faith*, 1821-1822, second edition 1830-1831), will systematize the intuition of 1799 in a soberer form: piety is defined there as the feeling of absolute dependence, *schlechthinniges Abhängigkeitsgefühl* (1830 spelling: *schlechthiniges*), the Leitsatz of § 4 stating that we know ourselves to be “schlechthin abhängig”, absolutely dependent, that is, in relation with God. The passage from the intuition of the universe to the feeling of dependence is not without significance. The intuition of 1799 was active, almost aesthetic; the feeling of dependence is passive, it expresses creatureliness. But the principle remains the same: religion has its seat in the immediate feeling of self, not in knowledge or in the will.

This position is, I believe, the first modern attempt to leave *institutional* religion without leaving *lived* religion, and as such it prefigures the whole contemporary shift from religion to spirituality. Schleiermacher dismisses dogma, the text, the miracle in the vulgar sense, personal immortality and even, in 1799, the necessity of God; he keeps experience. Not as a private matter: the last speeches insist on the religious community, on the Church as the free communication of intuitions, and on the plurality of positive religions, each being a singular way of intuiting the universe. He does not found a new religion; he claims to unveil the essence of all of them.

The objection will come from two sides. Hegel will reproach him for having reduced religion to a feeling, that is, to what is most subjective, most arbitrary and most animal in man. Feuerbach, later, will take the lesson in the other direction: if religion is feeling, and if feeling is human, then God is human feeling objectified. Schleiermacher unintentionally opened the door to anthropological reduction: the feeling of the infinite, detached from all objective truth, can always be reinterpreted as a feeling of self. Schleiermacher must be given his best defence here: he never said that the infinite was a product of feeling; he said that feeling was the place where the infinite gives itself. But his theory contains no means of distinguishing these two theses. This is the structural weakness of every spirituality of experience, which we shall find again in William James and in Rudolf Otto: experience proves that one feels, it does not prove what one feels.

> The *Speeches on Religion* appeared in 1799; the three texts that follow frame them within ten years and say the same thing in verse. In Martha's garden, Gretchen puts to Faust the question that German has since called the Gretchenfrage: “Nun sag, wie hast du's mit der Religion?”, tell me, how do you stand with religion? Faust answers with a refusal of names: “Erfüll davon dein Herz, so groß es ist, / Und wenn du ganz in dem Gefühle selig bist, / Nenn es dann, wie du willst, / Nenn's Glück! Herz! Liebe! And Gretchen: “Ungefähr sagt das der Pfarrer auch”, the pastor says roughly that too.
>
> Wordsworth, the year before the *Speeches*, felt above the Wye “A presence that disturbs me with the joy / Of elevated thoughts; a sense sublime / Of something far more deeply interfused, / Whose dwelling is the light of setting suns, / And the round ocean, and the living air, / And the blue sky, and in the mind of man”. Hölderlin, two years earlier, opened *Hyperion* on the same wish: “Eines zu sein mit Allem, das ist Leben der Gottheit, das ist der Himmel des Menschen. Eines zu sein mit Allem, was lebt, in seliger Selbstvergessenheit wiederzukehren ins All der Natur, das ist der Gipfel der Gedanken und Freuden”, to be one with all, that is the life of the godhead, that is the heaven of man; to be one with all that lives, to return in blissful self-forgetfulness into the All of nature, that is the summit of thoughts and joys.
>
> Why here: “Sinn und Geschmack fürs Unendliche”, “Anschauung und Gefühl”: the generation of 1798 wrote Schleiermacher's religion before him, in Germany and in England, and Goethe gave its refutation in the same scene. Gretchen's “the pastor says roughly that too” is Hegel's objection, a feeling that has no criterion; and Faust, who does not believe, uses this speech to seduce the girl who does. The regime of experience is born in a garden, between a man who refuses to name and a woman who asks for a name.

### Georg Wilhelm Friedrich Hegel (1770-1831): religion surpassed and preserved

Hegel arrived in Berlin in 1818; Schleiermacher was already teaching there, and the two men, colleagues at the university, cordially detested each other. Their quarrel is not a faculty anecdote: it sets against each other the two great answers to Kant. To understand it, one must start from Hegel's thesis on religion.

Hegel does not hold religion to be false: it says the true, and even the absolute true, but in an inadequate form. Religion and philosophy have, in his view, the same content: the absolute, spirit, God. They differ in form: religion presents this content in the form of *Vorstellung*, representation, that is, in images, narratives, sensible figures and external relations: a Father who sends his Son, a Son who dies and rises again, a Spirit who descends upon a community. Philosophy presents the same content in the form of the *Begriff*, the concept, that is, in thought that grasps the inner necessity of what the image sets side by side. The *Vorlesungen über die Philosophie der Religion* (*Lectures on the Philosophy of Religion*), delivered in Berlin in 1821, 1824, 1827 and 1831 and published after his death, say so unambiguously: there is no question whatever of philosophy overturning religion, for “vielmehr ist die Religion eben der wahrhafte Inhalt nur in Form der Vorstellung”, religion is rather precisely the true content, only in the form of representation. And Hegel adds that humanity did not have to wait for philosophy to receive the consciousness of truth.

The word that sums up this relation is *Aufhebung*, untranslatable because it unites three senses that English keeps apart: to cancel, to preserve, to raise up. Philosophy “sublates” religion: it cancels its representational form, preserves its content, and raises it to the form of the concept. This is a first figure of our question: Hegel leaves religion, since the religious form is declared inferior and provisional, but while preserving it entirely, since its whole content passes into philosophy. Neither a naive believer nor a critic of religion, he wants an exit that is a preservation. The whole Hegelian left will be born from the question whether this preservation is sincere.

Within this framework belongs an early text whose legacy is immense. In 1802, in *Glauben und Wissen* (*Faith and Knowledge*), published in the *Kritisches Journal der Philosophie* that he edited with Schelling in Jena, Hegel attacks the philosophies of Kant, Jacobi and Fichte, which he accuses of having abandoned the knowledge of God to faith and thereby separated the finite from the infinite. The conclusion of the text formulates, eighty years before *The Gay Science*, the death of God. The pure concept, Hegel writes, must designate the “unendlichen Schmerz”, the infinite grief, on which the religion of modern times rests, namely the feeling that “Gott selbst ist tot”, God himself is dead. This feeling, he adds, had only been expressed empirically by Pascal, whose words he quotes about a nature that “marque partout un Dieu perdu et dans l'homme et hors de l'homme”, everywhere marks a lost God, both within man and outside man. Philosophy must restore, the same sentence goes on, the “spekulativen Karfreitag”, the speculative Good Friday, “in der ganzen Wahrheit und Härte seiner Gottlosigkeit”, in the whole truth and harshness of its godlessness, so that the highest totality may rise again “in die heiterste Freiheit ihrer Gestalt”, into the most serene freedom of its shape.

Two remarks are called for. The first is philological. This “Gott selbst ist tot” is usually linked to the Lutheran Good Friday hymn *O Traurigkeit, o Herzeleid*, whose second stanza is by Johann Rist (1641). In *Glauben und Wissen*, Hegel does not quote this hymn: he quotes Pascal. In the *Lectures on the Philosophy of Religion*, on the other hand, he makes the connection himself: “Gott selbst ist todt, heißt es in jenem lutherischen Liede”. And he comments in the same place, on the death of Christ, that it is the “fürchterlichste Gedanke”, the most dreadful thought, that all that is eternal and all that is true is not, that negation is in God himself. The connection with the hymn, presented by tradition as a commentators' hypothesis, is thus made by Hegel himself, but in the *Lectures* and not in the text of 1802.

The second remark is one of substance. For Hegel, the death of God is not an event in the history of unbelief; it is a moment of the concept, and precisely the moment of negativity within the absolute itself. God dies in Christ, and this death is overcome in the resurrection, that is, in the Spirit of the community: a Good Friday that calls for an Easter Sunday. When Nietzsche takes up the formula, he will keep its grief and remove its resurrection. The whole history of the critique of religion in the nineteenth century is played out, on my reading, in the interval between Hegel's speculative Good Friday and the murder without resurrection of the madman of *The Gay Science*.

The quarrel with Schleiermacher is an attested response. In 1822, Hegel wrote a preface to the book of his disciple Hermann Friedrich Wilhelm Hinrichs, *Die Religion im inneren Verhältnisse zur Wissenschaft* (*Religion in Its Inner Relation to Science*). The first volume of Schleiermacher's *Glaubenslehre* had just appeared, with its definition of piety by the feeling of dependence. Hegel did not name his colleague, but all Berlin understood. If religion, he writes, is grounded in man only on a feeling, then this feeling has no other determination than to be the feeling of his dependence, “und so wäre der Hund der beste Christ, denn er trägt dieses am stärksten in sich, und lebt vornehmlich in diesem Gefühle”, and so the dog would be the best Christian, for he bears this feeling most strongly within him and lives chiefly in this feeling. And he adds: “Auch Erlösungsgefühle hat der Hund, wenn seinem Hunger durch einen Knochen Befriedigung wird”, the dog also has feelings of redemption, when a bone brings satisfaction to his hunger. The next sentence gives the positive thesis: in religion, spirit rather finds its liberation.

The sarcasm is cruel, but it contains a serious argument against every spirituality of experience. A feeling, as such, is indeterminate; it can have any content whatever; it contains in itself no criterion of truth. The feeling of dependence is felt by the dog towards its master, and by the servile subject towards his tyrant. If religion is only feeling, nothing allows us to distinguish piety from servility. Hegel demands that religion be thought, that is, that it have a true content that reason can recognize. It is the same demand that makes him scorn, in the *Phenomenology of Spirit* (1807), the “beautiful soul” that, “um die Reinheit seines Herzens zu bewahren”, in order to preserve the purity of its heart, flees contact with actuality.

Should we side with Hegel against Schleiermacher? On the argument yes, on the conclusion no. Hegel saw clearly: a religion founded on feeling alone is defenceless against psychological reduction, and Feuerbach will prove it. But his conclusion, that philosophy possesses the truth of religion better than religion itself, generates a symmetrical and graver danger. If philosophy is the truth of religion, the philosopher is the true priest and the system the completed dogma: Hegel leaves religion only by making philosophy a higher religion, that of those who understand. This is already the structure of the Averroist division: there are those who have representation, the people, and those who have the concept, the wise. Hegel does not govern believers, but he understands them better than they understand themselves, and that is another form of sovereignty.

The ambiguity of *Aufhebung* remains fertile, and it is what breaks the school apart after 1831. If philosophy preserves the content of religion, is it religion that is saved, or philosophy that takes its place? The Hegelian right will answer that Hegel demonstrated the truth of Christianity; the left will answer that he pronounced its dissolution. The words “left” and “right” come from this debate, and it is Strauss who will introduce them, in connection with Christ.

> **En littérature.** Jean Paul, *Siebenkäs* (1796) ; Germaine de Staël, *De l'Allemagne* (1813)
>
> The “Speech of the dead Christ, from the top of the edifice of the world, that there is no God” (“Rede des toten Christus vom Weltgebäude herab, daß kein Gott sei”) is a “flower piece” inserted into the novel *Siebenkäs*: the dreamer wakes in a churchyard at midnight, the dead rise from their graves, and Christ descends upon the altar: “alle Toten riefen: »Christus! ist kein Gott?« Er antwortete: »Es ist keiner.«[…] »Ich ging durch die Welten, ich stieg in die Sonnen und flog mit den Milchstraßen durch die Wüsten des Himmels; aber es ist kein Gott.[…] ich hörte nur den ewigen Sturm, den niemand regiert«”, all the dead cried out: Christ! is there no God? He answered: There is none. I went through the worlds, I climbed into the suns and flew with the Milky Ways through the deserts of heaven; but there is no God; I heard only the eternal storm that no one governs. To the children who ask whether they have no father: “Wir sind alle Waisen, ich und ihr, wir sind ohne Vater”, we are all orphans, I and you, we are without a father. Then the sleeper wakes: “Meine Seele weinte vor Freude, daß sie wieder Gott anbeten konnte”, my soul wept for joy that it could worship God again. Mme de Staël freely translated the dream in *De l'Allemagne*, under the title “Un Songe” (A Dream): “les morts s'écrièrent : Ô Christ ! n'est-il point de Dieu ? Il répondit : Il n'en est point.[…] j'ai regardé dans l'abîme et je me suis écrié : Père, où es-tu ? mais je n'ai entendu que la pluie qui tomboit goutte à goutte dans l'abîme, et l'éternelle tempête, que nul ordre ne régit, m'a seule répondu”, the dead cried out: O Christ! is there no God? He answered: There is none; I looked into the abyss and cried out: Father, where are you? but I heard only the rain falling drop by drop into the abyss, and the eternal storm, which no order rules, alone answered me; to the dead children who ask whether they have no father, Christ answers: “Nous sommes tous orphelins, moi et vous nous n'avons point de père”, we are all orphans, I and you, we have no father, and the world collapses. She stops there, without the awakening, and comments: it seems to her beautiful “de transporter ainsi au-delà de la tombe l'horrible effroi que doit éprouver la créature privée de Dieu”, thus to carry beyond the grave the horrible dread that the creature deprived of God must feel.
>
> Why here: six years before “Gott selbst ist tot”, a novelist had put the death of God into the mouth of Christ, and it was through Mme de Staël that the image passed into France, to Nerval, to Vigny, to Musset. The difference between the two versions is the whole stake of the section: in Jean Paul, the dream ends with an awakening and a God regained, as the speculative Good Friday ends with a resurrection; in Mme de Staël, the dream remains without awakening, and it is this version that Nietzsche will keep. The death of God with sublation or without sublation: Hegel and his left, already, in two translations of the same dream.
