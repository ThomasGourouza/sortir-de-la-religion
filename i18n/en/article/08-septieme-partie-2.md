### Nietzsche (1844-1900): the death of God as a task

We must begin with Schopenhauer, for it was against his first master that Nietzsche forged his relation to the divine. The debt, as we have seen, is immense and openly claimed. In 1887, in Book V of *The Gay Science*, he pays him a tribute that bears directly on our question: “Schopenhauer war als Philosoph der erste eingeständliche und unbeugsame Atheist, den wir Deutschen gehabt haben: seine Feindschaft gegen Hegel hatte hier ihren Hintergrund”, Schopenhauer was, as a philosopher, the first avowed and inflexible atheist that we Germans have had: this was the background of his hostility towards Hegel (FW 357). And he adds that “Die Ungöttlichkeit des Daseins galt ihm als etwas Gegebenes, Greifliches, Undiskutierbares”, the non-divine character of existence was for him something given, tangible, beyond discussion.

The sentence is historically very precise. Nietzsche sees in Schopenhauer's hostility towards Hegel a religious quarrel: Hegel, with his reason in history and his final reconciliation, remained a theologian in disguise, whereas Schopenhauer had had the courage to look at a world without providence. The same § 357 goes on to make Hegel the great delayer of atheism, the one who tried to persuade the Germans of the divinity of existence by means of the historical sense. Nietzsche thus places Schopenhauer on the side of probity and Hegel on the side of the reprieve granted to God. The response is attested, and it fixes the starting point: the question of God's existence is settled; what remains is to know what one does with existence once it is admitted that it is not divine.

It is on this point that Nietzsche breaks with his master. Schopenhauer drew a moral conclusion from the absence of God: since existence has no meaning, the will to live must be denied, and pity, which recognizes the suffering of all that lives, is the supreme virtue. Nietzsche sees in this conclusion the subtlest survival of Christianity. In *Der Antichrist*, § 7, he grants Schopenhauer the diagnosis only to turn it around: “Schopenhauer war in seinem Recht damit: durch das Mitleid wird das Leben verneint, verneinungswürdiger gemacht”, Schopenhauer was right in this: through pity, life is denied, made more worthy of denial; and the next sentence defines pity as “die Praxis des Nihilismus”, the practice of nihilism. The atheist Schopenhauer had kept the Christian morality of pity and the Christian ideal of asceticism: he had left God without leaving religion, and his piety, which Feuerbach directed towards Man, was directed in his case towards renunciation.

The *Genealogie der Moral* (*On the Genealogy of Morality*, 1887) carries the critique all the way to the person. In § 6 of the third essay, Nietzsche quotes Schopenhauer's description of aesthetic contemplation, in which one celebrates “den Sabbat der Zuchthausarbeit des Wollens”, the Sabbath of the penal servitude of willing, and he asks: supposing that Schopenhauer was a hundred times right for his own person, “was wäre damit für die Einsicht ins Wesen des Schönen getan?”, what would thereby have been gained for insight into the essence of the beautiful? In § 7, he goes further: Schopenhauer would have fallen ill, would have become a pessimist, “ohne seine Feinde, ohne Hegel, das Weib, die Sinnlichkeit und den ganzen Willen zum Dasein, Dableiben”, without his enemies, without Hegel, woman, sensuality and the whole will to exist, to stay there. The pessimist needed what he denied.

*Die fröhliche Wissenschaft* (*The Gay Science*) appeared in 1882; the fifth book was added in the second edition of 1887. The gap matters: the death of God is first announced (Book III), then, five years later, *interpreted* (Book V). The usual mistake is to read § 125 as an atheist thesis. It is the opposite: a scene that holds the atheists up to ridicule.

Book III opens, in § 108, with a parable: after the Buddha's death, his shadow was still shown in a cave for centuries. “Gott ist tot: aber so wie die Art der Menschen ist, wird es vielleicht noch jahrtausendelang Höhlen geben, in denen man seinen Schatten zeigt”, God is dead, but given the way men are, there will perhaps be caves for thousands of years yet in which his shadow is shown. The whole programme is there. The death of God is not the end of religion; it is the beginning of a long survival of its shadows. That the parable starts from the Buddha is not without significance: it was through Schopenhauer that Nietzsche knew Buddhism, and it is the Schopenhauerian shadow, that of a religion without God founded on the denial of the will, that is targeted at the same time as the Christian shadow.

§ 125 stages the “tolle Mensch”, the madman, who runs into the marketplace in broad daylight with a lantern, crying that he is looking for God. Those who surround him and mock him are precisely unbelievers. It is to them that he announces: “Wir haben ihn getötet, ihr und ich!”, we have killed him, you and I. The atheists of the marketplace believe that the absence of God is a settled matter, just one more opinion; the madman knows that it is an event, and a murder, whose consequences have not yet been measured. He then asks our question: “Welche Sühnefeiern, welche heiligen Spiele werden wir erfinden müssen?”, what festivals of atonement, what sacred games shall we have to invent? Henri Albert: “Quelles expiations, quels jeux sacrés serons-nous forcés d'inventer ?[…] Ne sommes-nous pas forcés de devenir nous-mêmes des dieux pour du moins paraître dignes des dieux ?”, what atonements, what sacred games shall we be forced to invent? […] Must we not ourselves become gods, if only to appear worthy of it? The exit from religion calls of itself for a new religion. Nietzsche says so in 1882, and in the form of a question.

No one arranged the meeting between this question and Dostoevsky's scene. The madman asks whether we shall not have to become gods to be worthy of the murder; that is Kirillov's position. He asks what sacred games we shall have to invent; that is the position of the Inquisitor and of Comte. The two paths of refoundation, which Dostoevsky had embodied in two characters, Nietzsche formulates as two questions in the same paragraph. The comparison is constructed, since Nietzsche had not read *Demons* in 1882; the convergence of two minds that did not yet know each other is all the more significant.

For Nietzsche, the shadows of God are everywhere one does not look for them. In morality first: people have stopped believing in the Christian God, but they go on believing in the value of pity, equality and disinterestedness, which have meaning only through him; this is the reproach he makes to Schopenhauer, and it will be the one he makes to the socialists, the democrats and the positivists. In language next: “wir werden Gott nicht los, weil wir noch an die Grammatik glauben”, we shall not be rid of God so long as we still believe in grammar (*Götzen-Dämmerung*, “Die "Vernunft" in der Philosophie”, 5), for grammar imposes a subject behind every act, a substance behind every becoming, and God is the supreme subject. In science finally, and this is the hardest blow.

§ 344, in Book V, is entitled “Inwiefern auch wir noch fromm sind”, in what sense we too are still pious. The will to truth, the refusal to be deceived and to deceive, the demand on which science rests, is not itself scientific: it is a faith. And Nietzsche confesses: “daß auch wir Erkennenden von heute, wir Gottlosen und Antimetaphysiker, auch unser Feuer noch von dem Brande nehmen, den ein jahrtausendealter Glaube entzündet hat, jener Christen-Glaube, der auch der Glaube Platos war, daß Gott die Wahrheit ist, daß die Wahrheit göttlich ist”; Albert: “nous aussi, nous qui cherchons aujourd'hui la connaissance, nous les impies et les antimétaphysiques, nous empruntons encore notre feu à l'incendie qu'une foi vieille de mille années a allumé, cette foi chrétienne qui fut aussi la foi de Platon et qui admettait que Dieu est la vérité et que la vérité est divine”, that we too, the seekers after knowledge of today, we godless ones and anti-metaphysicians, still take our fire from the blaze kindled by a faith thousands of years old, that Christian faith which was also the faith of Plato, that God is truth, that truth is divine.

The *Genealogie der Moral* draws the same conclusion by another path, and turns it into a thesis on the history of Christianity that anticipates the theories of secularization by a century. In § 24 of the third essay, Nietzsche observes that no philosophy has yet asked whether the will to truth itself needs a justification, because the ascetic ideal dominated all philosophy, “weil Wahrheit als Sein, als Gott, als oberste Instanz selbst gesetzt wurde, weil Wahrheit gar nicht Problem sein durfte”, because truth was posited as being, as God, as the highest authority itself, because truth was not allowed to be a problem at all. In § 27, he takes the step. Honest atheism, far from opposing the ascetic ideal, is its last form: “er ist die Ehrfurcht gebietende Katastrophe einer zweitausendjährigen Zucht zur Wahrheit, welche am Schlusse sich die Lüge im Glauben an Gott verbietet”, it is the awe-inspiring catastrophe of a two-thousand-year discipline in truth, which in the end forbids itself the lie of belief in God. And he states its law: “Alle großen Dinge gehen durch sich selbst zugrunde, durch einen Akt der Selbstaufhebung”, all great things perish through themselves, through an act of self-suppression. Christianity as dogma perished of its own morality; Christianity as morality must in turn perish of its own truthfulness, when that truthfulness asks what any will to truth is worth. Nietzsche indeed quotes himself: the formula about the two-thousand-year discipline already appears word for word in § 357 of *The Gay Science*, in connection with Schopenhauer's atheism, followed by this sentence: “Man sieht, was eigentlich über den christlichen Gott gesiegt hat: die christliche Moralität selbst”, one sees what really triumphed over the Christian God: Christian morality itself. The atheism of the admired master thus becomes a fruit of the Christian conscience, and Schopenhauer finds himself, once again, among the pious.

Nietzsche is describing here a Christianity that destroys itself out of fidelity to itself; what Marcel Gauchet will call, in 1985, the “religion de la sortie de la religion”, the religion of the exit from religion. The comparison is constructed, and Part Eleven will take it up again; Gauchet's thesis is already formulated, in genealogical terms, in a paragraph of 1887. Nietzsche even adds, in parentheses, that the same development took place in India, independently, with the Buddha, which makes the exit from religion through truthfulness a law of the ascetic religions, and not a Christian peculiarity.

Hence the conclusion of the *Genealogy*, which gives the whole of religious history its psychological foundation: the ascetic ideal expresses a fundamental fact of the human will, “lieber will noch der Mensch das Nichts wollen, als nicht wollen”, man would rather will nothingness than not will (III, 28); Albert: “l'homme préfère encore avoir la volonté du néant que de ne point vouloir du tout”. It is at this point that Nietzsche answers Schopenhauer. Schopenhauer believed that one could stop willing; Nietzsche shows that the denial of the will is still a willing, turned towards nothingness. Religion is not an error of the intellect, as in Spinoza, nor a projection of the essence, as in Feuerbach, nor the sigh of the oppressed, as in Marx; it is the form taken, for want of anything better, by the will to give suffering a meaning. As long as this will finds no other object, it will return to the ascetic ideal under new names.

Here Nietzsche escapes Stirner's objection, and that is why I place him with Spinoza among those who do not refound a religion. He does not claim to have left it: he knows, and he says, that his own critique is made with Christian fire. This knowledge is not a weakness of his position, it is its strength: the atheist who believes himself free of all faith is the man of the marketplace who laughs at the madman; the one who knows where his fire comes from can at least choose what he does with it.

There remains Jesus, who separates Nietzsche from all his supposed heirs, and who sets him head-on against the great biographer of the century. Renan's *Life of Jesus* (1863) had made Christ a genius and a hero, an incomparable man before whom humanity was to bow; the last page of the book invites the moderns, “éternels enfants”, eternal children, to bow “devant ces demi-dieux”, before these demigods. Renan left dogma while keeping the cult: Christ was no longer God, but he remained the object of an adoration, transferred from the supernatural to moral genius. This is a refoundation in the sense of this essay, and Nietzsche denounces it with a violence that is not merely a matter of temperament: he calls Renan, as we saw in Part Five, a buffoon in psychology, and says he remained a priest in his very bowels.

The response, which is attested, targets the gesture that Feuerbach, Strauss and Renan had in common: leaving the supernatural while keeping the veneration. But Nietzsche is not content to unmask Renan: he proposes another Jesus. *Der Antichrist* (*The Antichrist*), written in 1888 and published in 1895, is one of the most violent books written against Christianity, but its violence is aimed at Paul and the Church, not at Jesus. For the vocabulary of the hero and the genius, Nietzsche substitutes a word in which one usually hears Dostoevsky, though Nietzsche does not name him here: “das Wort Idiot” (§ 29). He imagines this type in “eine Welt, wie aus einem russischen Romane” (§ 31). And he states: “im Grunde gab es nur einen Christen, und der starb am Kreuz”, in truth there was only one Christian, and he died on the cross; Albert: “au fond il n'y a eu qu'un seul chrétien, et il est mort sur la croix” (§ 39). Christianity is the work of Paul, that “Dysangelist”, bearer of bad tidings (§ 42), who made the death of Jesus into a doctrine of judgement and eternal life. Nietzsche, moreover, does not doubt for a moment that Jesus existed.

Nietzsche's Jesus is an “idiot” in Dostoevsky's sense, that is, a being incapable of resistance, of resentment and of dogma, who lives in an inner kingdom and does not preach it. This portrait resembles, and not by chance, the silent Christ of “The Grand Inquisitor”, and the Church that betrayed him resembles the Inquisitor's. The comparison is constructed, since Nietzsche did not know the poem. Dostoevsky and Nietzsche nevertheless agree on one point: historical Christianity, the institution, dogma and authority, is the negation of the practice of Jesus. They part ways on what follows. Dostoevsky believes that this practice can still be lived, and that is Zosima; Nietzsche judges it beautiful and impossible, the symptom of a gentle decadence, and looks elsewhere.

Where does he look? This is the question, too often forgotten behind the hammer, of Nietzschean spirituality. At the beginning of Book IV of *The Gay Science* (§ 276), for the new year 1882, Nietzsche makes a wish; Albert: “Je veux apprendre toujours davantage à considérer comme la beauté ce qu'il y a de nécessaire dans les choses”, I want to learn more and more to see as beautiful what is necessary in things, then: “Amor fati : que cela soit dorénavant mon amour. Je ne veux pas entrer en guerre contre la laideur. Je ne veux pas accuser, je ne veux même pas accuser les accusateurs”, Amor fati: let that be my love from now on. I do not want to wage war against ugliness. I do not want to accuse, I do not even want to accuse the accusers, and finally: “je veux, quelle que soit la circonstance, n'être une fois qu'affirmateur !”, I want, whatever the circumstance, some day to be only a Yes-sayer! Here one can measure the closeness to Spinoza and the distance from him. Like Spinoza, Nietzsche wants to love necessity; but where Spinoza loved it because he understood it, Nietzsche loves it without justifying it. Amor fati is not a knowledge, it is an exercise of the will, a discipline of affirmation. This is what Ivan refused in advance: to love fate is to love the child's tear as well.

The other image of this spirituality is that of noon. In *Götzen-Dämmerung*, the chapter “Wie die "wahre Welt" endlich zur Fabel wurde” tells in six stages the history of an error, that of the true world opposed to the apparent world, from Plato to positivism. At the last stage, the abolition of the true world brings with it that of the apparent world, and Nietzsche punctuates: “Mittag; Augenblick des kürzesten Schattens; Ende des längsten Irrthums; Höhepunkt der Menschheit; INCIPIT ZARATHUSTRA”, noon; moment of the shortest shadow; end of the longest error; high point of humanity; here begins Zarathustra. The shortest shadow: this is the answer to § 108 of *The Gay Science*, where the shadow of God was to last for thousands of years. Noon is the hour when the shadows of God disappear, not because they have been fought, but because the sun is at its highest. Nietzschean spirituality is not a replacement religion; it is the attempt to live at the hour when there is no longer any world behind the world. Camus, as we shall see, will make of it the “pensée de midi”, the thought of noon.

The objection awaiting Nietzsche will come from his most attentive reader. Nietzsche wanted to overturn Platonism: to put life above truth, becoming above being, value above fact. But to overturn a hierarchy is still to think in its terms. “Value”, the “will to power”, the “eternal return” are answers to the metaphysical question par excellence: what are beings as a whole? Nietzsche would then be, not the first philosopher to have left metaphysics, but the last metaphysician. One can also put to him a simpler objection, which Dostoevsky might have formulated: are not amor fati and the overman, in their turn, figures of Kirillov, the man who makes himself god? And is not Zarathustra, with his discourses, his disciples and his parables, one more religion? Nietzsche himself feared so, since he wrote, at the end of his conscious life, that there was “Nichts in mir von einem Religionsstifter”, nothing in him of a founder of religion, and: “Ich will keine „Gläubigen“”, I want no “believers”.

These objections will come in the twentieth century, with Heidegger and Camus. In the meantime another response arises, which comes neither from the novelists nor from the philosophers: religion becomes an object of science, and this science, which many awaited as a weapon against it, will discover that the sacred does not let itself be dismissed so easily.
