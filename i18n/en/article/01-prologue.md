## Prologue: there is no such thing as “atheism”

The history of the critique of religion is readily told as a relay race. Lucretius supposedly passed the baton to Spinoza, Spinoza to the Enlightenment, the Enlightenment to Feuerbach, Feuerbach to Marx, Marx to Nietzsche, Nietzsche to Freud, and so on down to our contemporaries, each adding an argument to the pile until the pile finally crushed God. This story is false, and not only because it simplifies. It is false because the authors it lines up do not criticize the same thing, do not aim at the same goal, and above all criticize one another with more violence than they bring against the theologians. It is false, too, because it forgets that the harshest critique of religion has often been waged from within faith: by Pascal against the God of the philosophers, by Kierkegaard against Christendom, by Barth against religion itself, by Simone Weil against the consoling God. And it is false, finally, because it assumes that we know what the thing is that we are leaving.

It also begins too late and looks too narrowly. Our question was born neither with the Moderns nor with Christianity. Plato was the first to raise it, the other way round: he sought to found by reason a religion of the city, and in the same stroke he invented the prison for the impious. Aristotle made the god an object of science and noted that myths serve the laws. It was in medieval Judaism and Islam, with Maimonides in Cairo and Averroes in Córdoba, that philosophy first asked whether one can think God without representing him, and reserve for the learned a truth that the Law gives to others in images. They have their place here, as do the classical thinkers who built the God of the philosophers, Montaigne, Descartes and Leibniz, and Rousseau, who drew a civil religion from it. The essay remains centred on Christian and post-Christian Europe, and this must be said frankly: the “exit from religion” described by Weber and Gauchet is first of all a Christian story, and Jewish and Muslim societies have known other paths, which this essay touches on only in passing, through Averroes, Maimonides, Arkoun and Bidar. But Christianity did not forge the weapons of its own critique alone, and to forget this would be to perpetuate the relay fable. The essay finally widens the question beyond any particular religion: wonder at the fact that there is something, from Plato to Wittgenstein, is perhaps the barest form of spirituality, and it will have its own debate.

Two further questions run through the essay. Where does Christianity come from, since nearly all the critiques examined here are critiques of it? Part Two follows its birth between Athens and Jerusalem and examines, without indulgence, the thesis that Jesus never existed. Why do people believe? Part Nine asks anthropologists, psychologists, sociologists and the cognitive sciences what the religious disposition owes to fear, to desire, to society, to death and to the very structure of the mind.

### Eight regimes of critique

At least eight regimes of critique must be distinguished; they cannot be added together, and they will serve as the essay's map.

In the first, religion is an **error**: a false representation to be corrected, or translated into its truth. This is the regime of Epicurus and Lucretius, for whom the gods exist but take no care of us; of Spinoza, for whom God exists but is not what the vulgar believe; of Hume and d'Holbach, who explain belief by ignorance of causes; and finally of Feuerbach, for whom God is man misrecognized. In the second, religion is a **symptom**: not first of all a false thesis, but the expression of a world that needs it, and which must be changed in order to abolish it. This is the regime of Marx. In the third, religion is a **value**, or rather a way of evaluating, whose genealogy one traces, and which survives belief in God in morality, in science, in grammar itself. This is the regime of Nietzsche. In the fourth, the Christian religion and Western philosophy are caught together in a single history, that of metaphysics, and the “death of God” is not the victory of one over the other but their common completion. This is the regime of Heidegger, which Pascal had foreseen and which Marion turns around.

The next four regimes were born later. The fifth makes religion an **illusion** in the technical sense: not just any error, but a belief commanded by a desire, the desire for a protecting father. This is the regime of Freud. The sixth makes religion a **social function**: the sacred is the form in which a society represents its own strength to itself, and no society does without it. This is the regime of Durkheim and, through the sociology of rationalization, of Weber. The seventh raises the question of **secularization**: are the modern categories, the State, history, progress, theological categories in disguise, or new answers to inherited questions? This is the debate of Schmitt, Löwith, Blumenberg, Gauchet, Taylor and Habermas. The eighth shifts the whole terrain: it no longer asks whether dogmas are true, it starts from **experience**: intuition and feeling of the universe in Schleiermacher, individual experience in James, the numinous in Otto, mysticism in Bergson, inner experience in Bataille. It is this regime that forces us to widen the question of religion into that of spirituality.

Other authors create no new regime, but show that several are older than one thinks. Social function begins with Plato, who makes theology a law of the city, and with Aristotle, who writes that myths were added to persuade the multitude; Averroes gives its learned version, Montaigne its descriptive version, Rousseau its republican version, Girard its darkest, since he has the sacred arise from the sacrifice of a victim, which bears directly on this essay's criterion. Onto-theology takes as its target the god of Aristotle, Descartes and Leibniz. The negative theology of Plotinus and Maimonides anticipates the regime of experience, to which Wittgenstein, Levinas and Jaspers give its twentieth-century forms. Ricoeur gathers under the name of the school of suspicion Marx's symptom, Nietzsche's genealogy and Freud's illusion, and asks what one can still believe after them. Finally, the cognitive science of Boyer explains why religious beliefs are so easy to think, without saying anything about their truth.

These regimes do not complement one another, they accuse one another, and the accusation is almost always the same: *you think you have left religion, but you are still in it*. Stirner hurls it at Feuerbach, Marx at Stirner, Nietzsche at all the “atheists” of his century, Heidegger at Nietzsche, Camus at Marx, and, according to a reported phrase, Onfray at Comte-Sponville. It gives the essay its guiding question: **can one leave religion without founding another?**

### Religion and spirituality: two words, two etymologies

We still need to know what it is we are leaving. The Latin word *religio* received two competing etymologies in Antiquity, and their conflict is no grammarians' quarrel: it sets two conceptions of the thing against each other.

The first is Cicero's, in the *De natura deorum* (*On the Nature of the Gods*, 45 BCE). There the Stoic Balbus distinguishes the superstitious from the religious. The superstitious are those who spent whole days praying and sacrificing so that their children might survive them, *superstites*; the religious are those “qui autem omnia quae ad cultum deorum pertinerent diligenter retractarent et tamquam relegerent”, who carefully went back over and, as it were, reread everything that pertained to the worship of the gods, whence their name, “ex relegendo” (II, 72). Ciceronian *religio* is a scruple, an attentiveness, a rereading: a way of doing things exactly. Civic and ritual, it presupposes neither dogma, nor inner faith, nor a personal relation to a god.

The second etymology is Christian. Lactantius (c. 250-c. 325), tutor to Constantine's son, gives it in the *Divinae institutiones* (*Divine Institutes*, c. 303-311), expressly refuting Cicero: “hoc uinculo pietatis obstricti deo et religati sumus: unde ipsa religio nomen accepit, non ut Cicero interpretatus est a relegendo”, it is by this bond of piety that we are bound and tied to God, and it is from this that religion took its name, not from *relegere* as Cicero interpreted it (IV, 28, 3). He adds, not without irony: if the religious took their name from attentive rereading, those who pray several times a day ought to lose their title, since they reread far more (IV, 28, 10). With *religare*, religion becomes a bond, a personal obligation towards a single God. It is this bond that the moderns will seek to undo; and that is why the question “can one leave religion?” is first of all a Christian question.

Two modern definitions reproduce the same opposition twenty centuries apart. Émile Durkheim, in 1912, defines religion as a collective fact: “Une religion est un système solidaire de croyances et de pratiques relatives à des choses sacrées, c'est-à-dire séparées, interdites, croyances et pratiques qui unissent en une même communauté morale, appelée Église, tous ceux qui y adhèrent”, a religion is a unified system of beliefs and practices relative to sacred things, that is to say, things set apart and forbidden, beliefs and practices which unite into one single moral community, called a Church, all those who adhere to them (*The Elementary Forms of Religious Life*, book I, ch. 1). William James, ten years earlier, defines it as an individual fact: “the feelings, acts, and experiences of individual men in their solitude, so far as they apprehend themselves to stand in relation to whatever they may consider the divine” (*The Varieties of Religious Experience*, 1902, lecture II). James specifies that he takes this definition “arbitrarily”. Durkheim is on the side of Cicero and the Church, James on the side of the solitary mystic.

The word “spirituality” has lodged itself in the gap between these two definitions. In contemporary usage, it designates what remains of James's definition once Durkheim's has been removed: an experience without a Church, a relation to the absolute without dogma or obligatory community. But it has a more precise philosophical history, which Pierre Hadot and Michel Foucault have reconstructed. For Foucault, in the lecture of 6 January 1982 at the Collège de France, spirituality is “la recherche, la pratique, l'expérience par lesquelles le sujet opère sur lui-même les transformations nécessaires pour avoir accès à la vérité”, the search, practice and experience through which the subject carries out on itself the transformations necessary to have access to the truth (*The Hermeneutics of the Subject*, published in 2001, p. 16). Spirituality, in this sense, is a work of the subject upon itself; the Stoics and the Epicureans had one, so did Spinoza, and perhaps Nietzsche.

Hence a distinction that will run through the essay. Religion, in the strong sense, involves an **external absolute** to which the individual is bound and subjected, **dogmas** that state its nature, and a **community** that keeps its worship. Spirituality, in Foucault's sense, involves only an **exercise** through which the subject transforms itself. One can have the one without the other: a religion of pure observance without inner life, such as the one Cicero describes and which Kant will call *Afterdienst*; or a spirituality without God, such as that of the Epicureans, of Spinoza or of Comte-Sponville.

### Thesis

My thesis is twofold. On the one hand, every “exit” from religion examined here has reinstalled an absolute in the place of God: the city of civil religion in Rousseau, Man in Feuerbach and Comte, the Self in Stirner, emancipated Humanity in Marx, Society in Durkheim, Reason in Freud, History in the revolutionaries whom Camus indicts, Being in Heidegger, Civilization in Onfray. On the other hand, and this is the paradox, those who escape the trap are those who keep something of the religious *knowingly*: first Spinoza, who retains the name of God; then the Nietzsche of § 344 of *The Gay Science*, who admits that his own fire still comes from the Christian blaze.

Widening the question to spirituality makes it possible to state this more precisely, and this is the hypothesis I shall put to the test. What distinguishes a successful exit from a refoundation is perhaps not the presence of God, but that of an **absolute in whose name the sacrifice of the individual can be demanded**, and the critique of which becomes sacrilege; self-offering freely consented to does not fall under it. The criterion admits of degrees: refoundation in the strict sense installs an absolute that demands, as in Plato, Rousseau or Comte; soft refoundation is content with the need to consecrate without demanding anything, the need for which Engels will reproach Feuerbach and which we shall find again in Ferry. A spirituality of exercise, which transforms the subject without subjecting it to anything external, would be the only exit that does not refound. It has two serious adversaries. Durkheim, for whom no society can live without the sacred, and who would make every private spirituality a religion unaware of itself. And Blumenberg, for whom the question itself is badly posed, since it presupposes that whatever occupies the place of God is a god. They will have to be given their strongest form.

Dostoevsky occupies a place apart here. He does not argue, he embodies; and the figure he invents, the Grand Inquisitor, shows what becomes of a religion from which God has been removed. It will serve as a touchstone down to our contemporaries, among whom we shall find again, with Michel Onfray, the temptation to keep the Church while losing God. Voltaire, a century before Dostoevsky, had in fact written the Inquisitor's programme in a single alexandrine.
