## Conclusion: who leaves, and for what?

### Assessment of the refoundations

Can one leave religion without founding another? At the end of this journey, from Plato to our contemporaries, the series answers first in the negative, and with a regularity that ends up resembling a law. Refoundation does not even wait for the exit to take place. Plato, who was leaving nothing, had already shown what a religion founded by reason costs: a demonstrated theology, then a law, then prison for the impious and death for the repeat offender. Rousseau, in leaving the Church, refounds a civil religion and gives it back the death penalty. D'Holbach, who believed he was opposing nothing but physics to religion, ends the *Système de la nature* with a prayer to Nature. Leibniz justifies the evil of the part by the beauty of the whole, and asks the individual to sacrifice his complaint. Feuerbach leaves God by deifying Man, and Comte, who draws all the consequences of this gesture, organizes the cult of the Great Being with its sacraments and its calendar. Stirner leaves Man by absolutizing the Self. Marx leaves religion by promising an emancipation that has its form, and Löwith had only to trace Christian eschatology onto the philosophy of history to show it. Durkheim leaves the Churches by discovering that Society is the god of all gods, which is a way of sacralizing what one thought one was explaining. Freud leaves illusion by entrusting our future to “unser Gott Λόγος”, and he names this new god himself. Heidegger leaves the god of the philosophers by waiting for a god who saves. The twentieth century, finally, gave the law its most massive cases: the secular religions that Voegelin and Aron named, the deified People of 1938, the social order to come in whose name present generations were sacrificed, had their police, their camps and their trials. Closer to us, and in an altogether different register, Ferry leaves vertical transcendence by calling sacred whatever in the human surpasses us, and Onfray leaves faith by sacralizing the civilization that faith produced. Each time the dismissed absolute returns under another name, and each time the next critic denounces it. “Unsere Atheisten sind fromme Leute”: Stirner formulated the law of the whole series, and he did not escape it himself.

This law is not an accident in the history of ideas, and the inquiry into the causes of belief has shown why. What anthropologists, psychologists and sociologists have described is not a need for God, but a broader and more tenacious disposition: a need for meaning, for bonds and for protection, universal in its springs, variable in its intensity. This need survives God, as Feuerbach had seen, and it seeks other objects. Explaining why people believe, through fear, desire, society or the structure of the mind, therefore does not tell us whether one can stop believing without believing in something else; the explanation makes the question sharper, since the disposition that produces gods is also the one that produces refoundations. Becker and Girard, by two different paths, have even shown that sacrifice is not a late perversion of certain religions, but a spring of belief itself: one sacrifices to deny death, one sacrifices to appease violence. Refoundations are the natural slope of the exit, and the exits that do not refound are costly exceptions, which have to be willed, built and maintained. Norris and Inglehart add a condition that philosophers readily forget: these exceptions are possible, on the scale of a society, only where existence is secure enough for fear to stop looking for a victim.

We must, however, take seriously the three objections that threaten the thesis itself, and not this or that author. The first, already announced in the prologue, is Durkheim's. If every society lives on something sacred, if “le dieu n'est que l'expression figurée de la société”, the god is merely the figurative expression of society, then the question is idle: one never leaves religion, one only changes the sacred, and the series I have just drawn up is not a sequence of failures but the description of a necessity. The second, the other objection announced in the prologue, is Blumenberg's. If what modernity inherits from Christianity is not a content but a place, if the questions left open by theology have merely been “reoccupied” by heterogeneous answers, then speaking of “refoundation” is an abuse: Marx does not found a religion because he answers the question of the meaning of history, but because the question was posed once and for all and one can no longer fail to answer it. Durkheim says the thesis is too weak, because refoundation is inevitable; Blumenberg that it is too strong, because it sees gods wherever there are only places. The third objection is more disturbing, for it bears on the criterion itself. If Girard is right, the sacred is born of sacrifice: a community in crisis recovers its unity by discharging its violence onto a victim, then deifies that victim. The criterion of this essay would then not be one criterion among others, it would name the very origin of the religious; and Girard draws from it a consequence the modern critics had not foreseen, since the Gospels, by taking the side of the innocent victim, would have unveiled the mechanism, so that the exit from sacrifice would be the work of Christianity itself.

I believe that the first two objections neutralize each other, and that the third forces us to deepen the criterion rather than abandon it. Blumenberg is right against the lazy version of the secularization thesis, the one that sees a salvation in every promise and a Church in every institution: such a thesis is unfalsifiable, and it makes it possible to discredit any modern thought by finding it a theological ancestor. This is, moreover, the reproach Kelsen addressed to Voegelin's notion of political religion, when it becomes a genealogy of the whole of modernity. But Durkheim is right against the optimistic version of the exit, the one that believes a society can live without holding anything untouchable. Between the two, the notion of secular religion, understood in Aron's functional sense rather than in Voegelin's genealogical sense, provides the distinction Blumenberg lacked: not all reoccupations are equal. There are those that answer an inherited question with a modest hypothesis, and those that answer it with an absolute that demands. The criterion set out in the prologue, and refined in connection with Feuerbach, thus comes through Blumenberg's test, and I can formulate it one last time: one refounds a religion, in the sense that matters here, not when one occupies a place that theology left empty, but when one installs in that place **an absolute in whose name the sacrifice of the individual can be demanded**, and whose critique becomes a sacrilege; freely consented self-offering does not fall under it. This last point settles cases the journey had left undecided: Justin dying for having refused a cult, Clifford's scientist who renounces a consoling belief, Ferry's man ready to die for freedom refound nothing by their devotion alone; what refounds is the authority that demands this devotion of others. The criterion also has two degrees. Refoundation in the strict sense installs an absolute that demands: Plato's law, Rousseau's civil religion, Comte's Great Being, which requires the individual to efface himself (the disciple of the *Catéchisme* says so herself), the secular religions of the twentieth century with the History of the revolutionaries whom Camus accuses, which demanded victims, and Schmitt's political theology. Soft refoundation confines itself to consecrating without demanding: Feuerbach sacralizes the relation, Ferry calls sacred that for which some men agree to die; they immolate no one, but they feel the need to consecrate that Engels named, and it is through this need that consecration can become demand in their successors. Onfray's Civilization has already taken the step: it demands that one defend a liturgy in which one does not believe. By contrast, the question of the meaning of history that Blumenberg raises, or the social sacred that Durkheim describes, refound nothing as long as they demand no sacrifice and tolerate their own critique. As for Girard, I keep his anthropology and leave his apologetics: Christian history has known the stake, and Dostoevsky could imagine Christ condemned a second time by his Church. The lesson I draw from it is that no exit can avoid refounding if it does not refuse the scapegoat, and that this refusal needs no god in order to be demanded, even if it needed a religious history in order to be discovered. This refusal is the criterion seen from the other side: it is the condition of any exit without refoundation, not yet its form. The criterion has, finally, the advantage of not depending on the presence or absence of God. It allows us to say that some believers have left religion, in the sense in which Barth, Bonhoeffer and Weil denounce it, and that some atheists have remained in it.

The dispute over the right to believe gives this criterion its translation into the order of knowledge, and it has the advantage of addressing unbelievers as much as believers. Clifford held that it is always wrong to believe on insufficient evidence; James replied that one has the right to believe, at one's own risk, when the option is living, forced and momentous and reason cannot decide. I believe both must be kept. One has the right to believe where reason leaves the question open: that is James. This right ends where belief engages the lives of others without their having chosen it: that is Clifford, and it is the lesson of his shipowner. A belief that asks its believer only to risk his own life is a faith, or a wisdom; a belief that asks others to risk theirs, or that sacrifices them when they refuse it, is a refoundation. The rule holds against the Inquisitor, and it also holds against those who invoke Clifford to wage a crusade against the beliefs of others: Russell reproached believers for believing without reason, but what he chiefly believed was Lucretius, and the new atheists kept Clifford's maxim while forgetting his shipowner.

There remains the most serious adversary, not of this or that exit, but of the very idea of an exit. Leo Strauss grants the critics of religion everything one could wish, except the essential: neither philosophy nor revelation has refuted the other, and modern critique contented itself with mocking what it could not refute. If a total exit presupposed the completion of reason, then every exit that believes itself total is already a refoundation, and the only honest position left is one that acknowledges that the question is not settled. I do not believe Strauss is right to make the tension between Athens and Jerusalem an eternal return of the same: the history this essay has traversed shows that the question shifts, that it changes ground, and that faith itself has learned from its critics. But he is right on the essential point, and his lesson joins that of the whole series: leaving religion is not an accomplished event, it is a question that remains open, and those who believe it closed are the most exposed to giving themselves a god without knowing it. The inquiry into the birth of Christianity confirms this in another way. The religion the West seeks to leave was already an alloy: the God of the Greek philosophers had taken on the face of a crucified man in it, through Philo, through Justin, through Augustine, and it is this alloy that Pascal, Feuerbach, Nietzsche and Heidegger tried to undo, each from one end. The mythicist thesis, which thinks it settles everything by denying the existence of Jesus, misses precisely this object: it goes back to the regime of error, whereas the question is that of the construction of a figure. And the question raised from within Islam, by Arkoun and by Bidar, recalls that the exit from religion, Christian first, is not only Christian, that no tradition leaves religion by the same path, and that the Christian exit described by Weber and Gauchet is not the measure of the others.

### What the exceptions have in common

The exceptions are more numerous than one would have thought at the outset. Spinoza and Nietzsche, first; Simone Weil, who leaves consoling religion without leaving faith; Hadot, Camus, Wittgenstein, Murdoch; Jaspers and Ricoeur; and, on the borderline, Levinas. Hume, whom I place apart, refounds nothing either; Blumenberg and William James, finally, seem to be among them and are rather allies of the thesis. I do not believe they are all of the same rank, and a decision must be made.

What unites them first is a **genealogical lucidity**: they know what they owe to what they criticize. Spinoza keeps the name of God, and he does not keep it out of prudence; Nietzsche admits that he borrows his fire “von dem Brande[…], den ein jahrtausendealter Glaube entzündet hat”; Hadot's “spiritual exercises” bear a Christian name, that of Ignatius of Loyola's *Exercises*, and Hadot turns the debt around: it was the Christians who inherited the philosophical exercises of Antiquity; Camus knows that his Ivan does not deny God but judges him, and that he “inaugure l'entreprise essentielle de la révolte qui est de substituer au royaume de la grâce celui de la justice”, inaugurates the essential undertaking of revolt, which is to substitute for the kingdom of grace the kingdom of justice. This lucidity is what the atheists of the marketplace lack, from the militant materialists to the new atheists, who believe that religion is an error to be got rid of like a superstition, and who for that reason do not see the shadows of God in their own faith in science. Western Buddhism, as we have seen, offers the counterproof.

What unites them next is the **absence of a sacrificial absolute**. None of them asks the individual to immolate himself to a higher authority. Spinoza's beatitude is not a reward purchased by sacrifice: “Beatitudo non est virtutis præmium, sed ipsa virtus”, beatitude is not the reward of virtue, but virtue itself. Nietzsche's amor fati is not obedience to fate but a consent that transforms the one who consents. Camus's measure is expressly directed against the deification of history: “Au midi de la pensée, le révolté refuse ainsi la divinité pour partager les luttes et le destin communs”, at the noon of thought, the rebel thus refuses divinity in order to share the common struggles and the common fate. The case of Murdoch is more instructive, because she unambiguously installs an absolute, the Good, sovereign and transcendent. But this absolute demands that no one be immolated to it; it demands that we stop immolating ourselves to our own fantasies: “We cease to be in order to attend to the existence of something else.” One does not govern men in the name of a sun that commands nothing. The criterion therefore does not condemn every absolute: it condemns the absolute that demands the sacrifice of others and has priests to speak in its name.

What unites them, finally, is a **practice** rather than a doctrine. What Spinoza, Nietzsche, Hadot, Camus or Murdoch propose is not a new belief, it is a way of transforming oneself: knowledge of the third kind, the incorporation of the heaviest thought, the exercise of the view from above, the revolt that never ceases to measure itself, patient attention to what is. This is what Foucault called spirituality, the practice by which the subject carries out on himself the transformations necessary to gain access to the truth, and it is, transposed outside Christianity, what Bergson called dynamic religion, as opposed to static religion (a constructed comparison: for Bergson, dynamic religion is Christian mysticism). Spirituality so understood is not a weakened religion, nor the refuge where religion survives its critique; it is what remains when religion has been stripped of its two functions of power, the guarantee of meaning and submission to the absolute.

But we must now distinguish, among these exceptions, a **position**, a **family of attitudes**, and a few neighbouring figures who belong to neither. The position articulates an ontology and a practice, and it has three versions. Spinoza and Hadot give the version of immanence: the Whole, and exercises that make it possible to relate to it without the mediation of Church or dogma; it is, moreover, towards Spinoza that contemporary secular spiritualities tend, more or less consciously, Comte-Sponville's first of all. Murdoch gives the version of transcendence without God: a real Good that judges the world instead of blessing it, and that one reaches through attention. Wittgenstein gives the silent version: where the others offer an ontology and exercises, he offers a limit and a wonder, he holds the place left empty by the death of God without filling it, and he refuses even the name. This is, I believe, the strictest of the three versions. Nietzsche and Camus form rather an attitude, more unstable, because it has no settled ontology: Nietzsche oscillates between the lucidity of FW 344 and the prophetic temptations of Zarathustra, and it is not certain that he always resisted the latter; Camus refuses the absolute in the name of measure, but he himself acknowledges that revolt must constantly watch over itself so as not to turn into what it fights. The reservation I made about his sacred Mediterranean stands, but it bears on an aesthetic myth, not on an absolute that demands: it makes his position more unstable, it does not place it among the refoundations.

The believing exceptions form a group apart, and they are decisive for the thesis, because they show that the criterion crosses the frontier between believers and unbelievers. Simone Weil is its most radical figure, making atheism a purification of faith. Jaspers and Ricoeur join her with less radicalism and more prudence: they are the witnesses of a faith that has passed through critique, who do not refound but listen. Levinas remains in suspense. He leaves the God of the philosophers through ethics and not through being, and his absolute is the singular face of the other man, which forbids him to sacrifice an individual to an abstraction; but his responsibility without reciprocity is an infinite demand, and I am not sure it entirely escapes the criterion, even if I lean towards counting him among the believing exceptions. Plotinus and Maimonides, finally, showed that the negation of every image can be carried out from within a tradition, demanding nothing but the asceticism of thought; but they remain, the one in a school, the other in the Law, and they are precursors rather than exceptions.

Three figures, to conclude, are exceptions of none of these families. Hume leaves without refounding, but through a suspension of judgement that he himself admitted was hard to maintain outside the study, and which proposes no way of living with the Whole: he is the sceptic of the series, not its sage. Blumenberg proposes no spirituality, but an intellectual hygiene, which consists in not confusing an inherited question with an imposed answer. William James, likewise, proposes not a wisdom but a right: that of believing at one's own risk, which no one has the right to impose on others, either in the form of faith or in that of unbelief. Blumenberg and James are allies of the thesis, the one in the order of history, the other in that of belief; they are not examples of it. Several authors, finally, do not refound and yet are not exceptions for all that. Clifford and Löwith propose no way of living with the Whole: the first offers only an ethics of belief, whose absolute demands sacrifice only of oneself, the second only a Stoic withdrawal that one may doubt is habitable. Montaigne, Barth, Bonhoeffer and Arkoun remain within a tradition: Montaigne as a practising Catholic who stops speaking of salvation, Barth and Bonhoeffer within revelation, in whose name they leave religion, Arkoun within an Islam he wants to reopen to its history. Bergson escapes the deification of man only narrowly, at the price of a metaphysics of life. Comte-Sponville refounds nothing, but his wisdom renounces protest, and I return to this below. Buddhism, for its part, is neither an exception nor an ally, but a test. It shows that religion is not defined by God, since a tradition without a creator God could have a Law, monks and sometimes states; and it confirms the criterion, since its doctrine demands no sacrifice and its institutions, in contact with power, have sometimes demanded it.

My answer to the question of this essay is therefore the following. One leaves religion without refounding one only if one renounces the idea of leaving it entirely, that is, if one agrees to keep, knowingly, something of what it carried. This something is neither a belief nor an institution, it is an orientation: the relation to a Whole that surpasses us and that we do not possess, or to a Good that we possess no more. The form that makes it possible to keep this orientation without turning it into a sacrificial absolute is a spirituality of exercise, of which Spinoza gave the most coherent ontology, Hadot the most exact historical description and Wittgenstein the strictest limit. Of this exit without refoundation, the refusal of the scapegoat is the condition, the spirituality of exercise the form, and wonder without debt the barest affect: to think being is not to owe it something. This spirituality presupposes a wisdom, which these spiritualities offer, and a security, which only institutions can guarantee. And it is never possessed once and for all, Strauss is right on this point: the claim to a total exit is itself the modern form of the absolute, and whoever believes he no longer has anything religious about him is the worst equipped to recognize the gods he gives himself.
