### Girard (1923-2015): the sacred is born of sacrifice

René Girard is the author in this part who bears most directly on the thesis. Trained at the École des chartes, he left in 1947 to teach in the United States, where he spent his whole career, notably at Johns Hopkins, at Buffalo and then at Stanford, and he came to anthropology through literature. His first book, *Deceit, Desire and the Novel* (1961), starts from an observation about the great novelists, Cervantes, Stendhal, Flaubert, Proust and Dostoevsky: their characters never desire an object directly; they desire it because someone else desires it. Don Quixote wants to be a knight because Amadis was one; Emma Bovary loves through the novels she has read. Human desire is not a straight line between a subject and an object but a triangle whose apex is a model. Girard calls this desire **mimetic**.

Girard recounted that while writing the last page of *Deceit, Desire and the Novel*, on the final conversion of the heroes who renounce desire according to the other, he himself underwent, at the end of the 1950s, a conversion that brought him back to the Catholicism of his Avignon childhood. The biography does not explain the thesis, but it says where the author speaks from: Girard speaks as a believer. Elected to the Académie française in 2005, he remained admired by theologians, debated by anthropologists, and more or less ignored by the philosophers of the French university.

The discovery would have remained a thesis of literary criticism without its consequence. When the model is distant, like Amadis for Don Quixote, imitation is harmless. When the model is close, desiring the same object in the same world, he becomes a rival, the rival an obstacle, and the obstacle an even more fascinating model. Mimetic desire breeds rivalry, and rivalry too spreads by imitation: each imitates the other's violence, differences fade, and the whole community slides towards what Girard calls the **mimetic crisis**, a war of all against all in which nothing any longer distinguishes the adversaries. This is the hypothesis of his second book, *Violence and the Sacred* (1972), and it is there that Girard encounters religion.

How does a community get out of it? Girard answers with a hypothesis that nothing allows us to observe, but which he believes he can read in myths the world over. The violence of all against all, because it is mimetic, can suddenly turn into the violence of all against one. It is enough for an individual, singled out by some trait or other, an infirmity, a strangeness, a royal position, to draw the accusations upon himself; imitation does the rest, and the unanimous community turns against him and kills him. This collective murder has a miraculous effect: peace returns, since all the violence has been discharged onto a single victim. The community, which does not understand what has happened, attributes the return of peace to the victim itself. The victim was guilty, since it was killed; it is beneficent, since its death saved everyone. It becomes sacred, that is, at once accursed and divine. Girard sums up the thesis in a sentence that has become famous, which makes violence the true heart and secret soul of the sacred.

All archaic religion proceeds, according to Girard, from this **victimage mechanism**. Myth is the story of the lynching told from the point of view of the lynchers, who do not know that they have killed an innocent: the god in it is always guilty of something, of incest like Oedipus, of a fault like the Pharmakos. Rite is the controlled repetition of the founding murder, on a substitute victim, human or animal, so as to reproduce its pacifying effects: this is **sacrifice**. Prohibitions are the measures taken to prevent the return of the crisis, by separating what mimetic desire tends to confuse. The gods, finally, are deified victims. Girard here takes up all of Frazer's material, his sacrificed kings and his scapegoats, but he reverses its interpretation: where Frazer saw an error of reasoning, Girard sees a necessary misrecognition, without which the mechanism would not work. If the lynchers knew that their victim was innocent, peace would not return.

Girard builds his demonstration on *Oedipus the King*, which shows how he reads a myth. Thebes is ravaged by plague, the image of the crisis in which differences fade; the city looks for the culprit; Oedipus and Tiresias, then Oedipus and Creon, accuse each other in a series of symmetrical exchanges in which each becomes the double of the other. And the city at last finds its culprit, the lame stranger who became king, who has committed the two undifferentiating crimes par excellence, parricide and incest, the crimes that confuse generations and places. Oedipus is driven out, the plague ceases. For Girard, Sophocles' tragedy is half lucid: it shows the symmetry of the accusations and lets us suspect that Oedipus's guilt might be a collective verdict rather than a fact; but in the end it confirms it, and the myth it takes up never doubts it for a moment. The case of Oedipus throws light, by contrast, on that of Socrates, whom the trial for impiety likewise singles out to the unanimity of a city in crisis; and Plato, as we have seen, wrote the law that would have condemned him a second time. The comparison is constructed: the city of the philosophers was not safe from the mechanism it believed it had mastered.

Ten years later, *The Scapegoat* (1982) applies the same grid to texts that no one takes for myths. Girard starts from a poem by Guillaume de Machaut, which tells how, in the middle of the fourteenth century, the Jews were accused of poisoning the wells during the Black Death and were massacred. The modern reader does not hesitate for a second: the plague is real, the accusation false, the massacre real. From this Girard draws what he calls the “stereotypes of persecution”: a crisis that effaces differences, undifferentiating crimes imputed to the victims, signs that single them out, minorities, the infirm, foreigners, and the collective violence itself. If we know how to read these stereotypes in a medieval text, he asks, why not in myths, which display all of them? The strength of the argument is methodological: it does not ask us to invent an original murder; it asks us to treat myths as we treat chronicles of persecution, by taking the side of the victim. The last great book, *I See Satan Fall Like Lightning* (1999), gives this reversal its biblical name: Satan, in the Gospels, is the accuser, the principle of mimetic desire and of the unanimous crowd, and his fall is the end of his power to accuse without being unmasked.

One can see why Girard matters to this essay. The criterion I have proposed says that a religion is refounded when an absolute is installed in whose name the sacrifice of the individual can be demanded. Girard says more: religion is **born** of the sacrifice of an individual to the unanimity of the group. What the essay described as a symptom of refoundation would be the birth certificate of every religion. If the sacred is collective violence transfigured, then each time an exit from religion singles out a victim, Plato's impious man, the citizen who violates Rousseau's civil religion, the class enemy, the traitor to the Nation, it reproduces the founding gesture and refounds, literally, a religion. Dostoevsky's Grand Inquisitor is not only a parable of power; he is, in Girardian terms, the priest who manages the victimage mechanism and who protects the crowd from the truth.

But Girard also reverses the thesis, and it is there that he becomes truly original. There are, according to him, texts that tell the lynching from the point of view of the victim, and that proclaim its innocence: those of the Bible. Joseph, sold by his brothers, is innocent; Job, accused by his friends, is innocent; Isaiah's suffering servant is innocent; the Psalms give voice to the man whom the crowd assails. The Gospels complete this unveiling: the Passion is a unanimous lynching, in which even the disciples fail, but it is told by those who know that the victim is innocent, and who proclaim it. Christianity, for Girard, is therefore not one more sacrificial religion; it is the revelation of the sacrificial mechanism, the revelation of “things hidden since the foundation of the world” (Mt 13, 35), which give their title to the book of 1978, written in dialogue with two psychiatrists, Jean-Michel Oughourlian and Guy Lefort. This revelation has a historical consequence: once unveiled, the mechanism no longer works, since it required misrecognition. The societies that have received the Gospels can no longer reconcile themselves at the expense of a victim without knowing, at least dimly, that they are committing an injustice. Hence the modern concern for victims, which Girard regards as the deepest legacy of Christianity, and hence also, for want of a regulating mechanism, the rise of a violence that nothing any longer contains, the theme of his last book, *Battling to the End* (2007).

For a long time Girard refused to call the Passion a sacrifice, precisely because it unveiled sacrifice; at the end of a long exchange with the Jesuit theologian Raymund Schwager, he finally accepted the word in a new sense, that of the gift of self that refuses violence instead of undergoing it. This dispute over vocabulary is not secondary: it means that Christianity, for Girard, is at once the exit from sacrificial religion and a religion, which is the very thesis of Marcel Gauchet, whom we shall read in the part on secularization: Christianity as the religion of the exit from religion. The comparison is constructed, for the paths differ, Gauchet starting from political history and Girard from the anthropology of desire; yet the two bodies of thought share the same weakness, which is to make Christianity the end point of the religious history of humanity.

Girard responded, in attested fashion, to several authors in this essay. Against Freud, he takes up in *Violence and the Sacred* the hypothesis of *Totem und Tabu* (1912-1913), the original murder of the father by the horde of sons, and he pays tribute to him for having glimpsed the founding murder; but he reproaches him for having tied it to sexual desire and to the father, instead of to mimetic rivalry and to the arbitrary victim. Against Durkheim, he accepts that the sacred is social, but he refuses the idea that society worships itself in it: what the community worships is its own violence transfigured, and the reverse side of Durkheimian unanimity is a corpse. It is against Nietzsche that his response goes furthest. Nietzsche had ended *Ecce homo* with the formula “Dionysos gegen den Gekreuzigten”, Dionysus against the Crucified. Girard takes up the formula as the title of an essay and agrees with Nietzsche on the diagnosis: the difference between Dionysus and Christ does not lie in the story, for both die under the blows of a crowd; it lies in the point of view, since the Dionysian myth takes the side of the lynchers and the Gospel that of the victim. Nietzsche saw this better than the theologians, and he chose Dionysus, that is to say the crowd. From this Girard draws a grave charge against Nietzsche: the will to power that despises compassion for the weak is the return, in philosophy, of the persecutors' point of view.

There remain the objections of the anthropologists. The first concerns method: a founding mechanism that leaves traces only in the myths that conceal it is, by construction, unverifiable, and any absence of a victim in a myth can be read as further proof of concealment. The second concerns universality: many societies practise sacrifice as an exchange, a gift, a meal shared with the gods, without the lynching scene being found in it, and field ethnologists have often reproached Girard for treating the ethnographic material as Frazer did, by selective comparisons. The third concerns closure: a theory that makes its own religion the key to all the others lays itself open to the suspicion of having been constructed to lead there. These objections are strong, and Girard has not, I believe, entirely answered them.

In this immense construction two storeys must be separated, one of which carries my conviction and the other not. The first is anthropological: mimetic desire, the crisis, the singling out of a scapegoat, the misrecognition that allows the crowd to believe itself innocent. It is a hypothesis, unverifiable in its originary version, but of considerable descriptive power, and the history of the twentieth century, with its designated victims and its unanimous crowds, has given it terrible confirmations. It gives the criterion of this essay an anthropological foundation. The second storey is apologetic: the Bible and the Gospels would be the only revelation of this mechanism, and Christianity the only way out. It seems to me much more fragile. It neglects the other traditions that have thought the innocence of the victim or refused sacrifice, Greek tragedy itself, which sometimes lets the victim's lament be heard, or the refusal of blood sacrifice among the prophets, which Girard indeed ties to the Bible, but also the wisdoms of India or China, which he hardly examines. And it makes historical Christianity, which had its stakes and its Inquisitors, the vehicle of a revelation it has betrayed so often that Dostoevsky could imagine Christ condemned a second time by his Church. Girard replies that the Church betrayed the Gospels, and that betrayal presupposes revelation. So be it. But then the exit from sacrifice no longer belongs to a religion: it belongs to anyone who refuses to join the crowd, believer or not. I shall draw the consequence of this in the conclusion, against Girard's apologetics and with his anthropology.

### Becker (1924-1974): religion as heroism against death

Ernest Becker was neither a theologian nor a professional philosopher, but an American cultural anthropologist who had spent his career on the margins of the university, dismissed or barely tolerated from one post to the next. *The Denial of Death* appeared in 1973; Becker died of cancer in March 1974, and the book received the Pulitzer Prize for general non-fiction two months later. This chronology made the book's legend: written by a man who knew he was dying and who drew from it a theory of the whole of culture. But the legend must not hide the thesis, which gives our question an answer that neither Feuerbach nor Freud had formulated in this form.

The thesis can be put in a few words, and I paraphrase it for want of having consulted the original text: the idea of death, and the terror it inspires, haunt the human animal more than anything else, and they are the mainspring of its activity. Man is the only animal that knows it will die; Bergson had written as much in 1932 in almost identical terms, “Les animaux ne savent pas qu'ils doivent mourir”, animals do not know that they must die, and he already drew from it a function of religion, “une réaction défensive de la nature contre la représentation, par l'intelligence, de l'inévitabilité de la mort”, a defensive reaction of nature against the representation, by the intelligence, of the inevitability of death. Becker does not cite Bergson, and the encounter is my doing; but it shows that the intuition is not peculiar to American psychology. Becker adds to it an anthropology of the hero. Man is a living contradiction: a mind capable of representing the infinite, housed in a body that decays. Unable to look this contradiction in the face, he covers it over with what Becker calls a *causa sui* project, the effort to be one's own cause, and with heroism, that is, the conviction of having a worth that outlasts death. Every culture is then a hero system: a set of roles, stories and rites that allows each person to believe himself important beyond his own disappearance.

Religion is, for Becker, the vastest of these systems, and his position is not a simple reduction. Becker starts from Freud, but he corrects him through Otto Rank, the former disciple whom Freud had rejected, and through Kierkegaard. From Rank he retains that the fear of death is more fundamental than sexuality, and that the Oedipus complex itself is a *causa sui* project, the child's desire to become his own father. From Kierkegaard he retains the analysis of anxiety as the dizziness of freedom before nothingness, and the conclusion that the only honest way out is faith, which acknowledges dependence instead of denying it. The book's last chapter is ambiguous: Becker judges there that religion in the Kierkegaardian sense, the faith that accepts creatureliness, is the highest heroism, because it alone does not lie about the human condition. He is no apologist; he says only that, if one must choose a vital lie, the one that admits itself as such is better than those that do not know themselves. This is Pascal's position, which Becker arrives at without having sought it: Pascalian *divertissement* is the mechanism of denial he describes, “les hommes n'ayant pu guérir la mort, la misère, l'ignorance, ils se sont avisés, pour se rendre heureux, de n'y point penser”, men, having been unable to cure death, wretchedness and ignorance, have taken it into their heads, in order to be happy, not to think about them.

The posthumous book *Escape from Evil* (1975) draws from this anthropology a consequence that bears directly on the criterion. People who live by hero systems cannot bear others to contest them, for contestation reopens the question the system was meant to close. Social evil then arises from the denial of death: others are sacrificed to prove to oneself that one will not die, enemies are designated so that one's own symbolic immortality remains uncontested. This is a theory of sacrifice, which meets Girard's by another path: the two men did not read each other, as far as I know, and the comparison is constructed. For the one, sacrifice protects the individual against death; for the other, it protects the community against its own violence. In both cases, religion is inseparable from a victim.

Experimental psychology has sought to put this thesis to the test. From 1986 onwards Jeff Greenberg, Sheldon Solomon and Tom Pyszczynski drew from it a terror management theory: worldviews and self-esteem would serve as a buffer against death anxiety, and being reminded of death (mortality salience) should strengthen attachment to one's worldview and hostility towards those who contest it. In the founding experiment, in 1989, judges first invited to think about their own death set heavier bonds for a prostitute: “judges recommended especially harsh bonds for a prostitute when mortality was made salient” (A. Rosenblatt et al., *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* 57, 1989, p. 681-690, abstract). But a large coordinated replication, *Many Labs 4* (R. Klein et al., *Collabra: Psychology* 8, 2022), dealing with another classic experiment of the theory (Greenberg et al., 1994), did not find the effect, across seventeen laboratories and 1,550 participants. The theory is not thereby refuted, but one of its most cited effects is wavering, and it can no longer be taken as established.

Becker first gives a precise answer to the question “why do people believe?”: because we die, and we know it. Then a consequence that meets the thesis of this essay: if every culture is a hero system against death, then the exits from religion that install a new absolute, History, the Nation, Race, Civilization, are not accidents; they are replacement hero systems, which promise symbolic immortality to those who serve them and refuse it to those who contest them. The sacrifice criterion here receives a psychological explanation. But Becker leaves open a question he does not resolve: can one live without a vital lie? Lucretius answered yes, provided one understands that death is nothing to us; Becker does not believe so, and this is what leads him back to Kierkegaard. I believe he is wrong on this point, or rather that he confuses two things: the need for meaning, which is universal, and the need for immortality, which perhaps is not. The spiritualities of exercise, from Spinoza to Hadot, do not deny death; they seek to look at it without lying to themselves. They are precisely what Becker's theory holds to be impossible, and that is why they remain, in this essay, the case that puts this theory to the test.

>
> Ivan Ilyich, an ordinary magistrate, has lived a respectable life and is dying of an illness the doctors do not name; the textbook syllogism, Caius is a man, men are mortal, therefore Caius is mortal, had always seemed to him right for Caius and not for himself. In the last chapter, after three days of screaming, the fear falls away: “‘And death! Where is it?’ He looked for his accustomed fear and did not find it. ‘Where is death?’ He was no longer afraid, for there was no more death. Instead of death he saw light.[…] ‘Death is finished… Death no longer exists!’ he said to himself. He drew in a breath, which remained unfinished, stiffened and died”.
>
> Why here: this is the denial of death according to Becker, and its way out. Ivan Ilyich has spent his life in a replacement hero system, his career, the drawing room, whist, and this system collapses before Caius; Tolstoy has him die in a light that the essay, with Becker, leaves undecided: is it faith regained or the end of the vital lie? The text does not say God; it says that death no longer exists, which Lucretius also said.
>
> Grimm's tale tells the same thing in a page. A poor man chooses Death as godfather to his thirteenth child, having refused God, who gives to the rich and lets the poor go hungry, and the devil, who deceives; Death presents himself: “Ich bin der Tod, der alle gleich macht”, I am Death, who makes all equal, and the man replies: “du bist der rechte, du holst den Reichen wie den Armen ohne Unterschied”, you are the right one, you take the rich as well as the poor without distinction; the child becomes a famous physician, who heals when Death stands at the sick person's feet and gives up when Death is at the head; twice he cheats Death, for a king and then for the king's daughter, whom he wants to marry; Death then leads him into his cave where the lights of all lives are burning: “„Zeige mir mein Lebenslicht“ sagte der Arzt und meinte es wäre noch recht groß. Culture as a hero system against death, says Becker; the tale says that the hero always believes himself a tall candle.
