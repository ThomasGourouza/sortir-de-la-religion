### Berger (1929-2017) and Luckmann (1927-2016): the sacred canopy and invisible religion

Peter L. Berger and Thomas Luckmann first wrote together a book that changed sociology, *The Social Construction of Reality* (1966). What we take for reality appears in it as the product of a three-stage process: human beings externalize their activity in institutions, these institutions are objectivated and take on the appearance of a nature, and they are then internalized by each person as self-evident. The following year, in 1967, each published a book on religion on his own, and the two books say different things, almost opposite things, which still structure the sociology of religion.

*The Sacred Canopy* (*La Religion dans la conscience moderne*, in the title of its French translation) applies the theory of social construction to religion. Every society, Berger says, is an enterprise of world-building, and this world, which he calls by a Greek word, *nomos*, is an order of meanings that shields human beings from chaos. The individual torn from this *nomos*, by bereavement, exile or catastrophe, loses his bearings in experience and becomes, in a phrase Tim Knepper picks out, “anomic in the sense of becoming world-less”. Religion is the most ambitious form of this protection. It is not content to order society; it installs this order in the cosmos, and so makes it untouchable: the sacred canopy is the roof of meanings that each society stretches over its world. Religion makes people forget that the *nomos* was built; it presents it as existing since the beginning of time. This is Feuerbach's alienation, translated into sociology: man forgets that he is the author of his world, and receives it as a gift of the gods.

The chapter on theodicy is the most profound in the book. Every religion must account for suffering and death, which threaten the *nomos* at its most fragile point. Berger draws from this a thesis that overturns the Marxist critique. It is usually believed that religion consoles by promising happiness; Berger writes, on the contrary: “It is not happiness that theodicy primarily provides, but meaning”. Human beings can bear suffering, not its meaninglessness. One recognizes Nietzsche's sentence on the meaninglessness of suffering; Berger does not quote it, and the comparison is constructed. The consequence is clear: if religion provides meaning rather than happiness, it cannot be replaced by material progress, and the hope of the Enlightenment, which thought that religion would disappear along with poverty, rests on a misunderstanding.

Yet in 1967 Berger drew a prediction of secularization from his book. The canopy holds by what he calls plausibility structures: a religion is believed only if one's surroundings take it as self-evident. But modernity is pluralist; it brings everyone face to face with other beliefs, and pluralism undermines plausibility. A belief chosen among others no longer has the self-evidence of a world; it becomes a preference. Berger concluded that religion would decline; thirty years later, he publicly retracted. In the essay that opens *The Desecularization of the World* (1999), a volume he edited, he writes that the idea that we live in a secularized world is false: “The world today[…] is as furiously religious as it ever was, and in some places more so than ever”. Berger himself makes one exception, that of an “international subculture composed of people with Western-type higher education, especially in the humanities and social sciences”. The rise of militant Islam and of worldwide Pentecostalism seemed to him to refute the theory he had himself helped to establish. He later specified, in his last books, that modernity produces pluralism rather than secularization. The correction matters: Berger is right against the prophecy that religion would disappear, but he does not say that religion has remained the same. It has become a choice, and a choice does not have the same authority as a world.

Luckmann, the same year, had proposed another reading, subtler and closer to our spiritualities without God. *The Invisible Religion* (1967, a revised version of a German book of 1963) starts from a very broad definition of religion: religious is everything by which the human organism transcends its biological nature and gives itself a world of meaning. In this sense, no society is without religion, and the question of secularization is wrongly posed. What has changed, according to Luckmann, is not the presence of religion but its location. The Churches have lost their monopoly, and religion has moved into the private sphere, where each person composes his own system of ultimate meanings around new themes: self-expression, self-realization, autonomy, family and emotional life. This religion is invisible because it no longer passes through institutions that a sociologist could count; privatized, it has not disappeared.

Luckmann's contribution is to show that the “spiritual but not religious” of Part Twelve is not a contemporary curiosity but the modern form of an anthropological function: self-realization is the sacred of individualist society. The danger of his definition is that if everything that gives meaning is religion, the question of our essay becomes empty: one never leaves religion, since even the exit is one. This definition has the weakness of Durkheim's without its strength: a definition that encompasses everything no longer allows anything to be distinguished. Luckmann's private sacred, self-realization, does not ordinarily demand anyone's sacrifice; it can do so, when self-realization becomes a limitless imperative, but it does not do so by nature. Berger and Luckmann, read together, therefore give our question its two sociological terms: religion as a shelter of meaning, which does not disappear, and religion as an institution, which can recede. The exit from religion, in the sense of this essay, concerns the second; the need of which the first speaks does not condemn it in advance.

### Hervieu-Léger (b. 1947): religion as memory, believing without a lineage

Danièle Hervieu-Léger, a sociologist of religion and director of studies at the École des hautes études en sciences sociales, proposed in 1993 a definition of religion that shifts the question of Durkheim and Luckmann. She defines religion neither by its content, God or the supernatural, nor by its function, meaning or social cohesion, but by a particular mode of believing. In *La Religion pour mémoire*, she calls it “un dispositif idéologique, pratique et symbolique par lequel est constituée, entretenue, développée et contrôlée la conscience (individuelle et collective) de l'appartenance à une lignée croyante” (p. 119), an ideological, practical and symbolic apparatus by which the consciousness (individual and collective) of belonging to a believing lineage is constituted, maintained, developed and controlled. Everything hangs on the word lineage. To believe religiously is to believe in the name of a chain, invoking the authority of a tradition that goes back to founding witnesses and that one takes on the task of transmitting. What distinguishes a religion from a philosophy or a morality is not its object; it is this authority of memory.

The definition makes it possible to say what is happening to European societies without speaking either of disappearance or of return. Modernity, Hervieu-Léger explains, is structurally amnesiac: it values innovation, mobility, the present, and it undoes the chains of memory that transmitted belief. Believing does not disappear for all that; it comes loose from the lineage. People still believe, and a great deal, but without invoking the authority of a tradition, composing their belief from borrowed fragments. This is what she calls, in *Le Pèlerin et le converti. La religion en mouvement* (1999), a deregulation of believing. She draws from it two typical figures of contemporary religiosity. The pilgrim is the one who journeys on, who has experiences without stopping anywhere, who judges a belief by what it gives him and leaves it when it gives him nothing more. The convert is his apparent opposite: the one who chooses a strong belonging, often after a crisis, and who finds in a demanding community the identity that society no longer gives him. These two figures are the two faces of a single world: both choose, and it is this choice that makes them modern, even when the convert chooses the most rigid tradition.

These two figures replace a third. The religion of traditional societies had as its central figure the practitioner, the one who regularly performs the gestures prescribed by an institution, in a parish where he was born and where he will die. This figure has not disappeared, but it has ceased to be the norm, and Hervieu-Léger described its decline in France in *Catholicisme, la fin d'un monde* (2003), where she shows that the Catholic Church has lost not only adherents but the capacity to organize the common culture. What fades with the practitioner is religion as shared self-evidence, what Berger called the canopy. What replaces it is not unbelief but a religiosity of trajectory, judged by the authenticity of experience rather than by conformity to a rule. One recognizes here, transposed into sociology, the distinction the essay draws from Foucault and Hadot between religion as obedience and spirituality as exercise, except that the sociologist does not judge. It is the convert who touches on the criterion. He seeks a lineage because he did not receive one, and he is all the more demanding for having chosen it: it is in him that chosen religion can become once more a religion that demands sacrifices, and the recent religious radicalizations in Europe have shown this in a way the sociologist had anticipated without foreseeing its violence.

This analysis converges with that of the British sociologist Grace Davie, who had described post-war Britain as a society of *believing without belonging*, and with that of Marcel Gauchet, whom Part Eleven will examine. But Hervieu-Léger is more precise than Gauchet on one point. She does not say that structuring religion has disappeared and that private beliefs remain; she says that the need for a lineage survives the disappearance of lineages, and that it seeks substitutes. Hence the importance she gives to secular forms of collective memory, national, political, familial, which can take on a religious colouring when they present themselves as the continuation of a sacred chain. Here, through sociology, we meet the question of refoundations again. Barrès's Nation, the militants' Revolution, the Civilization that Onfray defends in *Décadence*, are substitute believing lineages: they authorize belief through fidelity to ancestors, and they can demand, like religions, that something be sacrificed to them.

This definition is useful because it distinguishes what the others conflate. The need for meaning, described by Berger and Becker, can be satisfied by a philosophy; the need for connection, described by Durkheim, by an association; but the need for a lineage, for an authority that precedes us and that we transmit, is specifically religious, in Hervieu-Léger's sense. And it is this need that the spiritualities without God of Part Twelve find hardest to satisfy. Hadot could invoke the lineage of the ancient schools, and that is his strength; Comte-Sponville invokes Spinoza and Epicurus, but by a personal choice, not by a transmission. The modern pilgrim has no lineage; he has a library. Is this a loss? Hervieu-Léger, as a sociologist, is right not to say. For our question, the answer is clear: a spirituality without a lineage escapes the risk of refoundation, since it has no ancestors in whose name to demand a sacrifice; it pays for this freedom with a fragility, since it is not transmitted. That is perhaps the price of every exit that does not refound.

### Boyer: why gods are easy to think

Pascal Boyer, a French anthropologist trained in Paris and Cambridge, who teaches at Washington University in St. Louis, published in 2001 *Religion Explained*, which became the manifesto of a field of research born in the 1990s: the cognitive science of religion. His question is not that of our nineteenth-century authors. He does not ask whether religion is true, nor what it is for, nor what desire it satisfies; he asks why certain religious ideas are so easy to acquire, to remember and to transmit, while others, just as conceivable, never appear. And his answer shifts the whole debate: religion rests neither on a special faculty nor on a special need, but on the ordinary functioning of the human mind.

The argument starts from inference systems. The human mind is neither a blank slate nor a general reasoner; it has specialized devices, shaped by evolution to deal with distinct domains: physical objects, living beings, persons and their intentions, social relations, contamination and danger. They work without our knowing it, which is why their products seem obvious to us. Religious concepts, Boyer shows, are concepts that violate on one precise point the expectations of one of these categories while respecting all the others: a spirit that has no body but sees, hears and remembers; a statue that listens to prayers; a dead ancestor who gets angry. These minimally counterintuitive concepts are better remembered than ordinary concepts, because they surprise, and better than concepts that are too bizarre, because they remain usable: one can reason about a jealous god, one cannot reason about a god who would be at once everything and nothing. Religion is neither a stroke of genius nor an aberration, but a by-product of mental devices that serve other purposes.

The subtlest point of the theory concerns the gods themselves. Why are supernatural beings almost always persons, who see, will and judge? Because the human mind is first of all a social mind, which spends its time wondering what others know about it and what they are going to do. Gods, for Boyer, are agents who have access to all strategic information: they know what we have done, what we have hidden, what others think of us. This is what makes them relevant to morality and social life, and it explains why one does not pray to a god to reveal the structure of the atom, but to see our distress or punish our enemy. The psychologist Justin Barrett added to this theory a device he calls the *hyperactive agent detection device*: our ancestors had an advantage in seeing a predator behind every rustle of leaves, even at the cost of being often wrong, rather than missing the one that was real, and we have inherited this tendency to detect agents where there is only chance. The idea goes back to the anthropologist Stewart Guthrie's book on faces in the clouds (1993), and its ancestry is older than its authors say, since Hume had formulated it in 1757: “We find human faces in the moon, armies in the clouds”, and by a natural propensity we ascribe malice or good will to everything that hurts or pleases us.

This theory explains very well what our authors explained badly: why religion is universal, why it takes similar forms everywhere, why it resists arguments so well. It also answers, in its own way, the question of Bergson, who held religion to be “coextensive à notre espèce”, coextensive with our species, and concluded that it “doit tenir à notre structure”, must be bound up with our structure: Boyer gives this structure a precise content, which Bergson could not have known. Finally, it contains a consequence that Boyer formulates, according to Anthony Campbell, with a certain relish: “science is every bit as 'unnatural' to the human mind as religion is 'natural'”. The Enlightenment's faith in the spontaneous disappearance of religion was therefore doubly naive: it misjudged the ease of belief and the difficulty of science.

But the theory is often credited with more than it claims. First, explaining why a belief is easy to acquire says nothing about its truth. This is what philosophy calls the genetic fallacy, and it works both ways: that our devices incline us to see agents everywhere does not prove that there are none, just as our natural aptitude for counting does not prove that numbers do not exist. Daniel Dennett, who popularized this research in *Breaking the Spell* (2006), sometimes forgets this; Boyer is more cautious. Feuerbach, on this point, was more lucid than his naturalist successors: he did not claim that religion was false because it came from man; he said that it told the truth about man. Next, the theory explains religious representations, spirits, gods, rites, but it poorly explains what interests our essay most: the experience of the mystics, spiritual exercise, conversion. The reviewer Joseph Bulbulia, in the Australian journal *Eras*, reproached it for this reason with a misleading title: religion is not a single cognitive dimension, and only some of its aspects are explained. Finally, and this is the most important point for us, the theory says why gods are easy to think, not why we need them. Boyer in fact rejects the notion of a religious need: there is, in his view, no need for God, but a disposition to conceive him when a culture offers him. This is the lasting contribution of the cognitive sciences to our question. They do not refute religion; they refute the idea that there exists in man an empty place, of exactly the shape of God, which the exit from religion would leave gaping.

### Norris and Inglehart: existential security, the empirical arbiter

All the preceding authors make hypotheses about the causes of belief; two political scientists set out to measure them. Pippa Norris, of Harvard University, and Ronald Inglehart (1934-2021), of the University of Michigan, founder of the great international survey of values, the *World Values Survey*, published in 2004 *Sacred and Secular. Religion and Politics Worldwide* (Cambridge University Press, second edition 2011). The book draws on surveys conducted in nearly eighty societies and proposes to revise the classical theory of secularization rather than abandon it, as Berger had done in 1999.

The thesis comes down to one notion, *existential security*. Religiosity depends, in their view, neither on the religious supply, as economists of religion maintain, nor on the progress of scientific knowledge, as the Enlightenment believed, but on the degree of vulnerability in which people live. Where the death of a child, famine, untreated illness, old age without resources, violence without recourse are daily threats, religion remains strong, because it provides insurance against what cannot be mastered. Where the welfare state, medicine and prosperity have made existence predictable, religiosity recedes from one generation to the next, without anyone having been convinced by an argument. The most secularized societies in the world are the most egalitarian and best protected, the Scandinavian countries; the most religious are the poorest and most exposed. And the case of the United States, long invoked against the theory of secularization, confirms it in their view: it is the most unequal and least protected of rich societies, and it is also the most religious.

The thesis has a second half, less often cited, which forbids reading it as a prophecy of the end of religions. Religious societies have more children than secularized societies. The result is a demographic paradox: rich societies are becoming secularized, but the share of the world's population living in religious societies is growing, so that the world, taken as a whole, is not becoming less religious. Berger, who saw the world as “furiously religious”, and the theorists of secularization, who saw Europe emptying, were therefore both right, each about one half of the world.

In *Religion's Sudden Decline. What's Causing It, and What Comes Next?* (Oxford University Press, 2021), published in the year of his death, Inglehart notes that the decline of religiosity accelerates from the end of the 2000s, the United States included. He explains it by the same security: religious norms favouring high fertility, indispensable when infant mortality was high, have lost their usefulness, and the religions that presented them as absolutes have been weakened by this.

This work has real limits. First, these are opinion surveys: they measure what people declare, the importance of God in their lives, the frequency of their prayer, not what they experience. Next, the correlation is very strong between countries but much weaker between individuals within the same country, and the authors had to respond on this point in their second edition: the poorest in a rich society are not always the most religious. Finally, the notion of religion they measure is Durkheim's more than James's: it captures practice and adherence, it poorly captures the diffuse spirituality that Luckmann and Hervieu-Léger describe. A society where no one goes to church any more but where everyone meditates could appear, in these surveys, perfectly secularized.

These limits do not take away from Norris and Inglehart their role as arbiters: their data decide between the explanations of belief. They prove Lucretius and Hume, Freud and Becker right, who derived religion from fear and distress: when distress recedes, religion recedes. They prove the Enlightenment wrong, which expected the exit from religion from the progress of ideas: it is not critique that secularizes, it is health insurance. But they also raise an uncomfortable question: if the exit from religion is the effect of security, it is not an act of freedom; a protected society no longer needs heaven, but it remains exposed to the return of insecurity, and the history of the twentieth century shows that substitute religions thrive precisely in crises.

Norris and Inglehart's thesis is opposed to another sociological explanation, from the United States. The so-called religious market school, associated with the names of Rodney Stark, Roger Finke and Laurence Iannaccone, explained American religious vitality by competition between Churches: where the state protects no denomination, the Churches must attract their faithful, and supply stimulates demand; where a state Church enjoys a monopoly, as in Scandinavia, it falls asleep and its faithful leave it. Norris and Inglehart reply that religious demand is not constant, as this theory supposes, but varies with security. The disagreement bears on our very question: is there a stable religious need, which supply alone causes to vary, or a disposition whose intensity depends on living conditions? The data argue rather for the second answer. The American sociologist Phil Zuckerman, in *Society without God* (2008), carried out fieldwork in Denmark and Sweden, among populations largely detached from any belief, and found there societies that seem to suffer from no lack that could be attributed to the absence of religion. This is, as we shall see, the strongest argument against the thesis of need.
