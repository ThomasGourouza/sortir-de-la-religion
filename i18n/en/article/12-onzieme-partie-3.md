### Marcel Gauchet (b. 1946): Christianity, the religion of the exit from religion

With Marcel Gauchet, the quarrel leaves Germany and changes its object. Blumenberg asked whether modernity owed anything to Christianity; Gauchet asks what a religion is, and answers that it is not first of all a belief. *The Disenchantment of the World: A Political History of Religion* (1985) is one of the most ambitious books French thought has devoted to our question, and its central formula seems written for this essay: Christianity is defined there as “la religion de la sortie de la religion”, the religion of the exit from religion.

The formula presupposes a definition of religion that is political before it is theological. A religion, for Gauchet, is a way of instituting the social: society receives its order from a source that is external, prior and untouchable. The rules of common life are not made by human beings; they are given to them by the gods, the ancestors, the time of origins. This is what Gauchet calls heteronomy, and he sees in stateless societies, those that ethnologists study, the purest form of religion: not the most mystical, but the one in which dispossession is most complete, in which the present has no power over a law fixed once and for all in the mythical past. Religion is not an illusion about heaven, as in Feuerbach; it is a way for human beings to divest themselves of their own instituting power.

The history of religions becomes the history of the loosening of this dispossession. The appearance of the State, by separating the powerful from the rest, already opens a breach: the law now has human representatives, and what has been established can be transformed. The great religions of the Axial Age, and above all monotheism, move the divine away from the world: a single God, transcendent, separate from creation, leaves the world to human beings in a way that gods mingled with nature did not allow. Christianity carries this movement to its end. Through the incarnation, God becomes man and consecrates the dignity of the human world; through the separation of the two realms, rendering unto Caesar what is Caesar's, it withdraws the political order from immediate divine law. It thus carries within its structure the possibility of a world that will do without it in order to organize itself. Gauchet also says that Christianity was “la religion possible d'une société d'après la religion”, the possible religion of a society after religion.

Gauchet himself had to correct a misreading: the exit from religion does not mean the disappearance of believers. In *La Religion dans la démocratie* (1998), he writes: “Sortie de la religion ne signifie pas sortie de la croyance religieuse, mais sortie d'un monde où la religion est structurante[…] et où elle définit l'économie du lien social”, the exit from religion does not mean an exit from religious belief, but an exit from a world in which religion is structuring and in which it defines the economy of the social bond. One may believe in God in a society that has left religion; one simply believes there as an individual, and faith becomes a matter of personal identity and choice, not the foundation of the common order. Democracy is the regime of a society that gives itself its own law, and that must therefore live without an external guarantor: it is autonomy after heteronomy.

Gauchet touches several figures of this essay. First, he proves the Nietzsche of the *Genealogy* (III, 27) and of § 357 of *The Gay Science* right on a level Nietzsche had not explored. Nietzsche said that Christian truthfulness had turned against Christianity; Gauchet says that the very structure of Christianity produced the world that makes it useless. The two theses converge: the exit from religion is a *Christian* event. Next, he ruins Onfray's position in *Décadence*: what Onfray describes as a death, the exhaustion of an organism, Gauchet describes as a fulfilment. Christianity does not die like a living thing that has exhausted its strength; it realizes itself in a world that no longer needs it in order to stand. I present this opposition as a constructed confrontation: I have found no text in which Gauchet discusses *Décadence*.

The strongest objection to Gauchet came from Quebec, and it is attested. The sociologist Fernand Dumont, relayed by Cantin, refuses to confine Christianity to the private sphere: to reduce religion to its instituting function is to decide in advance that faith has nothing more to say to the common world once that function is lost. I believe the objection is sound, but it bears less against Gauchet's history than against his definition. Gauchet defined religion by heteronomy, which can by definition recede: he built his concept in such a way that an exit from it is possible. What he does not say is what becomes of the demand for meaning when it no longer has an instituted form: Gauchet is the historian of the exit, not the thinker of what remains. He confirms, finally, what the prologue drew from Lactantius: the question “can one leave religion without founding another?” is first of all a Christian question.

Gauchet in fact debated this very question with Luc Ferry: a public debate is said to have brought them together in January 1999, and their exchange produced a book, *Le Religieux après la religion* (2004). The title states what is at stake: if religion has ceased to structure the social, what remains of the religious, of the dimension of existence that found expression in it? Not having read the book, I do not report their arguments; but their other works make it possible to say where the line runs. Gauchet describes an exit whose end point is autonomy, and he distrusts any attempt to reinject the sacred into democracy, which he judges destined to live without an ultimate guarantor. Ferry maintains that modern humanism has kept a form of transcendence, displaced from heaven to man. The one thinks the exit, the other what survives it, and this disagreement announces the passage from religion as institution to spirituality as experience.

### Charles Taylor (b. 1931): against subtraction stories

Charles Taylor, a Canadian and Catholic philosopher, published in 2007 *A Secular Age*, a book of nearly nine hundred pages that takes up the question from what he calls the conditions of belief. He distinguishes three senses of the word “secular”: public spaces emptied of all reference to God, where politics, the economy and science function without him; the decline of religious belief and practice that sociologists measure; and finally, the subject of the book, a change in the very conditions of belief: the shift, Taylor writes, “from a society where belief in God is unchallenged and indeed, unproblematic, to one in which it is understood to be one option among others, and frequently not the easiest to embrace”.

The polemical heart of the book targets a way of telling the story of modernity that Taylor calls “subtraction stories”. These are the accounts that explain modernity “by human beings having lost, or sloughed off, or liberated themselves from certain earlier, confining horizons, or illusions, or limitations of knowledge”. Modern man would be what remains once religion has been removed, as one strips a layer of paint to uncover the wall. Taylor argues that this is false: what remains when God is removed is not a bare nature; it is a new historical construction, with its own ideals, its own moral demands, its own imaginary.

Taylor describes this construction under several names. The immanent frame is the order that can be understood entirely without transcendence, a natural, social and moral order that holds up by itself. Exclusive humanism is the possibility, available on a wide scale for the first time, of a life oriented towards human flourishing with nothing beyond it: according to Taylor, “a purely self-sufficient humanism came to be a widely available option”, defined as “a humanism accepting no final goals beyond human flourishing”. Taylor finally describes what he calls the nova effect: not a retreat of religion before unbelief, but an explosion of intermediate positions, deisms, spiritualities, humanisms, religions of art, which proliferate between faith and atheism. I have not found an exact formula of Taylor's for these last three terms; I give them in paraphrase.

The kinship between Taylor and this essay is great, and the difference instructive. Taylor rejects subtraction stories as I reject the idea of a total exit: one does not leave religion as one leaves a room, with a neutral void left behind. But he draws from this a conclusion I do not draw: exclusive humanism is one construction among others, and Christian faith remains a living option, which the secular age makes more difficult but not less true; he speaks of a fullness that everyone seeks, believer or unbeliever, and that exclusive humanism struggles to name. Taylor is a loyal believing adversary of modern critique: he does not deny the legitimacy of unbelief, he denies that it is the simple result of a lucidity at last achieved. The objection he makes to Feuerbach, to Marx, to Freud is the same as Stirner's, turned around: you think you have subtracted God, but you have built something else, and you must answer for what you have built. This comparison with Stirner, which Taylor does not make to my knowledge, is a constructed confrontation.

### Jürgen Habermas (1929-2026): translation and the post-secular society

The last great actor in the quarrel is Jürgen Habermas, long the philosopher of modernity as an unfinished project, who was expected to defend secular reason against the religions. On 14 October 2001, a month after the attacks of 11 September, he received in Frankfurt the Peace Prize of the German Book Trade and delivered a speech entitled “Glauben und Wissen”, *Faith and Knowledge*, which surprised his readers. In it he introduced an expression that would become famous: one must ask, he said, what secularization means “in unseren postsäkularen Gesellschaften”, in our post-secular societies.

A post-secular society is not a society in which religion returns in force, but a society that acknowledges that religious communities are not disappearing, contrary to what secularization theory predicted, and that must learn to live with them without renouncing the neutrality of the State. Habermas proposes a rule of reciprocity. The believing citizen must agree to translate his reasons into a language accessible to all, so that his convictions can enter public deliberation without requiring those who do not believe to adhere to his faith. But the secular citizen, for his part, must keep “einen Sinn für die Artikulationskraft religiöser Sprachen”, a sense of the articulating power of religious languages. He must not presume that religious languages say nothing that reason does not already know how to say, but admit that they have perhaps preserved moral intuitions, about dignity, suffering, guilt, reparation, that secular language has not yet managed to formulate.

Hence the central sentence of the speech: “Eine Säkularisierung, die nicht vernichtet, vollzieht sich im Modus der Übersetzung”, a secularization that does not annihilate is accomplished in the mode of translation. Habermas gives an example of it, taken from Genesis: man created in the image of God (Gn 1, 27). Faced with the possibilities of genetic engineering, this idea of creatureliness says something that secular ethics struggles to say: that a human being must not be the product of another human being's design. To translate is neither to keep the religious content as it is nor to throw it away; it is to carry it over into a language that all can understand. And Habermas gives this operation an illustrious model: Kant provided, he says, “das erste große Beispiel für eine säkularisierende und zugleich rettende Dekonstruktion von Glaubenswahrheiten”, the first great example of a deconstruction of the truths of faith that is at once secularizing and redemptive. One recognizes the Kant of Part Four, the Kant of *Die Religion innerhalb der Grenzen der bloßen Vernunft*: to reduce religion to morality, but to save in morality what was rational in religion.

In January 2004, Habermas debated at the Catholic Academy of Bavaria with Cardinal Joseph Ratzinger, who the following year would become Pope Benedict XVI, on the pre-political foundations of the constitutional State; the dialogue is attested under the title “Dialektik der Säkularisierung”, but I have read none of its formulas and I do not quote it. He then took up the whole question again in a vast genealogy, *Auch eine Geschichte der Philosophie* (2019), which I have not consulted.

Habermas opens a third way between Löwith and Blumenberg. Against Löwith, he does not think that modernity merely repeats religion under another name; against Blumenberg, he does not think it owes religion nothing. It owes it contents, but these contents are not stolen: they are *translated*, and translation is a labour that transforms what it transmits. This is the most reasonable position in the quarrel, and I believe it is true for a large part of our moral heritage. But it leaves our question intact, for Habermasian translation bears only on the *normative contents* of religion, what can enter public deliberation, and says nothing about what, in religion, does not allow itself to be translated into reasons: prayer, worship, the experience of presence, the relation to death, consolation. Habermas himself takes up Weber's phrase: in *Glauben und Wissen* (2001), he speaks of a biblical intuition “die in unserem Zusammenhang auch dem religiös Unmusikalischen etwas sagen kann”, which in our context can say something even to one who is religiously unmusical; that he applied the expression to himself is often reported, but I have not found the primary source. What does not translate is what Part Twelve will call spirituality: Habermas said how reason can inherit from religion; he did not say how the individual can live without it.

> Faust, in his study, opens the New Testament to translate the beginning of the Gospel of John, and cannot leave the word alone: “Geschrieben steht: Im Anfang war der Sinn.[…] Es sollte stehn: Im Anfang war die Kraft![…] Mir hilft der Geist! >
> Why here: this is Habermas's thesis in a single scene, and it has two sides. The secularization that does not annihilate is accomplished in the mode of translation: Faust translates, and his translation transforms what it transmits, the Logos become deed, which Marx will say again in the eleventh thesis on Feuerbach. But Goethe also shows what happens while one translates: the poodle growls, and it is Mephistopheles who steps out from behind the stove. Habermasian translation retains only the normative contents; the scene reminds us that what does not allow itself to be translated does not disappear for all that.
>
> Lessing had proposed, thirty years earlier, the model of all translations to come: “Erziehung giebt dem Menschen nichts, was er nicht auch aus sich selbst haben könnte: sie giebt ihm das, was er aus sich selber haben könnte, nur geschwinder und leichter. So revelation, too, gives the human race nothing that human reason, left to itself, would not also arrive at. Revelation as the pedagogy of reason: Habermas is its direct heir, and the essay's objection holds for both, what reason cannot give itself, prayer, worship, experience, remains outside the school.

### Assessment of the quarrel

The quarrel over secularization does not settle our question; it reformulates it and forces me to correct myself. Schmitt showed that one can want refoundation: political theology, openly assumed, is refoundation without a mask. Löwith gave the thesis its historical support: philosophies of history are soteriologies without God, and that is why they were able to demand sacrifices. Voegelin and Aron gave the criterion its most massive cases, the secular religions of the twentieth century. Blumenberg showed that the thesis, taken in its extensive version, proves too much, and forced it to make its criterion precise: there is refoundation only when an absolute demands the sacrifice of the individual. Strauss recalled that the exit itself has not been demonstrated, and that the question remains open. Gauchet showed that the exit from religion is itself a religious event, and that our question is a Christian one. Taylor showed that exclusive humanism is not what remains when God is subtracted, but a construction that must answer for itself. Habermas showed that an exit that does not destroy is a translation.

These authors nonetheless have one thing in common: all of them speak of religion as an institution, an order, a collective structure of meaning, a set of normative contents. None really speaks of what the individual lives through when he prays, meditates, recollects himself, or seeks to transform his own existence. It is perhaps there that the possibility of an exit without refoundation is decided. One can leave a Church without leaving the demand it answered; one can stop believing without ceasing to seek a way of life that engages one's whole existence. At the end of the twentieth century this demand received a name that is today travelling around the world: spirituality. We must now ask whether this word designates a real way out or a new shadow of God in the cave.
