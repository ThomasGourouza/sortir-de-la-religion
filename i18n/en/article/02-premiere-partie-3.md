### Maimonides (1138-1204): of God, we know only what he is not

Moses ben Maimon was also born in Cordoba. His family fled after the Almohad conquest of 1148, wandered through Andalusia and Morocco, then settled in Egypt, at Fustat, where Maimonides became head of the Jewish community and physician at the court of Saladin. We owe him the greatest code of Jewish law, the *Mishneh Torah*, written in Hebrew, and the greatest book of Jewish philosophy of the Middle Ages, written around 1190 in Arabic set down in Hebrew characters: *Dalālat al-ḥāʾirīn*, which the Hebrew translators rendered as *Moreh nevukhim*, the Latins as *Dux neutrorum* or *Dux perplexorum*, and Salomon Munk, in the standard French translation (1856-1866), as *Le Guide des égarés*, the guide of those who have gone astray. Munk notes that the translation closest to the literal sense would be *Guide des perplexes*, guide of the perplexed, but that it would have “l'inconvénient de paraître prétentieuse”, the drawback of seeming pretentious.

The introduction defines its addressee with a precision that speaks volumes. The book aims “de donner l'éveil à l'homme religieux chez lequel la vérité de notre Loi est établie dans l'âme et devenue un objet de croyance, qui est parfait dans sa religion et dans ses mœurs, qui a étudié les sciences des philosophes”, to awaken the religious man in whose soul the truth of our Law is established and has become an object of belief, who is perfect in his religion and his morals, who has studied the sciences of the philosophers, but who is “embarrassé par le sens extérieur (littéral) de la Loi”, troubled by the external (literal) sense of the Law, so that he “reste dans l'agitation et dans le trouble”, remains in agitation and confusion (Introduction, trans. Munk). The perplexed man is neither the unbeliever nor the simple, but the cultivated believer who has read Aristotle and can no longer read the Bible to the letter. Maimonides does not invite him to choose: he teaches him to read.

The first lesson concerns names. The Bible speaks of a God who sees, who hears, who comes down, who sits, who has a hand and a face. A large part of the first part of the *Guide* is a lexicon of these terms, showing for each that it is a homonym, and that taken in its proper sense it would make God a body. Yet God's incorporeality is demonstrated, and it governs the reading. Maimonides says so with a frankness that Spinoza will copy out in order to combat it: if he rejects the eternity of the world, it is not because of the verses on creation, for “de même que nous avons expliqué ces endroits de l'Écriture de façon à éloigner de la nature de Dieu toute matérialité, nous aurions également trouvé moyen d'interpréter les passages sur la création dans un sens favorable à l'éternité du monde”, just as we have explained those places in Scripture so as to remove all materiality from the nature of God, we would likewise have found a way to interpret the passages on creation in a sense favourable to the eternity of the world; it is only that the eternity of the world “n'est établie par aucune démonstration”, is established by no demonstration (*Guide*, II, 25, quoted in the *Theological-Political Treatise*, ch. VII, trans. Saisset). The revealed text yields before demonstration, not before mere probability. Creation is therefore upheld, but for want of a proof to the contrary, and Maimonides leaves open, in the eyes of an attentive reader, a question that faith believed closed.

The second lesson goes further and founds a tradition. If no name fits God in its proper sense, nothing positive can be said of him. “Nous ne saisissons de lui autre chose, si ce n'est qu'il est, mais non pas ce qu'il est”, we grasp nothing of him except that he is, but not what he is (I, 58). The only legitimate attributes are negative: to say that God is living is to say that he is not dead; powerful, that he is not powerless. Knowledge of God advances by subtraction: “à mesure que tu augmentes les négations à l'égard de Dieu, tu t'approches de la perception”, as you multiply the negations concerning God, you draw nearer to apprehension (I, 59). The end of this path is silence. Maimonides finds it in Psalm 65, 2, *lekha dumiyya tehilla*, which he reads thus: “pour toi le silence est la louange”, for you, silence is praise (I, 59). For, he adds, “quoi que ce soit que nous disions dans le but d'exalter et de glorifier (Dieu), nous y trouverons quelque chose d'offensant à l'égard de Dieu”, whatever we say with the aim of exalting and glorifying (God), we shall find in it something offensive towards God.

This negative theology joins the critique of religion: from within faith, it destroys all the representations of God that popular faith sustains: the God who grows angry, who rewards, who hears prayers in the way a king hears a petition. Eckhart received it; he quotes “Rabbi Moyses” abundantly in his Latin commentaries, and the debt is well enough established for a recent study to bear precisely that title, “*Verba sunt Rabbi Moysis*”. When Eckhart prays God to rid him of God, he carries into prayer the negation that Maimonides had carried into silence.

It also has a modern adversary. Feuerbach, in *Das Wesen des Christenthums*, attacks the theology that denies God every positive predicate. A God of whom every determination is denied, he says, has an existence “ohne Existenz”, an existence without existence, indistinguishable from nothingness; and the negation of the positive predicates of the divine essence is nothing other, in his view, than “ein subtiler, verschlagner Atheismus”, a subtle and cunning atheism, which keeps the appearance of religion and is not recognized as negation (1841, Introduction, ch. 2, p. 21-22 of the first edition). Feuerbach does not name Maimonides in this passage, so the confrontation is constructed; it is the best objection that can be made to the *Guide*. For Feuerbach, religion lives on predicates: to remove them all is to leave it without daring to say so. For Maimonides, true religion begins when they are removed; to keep them is idolatry. I believe Feuerbach is right about the ordinary believer, who does not pray to a God of whom he knows nothing, and Maimonides about the philosopher, who cannot worship a God he understands. This disagreement runs through the whole essay: it sets those for whom religion is a representation against those for whom it is the surpassing of all representation.

The third lesson, the most surprising, makes Maimonides, in my view, one of the first theorists of the historical function of rites. In the third part of the *Guide*, he undertakes to give the reasons for the commandments, the *ṭaʿamei ha-mitzvot*, and comes up against the sacrifices, which occupy an immense place in the Torah and for which no philosophical reason accounts. His answer is historical: God proceeded with the Hebrews as nature proceeds with the infant, by degrees: “comme il est impossible de passer subitement d'un extrême à l'autre, l'homme, selon sa nature, ne saurait quitter brusquement toutes ses habitudes”, since it is impossible to pass suddenly from one extreme to the other, man, by his nature, cannot abruptly abandon all his habits (III, 32). Now the universal worship of the time consisted in offering animals in the temples of idols. To forbid all worship of that kind would have been as inconceivable as a prophet coming today to say that God forbids prayer and fasting. “C'est pourquoi Dieu laissa subsister ces différentes espèces de cultes ; mais, au lieu d'être rendues à des objets créés et à des choses imaginaires, sans réalité, il les a transférées à son nom”, that is why God allowed these various kinds of worship to remain; but instead of their being rendered to created objects and to imaginary things without reality, he transferred them to his name (III, 32). The sacrifices are not willed for their own sake; they are a detour, like the detour through the desert that God has the people take on leaving Egypt so as not to expose them to war (Ex 13, 17-18), an example Maimonides himself invokes.

Let us weigh this thesis. It says that part of the revealed Law is explained by the historical conditions of its revelation and by the psychology of a people, that it is a pedagogical concession and not an eternal truth: a functional and historical explanation of worship, given from within faith by the greatest rabbi of his century. Hume, Durkheim and Weber will say in other ways what rites do to human beings; Maimonides had said it by referring it to the wisdom of God. The step separating his explanation from an explanation without God is a short one: it is enough to remove the divine “ruse” and keep the history. Maimonides does not take it, but he showed where it lay.

There remains the question of writing. The *Guide* itself announces that it contradicts itself, and that it does so on purpose. Among the seven causes of contradiction it lists in its introduction, the seventh is “la nécessité du discours, quand il s'agit de choses très obscures dont les détails doivent être en partie dérobés et en partie révélés”, the necessity of discourse, when it concerns very obscure matters whose details must be partly concealed and partly revealed; and in that case, “le vulgaire ne doit d'aucune manière s'apercevoir de l'endroit où existe la contradiction, et l'auteur quelquefois cherche toute sorte d'expédients pour la dérober”, the vulgar must in no way notice the place where the contradiction lies, and the author sometimes seeks every kind of expedient to conceal it. He adds that the divergences in the present treatise arise from the sixth and seventh causes. This is Averroes's arrangement, carried over from teaching into writing: the book speaks to two audiences at once. Leo Strauss will make these lines the key to a reading of all premodern philosophy (Part Eleven); questionable elsewhere, this reading, where Maimonides is concerned, merely takes the author at his word.

Spinoza then intervenes, and his response is attested, by name, and of rare violence. In chapter VII of the *Theological-Political Treatise*, he examines Maimonides's method and judges it inapplicable and harmful: it assumes that the prophets were philosophers, that the clearest literal sense may be tortured in the name of reason, and that one must defer to the learned. He concludes: “Il faut donc absolument rejeter la méthode de Maimonide comme inutile, dangereuse et absurde”, the method of Maimonides must therefore be utterly rejected as useless, dangerous and absurd (ch. VII, trans. Saisset). And in chapter XV he makes it the origin of an error: the first among the Pharisees to declare that Scripture must be bent to the demands of reason “fut Maimonide”, was Maimonides. The break is complete, and it is the true starting point of the modern critique of the Bible. Maimonides accommodated Scripture to reason in order to save it; Spinoza separates them in order to free them both. Scripture teaches not truth but obedience; it therefore has no need to be true in the philosophical sense, and philosophy has no need of it.

Yet Spinoza owes Maimonides more than he admits. The idea that Scripture speaks according to the capacity of the people and that its images are concessions to their imagination is already in Maimonides; Spinoza takes it up while removing its divine guarantee. Negative theology, which denies God every human predicate, prepares the God of the *Ethics*, who loves no one and has neither understanding nor will in the human sense. The separation of the sage and the vulgar, for which he reproaches Maimonides in interpretation, he keeps in life: beatitude is for the sage, obedience for the multitude. What he rejects is that the sage should have authority over the meaning of the text, not that there should be sages.

Where should Maimonides be placed? He does not leave religion and does not claim to: he is the legislator of his community and the guardian of its Law. But within this fidelity, he practises three of the regimes of critique. He treats the representations of God as **errors** to be corrected; he explains part of worship by its historical **function**; and he leads the knowledge of God to a silence that closely resembles the **experience** of the mystics. What he does not admit is that the people should be deprived of the Law. Like Averroes, he divides; but where Averroes divides human beings, he divides the text, and he writes a book that says everything only to the one who knows how to read. The exit from religion, which in Spinoza will be a matter of hermeneutics before it is a matter of metaphysics, begins with this quarrel among readers.

### Meister Eckhart (c. 1260-1328): praying God to be rid of God

The Christian prehistory begins with a scandal. The Dominican Eckhart of Hochheim, master of theology in Paris, preached in Strasbourg and Cologne, in German, to nuns and to lay people, sermons of an audacity without equal. He died during his trial; the bull *In agro dominico* of John XXII, in 1329, condemned twenty-eight propositions drawn from his works. I do not settle the question of his orthodoxy, which is still debated; I read a sermon that contains, four centuries before the Enlightenment, the most radical critique of the image of God.

The sermon numbered 52 in Josef Quint's critical edition comments on the first beatitude, “Beati pauperes spiritu”, blessed are the poor in spirit. Eckhart gives there a definition of poverty that rejects every pious definition. In Gustav Landauer's modern German translation (1903): “Das ist ein armer Mensch, der nichts will und nichts weiss und nichts hat”, he is a poor man who wills nothing, knows nothing and has nothing. To will nothing is not even to will to accomplish the will of God: Eckhart specifies that as long as a man's will is to accomplish the most dear will of God, he does not have the poverty of which he speaks, for he still has a will. To know nothing is not even to know that God is at work in oneself; to have nothing, not even to be a place where God might work.

From this Eckhart draws a prayer: “Darum bitte ich Gott, dass er mich Gottes quitt mache”, therefore I pray God to rid me of God. The Middle High German reads, in Franz Pfeiffer's edition: “hier umbe so bite ich got, daz er mich quit mache gotes” (*Deutsche Mystiker des vierzehnten Jahrhunderts*, vol. II, Leipzig, 1857, sermon LXXXVII, p. 283, which is sermon 52 in Quint's numbering); I have not checked the text established by Quint (DW II). Eckhart justifies the request by a distinction: the God of whom one must be rid is God as creator, the God who stands over against the creature, the one the creature represents to itself; beyond him there is a being without mode, “über Gott und über Unterschiedenheit”, above God and above all difference, where the soul was before it was created.

History has read this sentence in two ways. The first, orthodox at least in intention, sees in it negative theology pushed to its limit, that of Pseudo-Dionysius, for whom God surpasses all names, including the name of God. To pray to be rid of God is to pray to be rid of the idol one makes of him, in order to reach the nameless Godhead. The second is modern, and it will make Eckhart an ancestor of the spiritualities without God: detachment, *Gelassenheit*, becomes an exercise in dispossession that no longer needs any object. Schopenhauer will hail in Eckhart a brother of the sages of India; Simone Weil will speak of decreation.

Eckhart establishes what modern critics will often forget: the critique of the representation of God, far from being an invention of unbelief, lies at the heart of Christian mysticism. When Feuerbach comes to say that the believers' God is a projection of man, he will be saying what Eckhart says of God the creator, and will draw the opposite conclusion: Eckhart leaves the image of God to enter the Godhead, Feuerbach to return into man. The two gestures have the same form. The confrontation is constructed; it shows at least that the boundary between the critique of religion and spirituality does not run where one thinks.

### Transition: from the Law to reason

At the close of the Middle Ages, the critique of religion does not yet exist as a project, but it already has three of its gestures. The first is political: in Plato, Aristotle and Averroes, religion appears for the first time as a function of the city, which the philosopher can judge useful without holding it literally true. The second is therapeutic: Epicurus and Lucretius deliver people from the fear of the gods without denying the gods. The third is apophatic: Plotinus, Maimonides and Eckhart deny of God everything that is said of him, to the point that Feuerbach will see in negative theology a subtle atheism. They share one trait, which will fade in the classical age: they all presuppose an elite, philosophers, sages or mystics, and a multitude left with its images. We must first go back in time to follow the birth of Christianity, which received the God of the Greek philosophers long before the Moderns reworked him and democratized the question.
