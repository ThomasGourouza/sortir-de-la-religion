### David Friedrich Strauss (1808-1874) and Bruno Bauer (1809-1882): myth and imposture

In 1835 and 1836 the two volumes of *Das Leben Jesu, kritisch bearbeitet* (*The Life of Jesus, Critically Examined*) appeared in Tübingen. Their author, twenty-seven years old, who had heard Hegel in Berlin shortly before his death, would lose his post at the Tübingen seminary because of the book, and a professorship in Zurich would be withdrawn from him in 1839 under popular pressure. Rarely has a work of scholarship caused such a scandal, and rightly so: it raises, with all the resources of philology, the question that runs all the way to Onfray, that of the historical value of the gospel narratives.

Strauss's answer comes down to one notion: myth. Before him, two readings were at odds. The supernaturalist reading took the miracles as facts; the rationalist reading, that of a Paulus, also took them as facts, but natural and misunderstood ones: Jesus did not walk on the water, he walked along the shore, and the disciples saw wrongly. Strauss dismisses both readings alike, because both assume that the Gospels report events. He proposes a third way: the gospel narratives are myths, that is, according to the definition he gives in the introduction, “nichts Andres, als geschichtartige Einkleidungen urchristlicher Ideen, gebildet in der absichtslos dichtenden Sage”, nothing other than history-like clothings of early Christian ideas, formed in legend that composes without intention.

Everything hangs on the adverb *absichtslos*, without intention. Myth is not a lie: it has no author who knows he is inventing. It arises in a community that awaits a Messiah and that, convinced Jesus was he, tells his life according to the features the Scriptures attributed to the Messiah: birth in Bethlehem, descent from David, the healings foretold by Isaiah. The narrative is not fabricated to deceive; it is produced by faith itself, which cannot picture the Messiah otherwise. This notion ruins at a stroke the old Enlightenment hypothesis of priestly imposture, found in the clandestine treatise of the *Three Impostors* and which Voltaire only half shook off. The evangelists are not forgers; they are believers who think in images.

But Strauss does not stop there, and this is what makes him more than a philologist. In the preface he asserts that his critique leaves the heart of faith intact: “Den inneren Kern des christlichen Glaubens weiss der Verfasser von seinen kritischen Untersuchungen völlig unabhängig”, the author knows the inner core of the Christian faith to be entirely independent of his critical inquiries. The supernatural birth, the miracles, the resurrection and the ascension, he adds, “bleiben ewige Wahrheiten, so sehr ihre Wirklichkeit als historischer Fakta angezweifelt werden mag”, remain eternal truths, however much their reality as historical facts may be doubted. One recognizes the Hegelian structure: the narrative is the representation, the eternal truth is the concept.

And what is this concept? The *Schlussabhandlung*, the concluding dissertation, states it in pages that are, in my view, the true starting point of Feuerbach. Strauss asks whether the idea of the unity of divine and human nature must have been realized once, in one individual, and never again. He answers that this is not how the idea realizes itself: it does not like to pour all its fullness into a single specimen; it unfolds in a multiplicity of individuals. Then comes the thesis: “Das ist der Schlüssel der ganzen Christologie, dass als Subjekt der Prädikate, welche die Kirche Christo beilegt, statt eines Individuums eine Idee, aber eine reale, nicht Kantisch unwirkliche, gesetzt wird”, this is the key to the whole of Christology: as the subject of the predicates the Church assigns to Christ, one posits, instead of an individual, an idea, but a real one, not unreal in the Kantian manner. And the idea is named: “Die Menschheit ist die Vereinigung der beiden Naturen, der menschgewordene Gott”, humanity is the union of the two natures, God become man. Strauss goes on: humanity is the worker of miracles, since spirit subdues nature ever more; it is the sinless one, it is the one that dies, rises again and ascends to heaven.

We can see what has happened. Strauss has performed the operation Feuerbach will theorize six years later: he has transferred the predicates of Christ to the human species, to the *Gattung*. The very word *Gattung* appears in his text: the properties the Church attributes to Christ contradict one another in an individual; they agree “in der Idee der Gattung”, in the idea of the species. Feuerbach's divinized Man is already there, in 1836, from the pen of a Hegelian who believes he is saving Christianity. The most radical historical criticism of its time thus ends, not in the abolition of the divine, but in its transfer onto humanity. The English reception confirms it in its own way: Strauss's first English translator (1846) would also be Feuerbach's translator in 1854, Marian Evans, who was to become George Eliot. The title of her translation of Feuerbach presents her precisely as “Translator of "Strauss's Life of Jesus"”.

Bruno Bauer goes further, and in another direction. First a theologian of the Hegelian right, he moved to the extreme left at the end of the 1830s, lost his *venia legendi* in Bonn in 1842, and argued in his *Kritik der evangelischen Geschichte der Synoptiker* (1841-1842) that the Gospels are not the product of a collective legend, as Strauss held, but the conscious literary work of one author, the evangelist Mark, on whom the others depend. Against Strauss's *absichtslos*, then, there is an author, an intention, a creation; hence, in the late Bauer, doubts about the historical existence of Jesus himself. Engels, who had known Bauer in his Berlin days, paid him a qualified tribute in 1882 in “Bruno Bauer und das Urchristentum”. He judges the dependence of the Gospels on one another irrefutably demonstrated, and concedes that, since the content of the Gospels proves almost entirely unverifiable historically, “so daß man selbst die geschichtliche Existenz eines Jesus Christus für fraglich erklären kann”, one may even declare the historical existence of a Jesus Christ doubtful. But Engels rejects the Enlightenment conclusion that Bauer comes close to: one is not done with a religion that subjugated the Roman Empire “indem man sie einfach für von Betrügern zusammengestoppelten Unsinn erklärt”, by simply declaring it nonsense patched together by impostors. It must be explained by its historical conditions.

Strauss and Bauer thus trace, as early as the 1840s, the two paths between which the whole historical criticism of Christianity will oscillate, down to Onfray. For Strauss, the narratives are myths born of a community's faith, and their kernel of truth is an idea; for Bauer, they are a literary creation behind which there may be no one at all. The first path leads to a religion of humanity, the second to a thesis of non-existence, and neither leaves religion: the first transfers it, the second reduces it to a fiction whose power remains to be explained, which Bauer does not do and for which Engels reproaches him. As we saw in Part Two, Michel Onfray's *Théorie de Jésus* takes up, without always knowing it, Bauer's path while lending it Strauss's vocabulary: Jesus “as a concept”.

Strauss's story has an epilogue, which prompts an attested response from Nietzsche. Nearly forty years after *Das Leben Jesu*, Strauss published *Der alte und der neue Glaube* (*The Old Faith and the New*, 1872). In it he asks four questions: are we still Christians? do we still have a religion? how do we conceive the world? how do we order our life? To the first he answers no. To the second he answers “ja oder nein, je nachdem man es verstehen will”, yes or no, depending on how one wishes to understand it: there remains a piety towards the universe, “dieselbe Pietät, wie der Fromme alten Stils für seinen Gott”, the same piety as the old-style believer feels for his God, a feeling of dependence on the All, which the Darwinian science expounded in the next chapter does not abolish but orients. The most feared critic of Christianity ends by proposing a religion of the universe, bourgeois and comfortable, in which Beethoven and Haydn take the place of liturgy. In 1873 the young Nietzsche devoted to him the first of his *Unzeitgemäße Betrachtungen*, *David Strauss, der Bekenner und der Schriftsteller* (*David Strauss, the Confessor and the Writer*), in which he makes him the type of the “Bildungsphilister”, the cultivated philistine. Nietzsche does not reproach Strauss for being too critical, but for not being critical enough: he has done away with the Christian God and kept Christian morality intact, without seeing that the one does not stand without the other, the first statement of the objection that *The Gay Science* will make famous. Strauss thus travels in a single lifetime the movement I describe over a century: from the critique of dogma to a substitute religion.

### Ernest Renan (1823-1892): the god become an incomparable man

The *Vie de Jésus* (*The Life of Jesus*) appeared in Paris in 1863. Its author had left the seminary of Saint-Sulpice in 1845; he was a philologist of the Semitic languages, and he had just been suspended from his chair at the Collège de France for having, in his inaugural lecture of 1862, called Jesus “un homme incomparable”, an incomparable man. A great publishing success, the book sold tens of thousands of copies within a few months, was translated throughout Europe and provoked hundreds of refutations.

Renan does three things at once, and they must be told apart to understand why Nietzsche would hate him and Dostoevsky would fight him.

The first is a principle of method, laid down in the introduction with a clarity the Germans had lacked. Renan imagines a wonder-worker presenting himself, and a commission of scholars checking his miracle under controlled, repeatable conditions. Never, he observes, has a miracle occurred under such conditions; the wonder-worker has always chosen his subject, his setting, his audience; and most often it is the people themselves, “par suite de l'invincible besoin qu'il a de voir dans les grands événements et les grands hommes quelque chose de divin”, owing to their invincible need to see something divine in great events and great men, who create the legends after the fact. Hence the principle: “un récit surnaturel ne peut être admis comme tel, qu'il implique toujours crédulité ou imposture, que le devoir de l'historien est de l'interpréter et de rechercher quelle part de vérité, quelle part d'erreur il peut receler”, a supernatural narrative cannot be accepted as such, it always implies credulity or imposture, and the historian's duty is to interpret it and to seek out what share of truth and what share of error it may conceal. Renan, like Strauss, looks for the truth behind the narrative, but his method is positivist, not Hegelian: the supernatural is excluded on principle, not by dialectic. And the word “imposture” reappears, alongside “credulity”.

The second is an experience, which Renan recounts with an emotion that did much for the book's success. In 1860-1861 he travelled in Galilee on an archaeological mission. The agreement of texts and places, the harmony of the gospel ideal with the landscape, were for him “comme une révélation”, like a revelation. He goes on: “J'eus devant les yeux un cinquième évangile, lacéré, mais lisible encore, et désormais, à travers les récits de Matthieu et de Marc, au lieu d'un être abstrait, qu'on dirait n'avoir jamais existé, je vis une admirable figure humaine”, I had before my eyes a fifth gospel, torn but still legible, and from then on, through the narratives of Matthew and Mark, instead of an abstract being one would say had never existed, I saw an admirable human figure. The sentence is aimed at Bauer's thesis: against the abstract being one would say had never existed, Renan sets the human figure recovered in Galilee, and he is thus the anticipated adversary of all the mythicists.

The third is a cult. The *Vie de Jésus* is not a cold book: it ends in a genuine doxology. Having said that we moderns labour without reaping, Renan concludes: “Mais quels que puissent être les phénomènes inattendus de l'avenir, Jésus ne sera pas surpassé. Son culte se rajeunira sans cesse; sa légende provoquera des larmes sans fin; ses souffrances attendriront les meilleurs coeurs; tous les siècles proclameront qu'entre les fils des hommes, il n'en est pas né de plus grand que Jésus”, but whatever the unexpected phenomena of the future may be, Jesus will not be surpassed. His cult will renew its youth unceasingly; his legend will draw tears without end; his sufferings will move the best hearts; every century will proclaim that among the sons of men there has been born none greater than Jesus. Christ ceases to be God; he becomes the greatest of men; and his cult is declared eternal. Renan is a pure example of an exit from religion that preserves it in the form of admiration. It is Christianity minus the supernatural, that is, a religion of the great man.

Two attested responses measure what is at stake. The first is Nietzsche's, who read Renan with growing hostility. In *Der Antichrist* (written in 1888, published in 1895), he writes: “Herr Renan, dieser Hanswurst in psychologicis, hat die zwei ungehörigsten Begriffe zu seiner Erklärung des Typus Jesus hinzugebracht, die es hierfür geben kann: den Begriff Genie und den Begriff Held”, Monsieur Renan, that buffoon *in psychologicis*, has brought to his explanation of the type of Jesus the two most unfitting concepts there could be: the concept of genius and the concept of hero. And in *Götzen-Dämmerung* (completed in 1888, published in January 1889), he goes further: what is the use of all the free thought, modernity and mockery, “wenn man mit seinen Eingeweiden Christ, Katholik und sogar Priester geblieben ist!”, when one has remained, in one's entrails, a Christian, a Catholic and even a priest! Renan believes he has left the Church; he has merely changed altars.

The second response is Dostoevsky's. In *A Writer's Diary* of 1873, he recalls Renan, who, unlike Belinsky, stopped before the figure of Christ, proclaiming in his book “полной безверия”, full of unbelief, the *Vie de Jésus*, that Christ nonetheless remains “идеал красоты человеческой”, the ideal of human beauty. The connection with Renan is attested, and we know that the figure of a too human Christ, reduced to morality and admiration, is one of Dostoevsky's constant targets.

Renan opens a possibility that what follows will take up again and again: that of a spirituality without the supernatural, centred on an exemplary figure. Jesus becomes a master of life, like Socrates or the Buddha. The contemporary spiritualities that make Christ a sage, of which Frédéric Lenoir gave a popular version at the beginning of the twenty-first century under the title *Le Christ philosophe* (2007), are the direct heirs of Renan. Nietzsche's objection reaches them all: to admire Jesus as a genius or a hero is to misunderstand him twice, as God and as man. Between Renan and Nietzsche lies the whole difference between an exit that preserves religion as a cult of the great man and a genealogy that refuses every cult.

### Transition: from a quarrel of schools to the break

Schleiermacher shifted religion towards experience, to the point of admitting a religion without God; Hegel towards the concept, to the point of making philosophy its truth. Strauss transferred the predicates of Christ to humanity; Renan made Christ the greatest of men and declared his cult eternal. None of them wants to destroy religion; all want to save it by transforming it, and all transform it into something that keeps the place of the absolute: the universe, Spirit, Humanity, the great man.

Against this caution there rises, from 1841 onwards, the generation of the Young Hegelians, who take Hegel's *Aufhebung* and Strauss's transfer at their word and draw the consequence their masters did not dare to draw: if God is man, then one must say so, and stop worshipping him under another name. But in saying so, they merely displace worship once more.
