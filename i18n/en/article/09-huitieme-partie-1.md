## Part Eight: the sciences of religion and spiritual experience

Between 1900 and 1932, in barely a generation, religion changes its intellectual status. Until then, philosophers judged it: true or false, useful or harmful, alienating or consoling. From now on, scholars describe it. The sociologist asks what function it fulfils in the group, the psychologist from what experience it proceeds, the historian of religions what it has in common from one culture to another. This shift, neutral in appearance, is not neutral. For in ceasing to ask whether religion is true, the science of religion often discovers that it is *necessary*, and it rediscovers, under new names, the question of this essay.

Two families take shape, and they answer each other. The first, sociological, is that of Durkheim and Weber: religion is a collective fact, bound up with the cohesion of groups or with the rationalization of conduct, and its apparent disappearance raises the question of what replaces it. The second, psychological and phenomenological, is that of James, Otto, Freud and Bergson: religion is first of all an individual experience, a feeling, an emotion, and the question becomes that of its value. It is in this second family that the notion of spirituality is born, in the modern sense: a religious experience distinguished from the institution, from dogma and sometimes from God himself. The two families cross constantly. Durkheim cites James; Otto discusses James and Schleiermacher; Freud replies to Romain Rolland, a reader of the Indian mystics; Bergson discusses Durkheim and cites James. Never has the dialogue between authors been so dense or so well attested.

### Durkheim (1858-1917): one does not leave the sacred

*The Elementary Forms of Religious Life* appeared in 1912. The book studies Australian totemism, but its true object is religion in general, grasped in what Durkheim takes to be its simplest form. Its thesis is stated from the introduction onward: “la religion est une chose éminemment sociale”, religion is an eminently social thing. Religious representations are collective representations that express collective realities; rites are ways of acting that arise within assembled groups.

The definition of religion, given in the first chapter of Book I and quoted in the prologue, does not contain the word God, and that is its first bold stroke. The centre is no longer the divine, it is the sacred, and the sacred is defined by a separation: “Les choses sacrées sont celles que les interdits protègent et isolent ; les choses profanes, celles auxquelles ces interdits s'appliquent et qui doivent rester à distance des premières”, sacred things are those which prohibitions protect and isolate; profane things, those to which these prohibitions apply and which must remain at a distance from the former. Buddhism, a religion without a personal god, fits into this definition without difficulty; and, as we shall see, many things that do not call themselves religious fit into it too.

Where does the sacred come from? From society itself, which in moments of gathering experiences itself as a force superior to individuals, and which projects this force onto an emblem: the totem, then the god. Book II says so bluntly: rites, by tightening the bonds that attach the faithful to their god, really tighten those that unite the individual to society, “puisque le dieu n'est que l'expression figurée de la société”, since the god is only the figurative expression of society. The god of the clan, Durkheim adds, can be nothing other than the clan itself, but hypostatized.

One might think one was reading Feuerbach transposed from the species to the group. God was, for Feuerbach, only the projected human essence; he is, for Durkheim, only society represented. The comparison is constructed (Durkheim never cites Feuerbach), and the conclusions are opposed. Feuerbach wanted to repatriate the projected essence and dissolve religion into anthropology; Durkheim concludes that if religion expresses society, it is not an illusion, it is true in its own way. “Il n'y a donc pas, au fond, de religions qui soient fausses. Toutes sont vraies à leur façon : toutes répondent, quoique de manières différentes, à des conditions données de l'existence humaine”, there are thus, at bottom, no religions that are false. All are true in their own fashion: all answer, though in different ways, to given conditions of human existence. The believer is mistaken about the object of his worship; he is not mistaken about the force he feels. This is what makes Durkheim a formidable adversary of every critique of religion as error. Spinoza, Feuerbach, Onfray treat religion as a false representation; Durkheim answers them that religion is not first of all a representation.

The conclusion of the book develops this point, drawing on the testimony of believers themselves and citing in a note William James's *The Varieties of Religious Experience*. Believers, Durkheim writes, feel that the true function of religion “n'est pas de nous faire penser, d'enrichir notre connaissance[…] mais de nous faire agir, de nous aider à vivre”, is not to make us think, to enrich our knowledge […] but to make us act, to help us live. And: “Le fidèle qui a communié avec son dieu n'est pas seulement un homme qui voit des vérités nouvelles que l'incroyant ignore ; c'est un homme qui peut davantage”, the believer who has communed with his god is not merely a man who sees new truths of which the unbeliever is ignorant; he is a man who is capable of more. What is at stake is clear. If religion were a form of knowledge, science could replace it, and Durkheim indeed grants that, as regards knowledge, “la pensée scientifique n'est qu'une forme plus parfaite de la pensée religieuse”, scientific thought is only a more perfect form of religious thought. But if religion is a force for action, science does not replace it, for science always remains at a distance from action: faith, Durkheim writes, is “avant tout un élan à agir”, above all an impetus to act, and science, however far one pushes it, is never enough for that.

The culmination of the demonstration is a historical fact that Durkheim invokes twice: the French Revolution. In Book II he writes that society's aptitude for setting itself up as a god or for creating gods “ne fut nulle part plus visible que pendant les premières années de la Révolution”, was nowhere more visible than during the first years of the Revolution, when things purely secular by nature, “la Patrie, la Liberté, la Raison”, the Fatherland, Liberty, Reason, were transformed into sacred things; and he goes on: “Une religion tendit d'elle-même à s'établir qui avait son dogme, ses symboles, ses autels et ses fêtes”, a religion tended of itself to become established, which had its dogma, its symbols, its altars and its feasts. This is, given by a sociologist in 1912, a demonstration of the thesis of this essay: the most radical exit from the Christian religion that France has known produced, almost immediately, a new religion. The “sacred games” that Nietzsche's madman announced, the Revolution had already invented.

The general conclusion of the book draws from this a prophecy that answers Comte point by point, in attested fashion, and which must be quoted almost in full. Durkheim begins with an affirmation of principle: “Il y a donc dans la religion quelque chose d'éternel qui est destiné à survivre à tous les symboles particuliers dans lesquels la pensée religieuse s'est successivement enveloppée”, there is thus in religion something eternal that is destined to outlive all the particular symbols in which religious thought has successively wrapped itself. No society can do without reaffirming its collective sentiments in common, and he asks what essential difference separates an assembly of Christians celebrating the life of Christ from a gathering of citizens commemorating the institution of a new moral charter. Then comes the diagnosis of the present: “les anciens dieux vieillissent ou meurent, et d'autres ne sont pas nés. C'est ce qui a rendu vaine la tentative de Comte en vue d'organiser une religion avec de vieux souvenirs historiques, artificiellement réveillés : c'est de la vie elle-même, et non d'un passé mort que peut sortir un culte vivant”, the old gods are growing old or dying, and others are not yet born. This is what made vain Comte's attempt to organize a religion out of old historical memories, artificially revived: it is from life itself, and not from a dead past, that a living cult can emerge. And the prophecy: “Un jour viendra où nos sociétés connaîtront à nouveau des heures d'effervescence créatrice au cours desquelles de nouveaux idéaux surgiront”, a day will come when our societies will know once again hours of creative effervescence in the course of which new ideals will arise, and the conclusion: “Il n'y a pas d'évangiles qui soient immortels et il n'y a pas de raison de croire que l'humanité soit désormais incapable d'en concevoir de nouveaux”, there are no gospels that are immortal, and there is no reason to believe that humanity is henceforth incapable of conceiving new ones.

Durkheim proves Comte right on substance and wrong on method. Comte was right to think that no society lives without a cult; he was wrong to believe that a cult can be manufactured by decree, with a calendar of great men. Religions are not invented, they arise from collective effervescence. The consequence admits of no appeal: one does not leave the sacred, one changes it. The exit from the Christian religion is not an exit from religion, it is an interregnum, a “phase de transition et de médiocrité morale”, a phase of transition and moral mediocrity, while we wait for the gods that are not yet born.

I take Durkheim's position to be one of the two strongest objections to the very idea of an exit from religion, and a disturbing objection. It is strong because it is founded on an analysis of the social function of the sacred, and not on a preference; it is disturbing because the “hours of creative effervescence” that Durkheim longed for in 1912 did come, and they took the form of the political religions of the twentieth century, with their mass festivals, their dogmas, their symbols and their altars. Durkheim obviously did not wish for the totalitarianisms, but his theory gave no means of distinguishing a fruitful effervescence from a murderous one. This is the point at which Bergson, twenty years later, will attack him.

> **En littérature.** Émile Zola, *Lourdes* (1894) ; Louis-Ferdinand Céline, *Journey to the End of the Night* (1932)
>
> *Lourdes* follows a white train of the sick and the priest Pierre Froment, who has lost his faith and accompanies a paralysed childhood friend. Zola, who had made the journey in 1892 with his notebooks, describes the mechanics of the miracle as a contagion: the stories of cures passed around the carriage, the *Magnificat*; “Les habitants du village vont en foule attendre la miraculée sur la route, pendant que les cloches sonnent à la volée ; et, quand on la voit sauter lestement de la voiture, des cris, des sanglots de joie éclatent, on entonne le *Magnificat*”, the villagers go in a crowd to wait on the road for the woman miraculously healed, while the bells ring out in full peal; and when she is seen jumping nimbly down from the carriage, cries and sobs of joy burst out, and the *Magnificat* is struck up; and Pierre himself, in the heat of the carriage, feels that “la contagion agissait, il ne savait plus bien où s'arrêtaient le réel et le possible”, the contagion was at work, he no longer quite knew where the real ended and the possible began.
>
> Why here: this is the collective effervescence of *The Elementary Forms* described eighteen years before the book, by a novelist who observes it as an unbeliever and who undergoes its effect. The miracle of Lourdes is not, for Zola, an individual error; it is a social force, “la contagion du mystère”, the contagion of mystery, which produces the sacred through gathering, and from which even the faithless priest is not protected. Durkheim would say that Zola saw the god being born from the crowd, and that he was wrong to believe that one is cured of it by knowing it.
>
> Céline shows the same effervescence from the side where Durkheim did not look. Bardamu, at the beginning of *Journey to the End of the Night*, enlists on a whim behind a passing regiment, and discovers within a few weeks that the Fatherland is a sacred that demands the death of millions of men without anyone asking anything of it in return; he decides to be a coward, alone against the crowd, and the novel is the story of this desertion from all absolutes: war, the colony, the factory, medicine for the poor. The essay says that the French Revolution sacralizing the Fatherland is a demonstration of the thesis; Céline gives its counterproof: a man who refuses the effervescence of 1914, and who is left, precisely, with no sacred at all.
>
> Sources: Émile Zola, *Lourdes*, first day, IV and V (1894); Louis-Ferdinand Céline, *Journey to the End of the Night* (1932).

### Weber (1864-1920): disenchantment and the return of the gods

Max Weber approaches religion from the other end. What interests him is not the cohesion of the group but the rationalization of conduct, and to express it he coined the most famous phrase in the sociology of religion, “Entzauberung der Welt”, the disenchantment of the world. The word appears in *Die protestantische Ethik und der Geist des Kapitalismus* (*The Protestant Ethic and the Spirit of Capitalism*), published in 1904-1905; the phrase only enters it in the revision of 1920. In the revised version, Weber speaks of “Jener große religionsgeschichtliche Prozeß der Entzauberung der Welt”, that great process in the history of religion which is the disenchantment of the world, which begins with ancient Jewish prophecy; and he adds that “Die radikale Entzauberung der Welt ließ einen anderen Weg als die innerweltliche Askese innerlich nicht zu”, the radical disenchantment of the world inwardly allowed no other path than asceticism within the world.

“Disenchantment” is not first of all the loss of faith; it is the elimination of magic as a means of salvation. The enchanted world is one in which mysterious forces can be manipulated by rites, sacraments, formulas. Disenchantment is the work of the religions themselves: Hebrew prophecy, which condemns magic, then Calvinist Puritanism, which abolishes the sacraments as means of salvation and leaves the believer alone before a hidden God. Disenchantment is thus, as in Nietzsche, a self-suppression of the religious by the religious. The convergence with § 27 of the *Genealogy* is not fortuitous: Weber had read Nietzsche, and he alludes to him, as we shall see.

The result of this process is described in a dark page of *The Protestant Ethic*. The Puritan ascetic wanted to work in a calling; we are compelled to do so. Concern for external goods was to lie on the shoulders of the saint like a light cloak, “Aber aus dem Mantel ließ das Verhängnis ein stahlhartes Gehäuse werden”, but fate turned the cloak into a casing as hard as steel. And Weber contemplates, for the “last men” of this cultural development, the truth of a terrible saying: “Fachmenschen ohne Geist, Genußmenschen ohne Herz: dies Nichts bildet sich ein, eine nie vorher erreichte Stufe des Menschentums erstiegen zu haben”, specialists without spirit, sensualists without heart: this nullity imagines it has climbed to a level of humanity never before attained. The “letzten Menschen”, in quotation marks in the text, are those of the prologue of *Zarathustra*; the allusion to Nietzsche is transparent, but Weber does not name him.

But it is in the lecture *Wissenschaft als Beruf* (*Science as a Vocation*), delivered in Munich in 1917 and published in 1919, that Weber poses the question of our essay without circumlocution. He first defines disenchantment there: not that we know more than the savage about our conditions of life, but that we believe there are “prinzipiell keine geheimnisvollen unberechenbaren Mächte”, in principle no mysterious incalculable powers, and that everything can be mastered by calculation; “Das aber bedeutet: die Entzauberung der Welt”, but this means: the disenchantment of the world. Then he asks whether this process has a meaning, and it is Tolstoy he summons, reporting his answer: science is meaningless because it gives no answer to the only question important for us, “Was sollen wir tun? Wie sollen wir leben?”, what shall we do? how shall we live?

Weber's answer is original: disenchantment has not abolished the gods. It has multiplied them. “Die alten vielen Götter, entzaubert und daher in Gestalt unpersönlicher Mächte, entsteigen ihren Gräbern, streben nach Gewalt über unser Leben und beginnen untereinander wieder ihren ewigen Kampf”, the many old gods, disenchanted and hence in the form of impersonal powers, rise from their graves, strive for power over our lives and begin again their eternal struggle with one another. The true, the beautiful, the good, the sacred, the nation, the class: so many ultimate values that can no longer be ranked by a higher authority. Life, Weber writes, knows only “den ewigen Kampf jener Götter miteinander”, the eternal struggle of those gods with one another, that is to say the impossibility of deciding between the ultimate standpoints on life, and hence the necessity of choosing.

The gap with Durkheim is clear. Durkheim awaited new gods, born of a collective effervescence that would remake the unity of society. Weber observes that the gods have already returned, but in the plural, and that they will remake no unity. The exit from monotheism is not an exit from religion, it is a return to polytheism, a polytheism of values without temples or sacrifices, but not without victims. The thesis of our essay takes a new form here: one does not refound *a* religion, one refounds several, which wage war on one another.

Weber offers no consolation. He distinguishes the man who remains in science, accepting that he will receive from it no answer to the question of meaning, from the one who returns to faith. The latter must accomplish what Weber calls, in quotation marks, the “„Opfer des Jntellekts"”, the sacrifice of the intellect; and he adds that the capacity to accomplish this virtuoso performance is “das entscheidende Merkmal des positiv religiösen Menschen”, the decisive mark of the positively religious man. Weber does not despise this sacrifice: he judges it morally superior to the false reconciliation of those who dare not choose, and he concludes: “Wer dies Schicksal der Zeit nicht männlich ertragen kann, dem muß man sagen: Er kehre lieber, schweigend, ohne die übliche öffentliche Renegatenreklame, sondern schlicht und einfach, in die weit und erbarmend geöffneten Arme der alten Kirchen zurück”, to whoever cannot bear this fate of the age like a man, one must say: let him rather return, in silence, without the usual public advertisement of the renegade, but plainly and simply, into the arms of the old Churches, opened wide and mercifully.

What he refuses, on the other hand, is the third way, that of the prophets of the lectern and the manufacturers of religion. To those who await new prophets and new saviours, he recalls the song of the watchman of Edom, in the book of Isaiah: “Wächter, wie lang noch die Nacht? Der Wächter spricht: Es kommt der Morgen, aber noch ist es Nacht”, watchman, how much longer the night? The watchman says: the morning comes, but it is still night. Weber refuses both the return to the Church for himself and the manufacture of a new Church. He saw, like Nietzsche, that the exit from religion calls forth replacement religions, and he refused to give in to them, at the price of a solitude he does not hide.

Weber's position is honest, and hard to live. It escapes the reproach that Stirner addressed to the pious atheists, since it refuses any hierarchy of values grounded in the last instance; but it pays for this refusal with a kind of tragic heroism, that “like a man” which today sounds like a pose. One may wonder whether the ethic of responsibility, with its austere grandeur, is not itself an inner-worldly asceticism: the last form of the Puritanism Weber had described, applied this time to the absence of God. Weber would then be the last Puritan, as Nietzsche saw in Schopenhauer the last Christian.

### William James (1842-1910): religion as experience

With William James, the ground shifts. *The Varieties of Religious Experience*, published in 1902, brings together the Gifford Lectures delivered in Edinburgh in 1901-1902. The book is the founding charter of what would later be called spirituality.

From the second lecture onward, James distances himself from religion as an institution: his definition, quoted in the prologue and presented as arbitrary, relates religion to the individual in his solitude, where Durkheim related it to the Church. For Durkheim, the religious individual is a product of the group; for James, the religious group is a fallout, a second-hand routine, of the original experiences of a few individuals. Durkheim cites James in his conclusion, but in order to turn the Jamesian definition against itself: the strength that faith gives comes from the collective. The dialogue is attested, and it bears on the point that concerns us: can religion exist without a community? If James is right, a spirituality without a Church is possible; if Durkheim is right, it is only a weakened reflection of a collective cult.

James's method, a pragmatist one, takes shape against an adversary he calls “medical materialism”. He gives a cruel description of it: medical materialism “finishes up Saint Paul by calling his vision on the road to Damascus a discharging lesion of the occipital cortex, he being an epileptic”. James does not deny that religious states have physiological correlates; he denies that the origin of a state decides its value. He borrows his criterion from the Gospel, and from Jonathan Edwards: “By their fruits ye shall know them, not by their roots.”

This answer holds, by anticipation, against all the genealogical critiques of our series. Feuerbach explained religion by projection, Marx by misery, Nietzsche by ressentiment, Freud will do so by infantile desire: to all of them James replies in advance that origin does not settle value. For Freud, who writes twenty-five years later, the confrontation is constructed: to say that belief in God arises from the need for paternal protection says nothing about the truth or the fruitfulness of that belief. Freud knows this, moreover, since he will carefully distinguish illusion from error.

The most famous lectures of the book contrast two religious types, healthy souls and sick souls. James takes from Francis Newman the distinction between “the once‐born and the twice‐born”. The former live in a spontaneous optimism, without awareness of evil; the latter have passed through melancholy, the sense of sin or of the absurd, and have found peace only at the price of a second birth. James invites his audience to turn their backs on the “once‐born and their sky‐blue optimistic gospel” in order to listen to the “sick souls”. This typology illuminates our whole series: Ivan Karamazov is the sick soul par excellence, who refuses the sky-blue gospel of final harmony; Comte and Renan, with their religion of Humanity and of genius, are “once-born”. The question of spirituality then arises differently: it is not a matter of knowing whether one believes, but whether one has passed through evil.

At the summit of religious experience, James places mysticism, to which he devotes the sixteenth and seventeenth lectures. He draws out four marks. The first is ineffability: the subject says at once that his state “defies expression”, that its quality must be directly experienced and cannot be imparted. The second is the noetic quality: although similar to feelings, mystical states seem to those who live them to be also states of knowledge, “states of insight into depths of truth unplumbed by the discursive intellect”. The third is transiency, the fourth passivity. This description, empirical and non-confessional, makes it possible to recognize a kinship between Eckhart, Teresa of Ávila, the Sufi mystics, the Vedantic texts and certain testimonies of natural or induced experience. It grounds the possibility of a comparative spirituality, detached from any particular Church.

The conclusion is at once cautious and bold. James distinguishes what is common to all religious experiences from what he calls the “over‐beliefs” proper to each tradition. And what is common fits into a formula: “the fact that the conscious person is continuous with a wider self through which saving experiences come”; this content, he adds, seems to him “literally and objectively true as far as it goes”. And he ends by translating into schematic language what he calls the instinctive belief of mankind: “God is real since he produces real effects.”

James's position is generous, and fragile. It is generous because it takes religious experience seriously without demanding adherence to a dogma; it makes it possible to think a spirituality that is neither a Church nor an illusion. It is fragile because the criterion of fruits says nothing about content: the fanatic too produces real effects. And it raises a question that James leaves open: is the “wider self” God, the unconscious, nature, society? Durkheim answered society, Freud will answer the unconscious, Bergson will answer the élan vital. James leaves the question to the over-beliefs, and that, at bottom, is the very definition of modern spirituality: an experience that is certain, whose object remains undetermined.

> **En littérature.** Walt Whitman, *Leaves of Grass* (1855) ; Leo Tolstoy, *Confession* (1882)
>
> James ranks Whitman among the once-born souls, those whose religion is a form of health; “Song of Myself” is its profession of faith: “Why should I wish to see God better than this day? / I see something of God each hour of the twenty-four, and each moment then, / In the faces of men and women I see God, and in my own face in the glass, / I find letters from God dropt in the street, and every one is sign'd by God's name, / And I leave them where they are.”
>
> Tolstoy is, for James, the exemplary sick soul and “twice-born”, and he quotes him at length: “I felt, says Tolstoy, that something had broken within me on which my life had always rested, that I had nothing left to hold on to, and that morally my life had stopped.[…] Behold me then, a man happy and in good health, hiding the rope in order not to hang myself to the rafters of the room where every night I went to sleep alone.”
>
> Why here: the two texts are the very material of the *Varieties*, and they give the reader what the essay identifies as James's strength and fragility. Whitman finds God in faces without asking anything of anyone; Tolstoy finds him again in the faith of the peasants after having wanted to die; the criterion of fruits absolves them both, and says nothing about what they saw. “By their fruits ye shall know them, not by their roots”: the letter in the street and the hidden rope are fruits; of the root, pragmatism knows nothing, and says so.
>
> Sources: Walt Whitman, “Song of Myself”, 48, *Leaves of Grass* (1855); Leo Tolstoy, *Исповедь* (*Confession*, 1882), quoted in the translation given by William James, *The Varieties of Religious Experience*, lectures VI-VII (1902).
