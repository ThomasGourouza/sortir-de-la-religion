## Part Seven: the ordeal and the genealogy

By the middle of the nineteenth century, the critique of religion seemed to have reached its two limits. On one side Schopenhauer, the first declared German atheist, kept from religion what was deepest in it, the denial of the will, asceticism, compassion, and made of it a mysticism without God. On the other Comte, who had dismissed theology in the name of positive science, ended by organizing a cult, a calendar and a priesthood of Humanity. Between the mysticism without God and the Church without God, the question of this essay had already received its two extreme answers: one can leave religion while keeping its inner life, or while keeping its institutions. It remained to be seen whether either of them held.

The two authors who close the century do not answer with a new doctrine. They put doctrines to the test. Dostoevsky makes them live in persons and watches what they become when they are inhabited to the end. Nietzsche writes their genealogy and asks where the will to think them comes from. Both arrive at the same observation: the death of God does not leave a neutral world, but a world in which something must occupy the empty place. It is in this seventh part that the question “can one leave religion without founding another?” finds its sharpest formulation, posed by men who knew better than their predecessors what the answer costs.

### Dostoevsky (1821-1881): the ordeal of persons

The *Brat'ja Karamazovy* (*The Brothers Karamazov*) appeared in instalments in the *Russkij vestnik* in 1879-1880, a few months before Dostoevsky's death. The novel is the place where the preceding regimes cease to be theses and become persons, and answer one another without any voice possessing the last word. This is what Bakhtin called polyphony, and the notion must be taken seriously. One will not find in the novel the refutation of atheism that Pobedonostsev demanded of it, and Dostoevsky knew this: he announced Book VI, “Русский инок”, to him as the answer “на всю эту отрицательную сторону”, to this whole negative side, but an answer that is “не прямой”, not direct, “а лишь косвенный”, only indirect, given not point by point but “в художественной картине”, in an artistic picture. One will find in it something better than a refutation.

We must first establish what Dostoevsky actually read of the European critique, for one is tempted to credit him with a direct dialogue with Feuerbach or Strauss that the texts do not warrant. In the *Dnevnik pisatelja* (*A Writer's Diary*) of 1873, in the chapter “Old People”, Feuerbach and Strauss appear only as the idols of Belinsky, the critic who had initiated the young Dostoevsky into the atheist socialism of the 1840s; Dostoevsky reports that Strauss was spoken of with veneration in that circle. The reception is therefore mediated: the Hegelian left reaches Dostoevsky through Belinsky's mouth, as an atmosphere rather than as a book. Renan, on the other hand, is named directly, in the same *Diary*, as the author of the *Life of Jesus*, a book “full of unbelief”. Making Ivan answer Feuerbach will therefore be a constructed confrontation; making him answer Voltaire, an attested response, since Ivan quotes him.

Ivan Karamazov is first of all a Feuerbachian: he fights God not in the name of egoism, but in the name of suffering humanity. In Book V, chapter 4 (“Бунт”, Rebellion), he piles up before his brother Alyosha newspaper stories of cruelties inflicted on children, and he refuses the final harmony that would justify them: “Не хочу гармонии, из-за любви к человечеству не хочу”, I do not want harmony, out of love for humanity I do not want it. Henri Mongault translates: “C'est par amour pour l'humanité que je ne veux pas de cette harmonie”, it is out of love for humanity that I do not want this harmony. And the conclusion: “Не бога я не принимаю, Алеша, я только билет ему почтительнейше возвращаю”, it is not God that I refuse, Alyosha, I am only most respectfully returning him my ticket; Mongault: “Je ne refuse pas d'admettre Dieu, mais très respectueusement je lui rends mon billet”, I do not refuse to accept God, but I most respectfully return him my ticket.

The novel gives this refusal its genealogy. In the preceding chapter (V, 3), before telling of the martyred children, Ivan quotes to Alyosha a saying the reader will recognize: “был один старый грешник в восемнадцатом столетии, который изрек, что если бы не было бога, то следовало бы его выдумать, s'il n'existait pas Dieu il faudrait l'inventer”, there was an old sinner in the eighteenth century who declared that, if there were no God, he would have to be invented; Mongault: “il y avait un vieux pécheur, au XVIIIe siècle, qui a dit : Si Dieu n'existait pas, il faudrait l'inventer”, there was an old sinner, in the eighteenth century, who said: if God did not exist, it would be necessary to invent him. The old sinner is Voltaire, and the line comes from the *Épître à l'auteur du livre des Trois imposteurs* (1769): “Si Dieu n'existait pas, il faudrait l'inventer”, if God did not exist, it would be necessary to invent him. Ivan at once draws the Feuerbachian conclusion: “И действительно, человек выдумал бога”, and indeed, it is man who invented God. But he adds, and this is his whole singularity, that the astonishing thing is not that God exists, but that the idea of the necessity of God could have entered the head of an animal as savage and wicked as man, so holy, so touching and so wise is that idea, so much does it honour man.

This passage is read too quickly. Ivan does not repeat Voltaire; he turns him around. In Voltaire, the next line says what the invented God is for: “Que le sage l'annonce, et que les rois le craignent”, let the wise man proclaim him, and let kings fear him. The God of the *Épître* is a piece of the social order, guarantor of oaths and curb on the powerful, a useful God whom the wise man proclaims because society needs him. The line contains in germ the Grand Inquisitor, two chapters further on: an invented God, proclaimed by the one who knows, to hold in check those who do not know. By having Ivan quote Voltaire on the threshold of “Rebellion” and “The Grand Inquisitor”, Dostoevsky signals that he knows the lineage: the Inquisitor is not a Russian invention, but the culmination of the utilitarian deism of the Enlightenment. The response is attested in the strict sense, since the text names the saying; it is also constructed, since it is I who link the line of 1769 to the poem of 1879. I take the link to be a key to the chapter.

The novel returns to Voltaire in Book X, chapter 6, in the mouth of young Kolya Krasotkin, a schoolboy who plays the freethinker. Kolya declares to Alyosha: “можно ведь и не веруя в бога любить человечество, как вы думаете? Вольтер же не веровал в бога, а любил человечество?”, one can surely love humanity without believing in God, what do you think? Voltaire did not believe in God, and yet he loved humanity, didn't he? Alyosha replies gently that Voltaire did believe in God, but little, it seems, and that he loved humanity little, it seems. Beneath its comic air, the exchange engages what is essential: Kolya formulates, as a schoolboy would, Bayle's thesis: virtue does not depend on faith, and a society of atheists can be as humane as any other. Alyosha does not refute it; he answers with a nuance about love, as if he knew that the love of humanity, in one who no longer believes, risks remaining a love of the idea of humanity.

This exchange opens a constructed confrontation between Bayle and Ivan, which must be carried through to the end. In the *Pensées diverses sur la comète* (1682, under the title of *Lettre*, then 1683), Bayle had announced his “conjectures sur les mœurs d'une société d'athées”, conjectures on the morals of a society of atheists, and maintained, as we saw in Part Three, that such a society, provided severe laws punished crimes in it, would be no worse than a society of pagans, human justice being what makes the virtue of the greater number. The thesis has two faces: liberal, since it detaches morality from religion; deeply pessimistic, since it explains the virtue of believers themselves by fear of the constable rather than by fear of God.

Ivan seems to maintain the opposite thesis: without immortality, no virtue, therefore everything is permitted. The confrontation is in reality subtler. Bayle speaks of society, Ivan speaks of conscience. Bayle is no doubt right on his own ground: a society of atheists can hold together, with laws and prisons, and secularized societies have since amply demonstrated it. But Ivan does not ask whether a society of atheists can hold together; he asks whether a man alone, before himself, still has a reason not to kill when no one is watching. And the novel entrusts the answer to Smerdyakov, who kills precisely because he has taken the formula seriously, then to Ivan himself, who goes mad from having understood that he was the intellectual author of the murder. Bayle answered the question of order; Dostoevsky answers the question of guilt. Between them there is no contradiction but a displacement, and this displacement makes the greatness of the novel.

From his rebellion, Ivan draws the consequence his adversaries attribute to him, and he owns it. The formula “if God does not exist, everything is permitted”, in this proverbial form, is not in the novel. The novel contains a thesis that is always conditional, reported by several characters: Miusov in Book II, Rakitin (“Нет бессмертия души, так нет и добродетели, значит, всё позволено”, no immortality of the soul, so no virtue, which means everything is permitted), Mitya, Smerdyakov, the devil. And Ivan himself, in Book V, chapter 5, before Alyosha: “От формулы "всё позволено" я не отрекусь”, I will not renounce the formula “everything is permitted”; Mongault: “Comme je ne renierai pas cette formule que "tout est permis", alors c'est toi qui me renieras, n'est-ce pas ?”, since I will not renounce this formula that “everything is permitted”, then it is you who will renounce me, is it not? Just before, Ivan has already taken it up as his own: “Да, пожалуй: "всё позволено", если уж слово произнесено. Не отрекаюсь”, yes, if you like, “everything is permitted”, since the word has been spoken; I do not take it back.

This rebellion resembles nothing that came before. Ivan is not an anti-Spinoza out of ignorance: he refuses to *understand*. He says he is endowed with an “ум эвклидовский, земной”, a Euclidean, earthly mind (V, 3), incapable of grasping how parallel lines meet at infinity, and he refuses to have them made to meet for him. Where Spinoza said that to understand necessity is to cease suffering from it, Ivan answers that the suffering of a child must not be understood. He is also, avant la lettre, the anti-Zarathustra: he refuses to *consent*. He is finally the anti-Hegel, and this is perhaps the deepest point: the final reconciliation in which all sorrows find their meaning in the whole, Ivan refuses in advance, not because he judges it impossible, but because he judges it obscene. He prefers, he says, to remain with his unavenged suffering and his unappeased indignation, even if he is wrong.

The “poem” of the Grand Inquisitor, in Book V, chapter 5, is in my eyes the centre of gravity of our whole question. In Seville, in the sixteenth century, Christ returns; the old cardinal has him arrested and explains to him, through a whole night, why he must be burned. Christ wanted men to follow him freely; but men do not want freedom, they want bread and someone before whom to bow. The Church has therefore “corrected” the work of Christ by founding it on three forces: “чудо, тайна и авторитет”, miracle, mystery and authority. Mongault: “Nous avons corrigé ton œuvre en la fondant sur le miracle, le mystère, l'autorité”, we have corrected your work by founding it on miracle, mystery, authority. The Inquisitor does not believe in God; he half says so, and this is his tragic greatness: he alone bears the weight of truth so that the others may be happy in the lie.

Here, in a single figure, is the novel's answer to our question. The Inquisitor is exactly what one obtains when one leaves God while keeping religion: an atheist who holds faith to be indispensable to men, and who governs it out of love for them. He is the political culmination of the Feuerbachian critique, a religion of humanity administered by those who know. He is also, by a comparison I construct, a transposition of Comte as high priest of Humanity: a spiritual power that does not believe in the supernatural, but knows that men need worship, mystery and direction. But Comte believes in his Great Being; the Inquisitor believes only in the weakness of men. The Inquisitor is a Comte who has become lucid, and therefore tragic.

And Christ's answer is not an argument. He is silent, then: “тихо целует его в его бескровные девяностолетние уста”, he kisses him gently on his bloodless ninety-year-old lips; Mongault: “le Prisonnier s'approche en silence du nonagénaire et baise ses lèvres exsangues”, the Prisoner silently approaches the old man of ninety and kisses his bloodless lips. The kiss refutes nothing: it refuses to enter the order of justification, which is the Inquisitor's. One may see in it an answer of another order, which is neither a thesis nor an institution, but a gesture, a way of being before the other: what the twentieth century will call spirituality, what remains of the religious once dogma and the Church are taken away.

The answer of the whole novel is of the same order. Book VI, devoted to the starets Zosima, opposes to Ivan not a theodicy but a form of life, which comes down to two sentences. That of Zosima's younger brother, Markel, as he is dying: “всякий пред всеми за всех и за всё виноват”, each is guilty before all, for all and for everything. And that of Zosima on hell: “Отцы и учители, мыслю: "Что есть ад?" Рассуждаю так: "Страдание о том, что нельзя уже более любить"”, fathers and teachers, I ask myself: what is hell? the suffering of no longer being able to love; Mongault: “la souffrance de ne plus pouvoir aimer”, the suffering of no longer being able to love. This is not a proof of God, but the description of a way of existing in which Ivan's question is transformed without being resolved. The novel does not say that Ivan is wrong; it says that one cannot live in his position, and that whoever wants to live in it goes mad.

This answer is strange in relation to the series. Spinoza answered the need for God with knowledge, Feuerbach with reappropriation, Marx with the transformation of the world, Comte with the organization of a cult. Zosima answers with a conversion of the gaze, which does not abolish evil but changes the subject's relation to evil. It is not a doctrine that could be set against Ivan's, it is an exercise, in the sense in which Hadot will speak of the spiritual exercises of Antiquity. The comparison is constructed; it announces the last part of this essay: the answer the novel opposes to the Inquisitor's religion is not another religion, it is a spirituality. Within the novel itself, moreover, this Christianity is suspect to the institution: Zosima's corpse smells, against the expectation of the faithful who hoped for a miracle, and the scandal shows that his faith has nothing to do with miracle, mystery and authority.

Dostoevsky also saw the other outcome of the death of God, the man who makes himself god. This is Kirillov, in *Besy* (*Demons*, 1871-1872), the engineer who kills himself to prove his freedom and to become god, according to the logic he states himself: “Если нет бога, то я бог”, if there is no God, then I am god. The Inquisitor and Kirillov sketch the two possible refoundations: religion without God administered by a few, and the divinization of man. Nietzsche, who would read *Les Possédés* in the French translation by Victor Derély (Plon, 1886) and copy passages from it into his notebooks from November 1887 to March 1888, without knowing *The Brothers Karamazov*, and Camus, who would know them very well, would each take them up in their own way.

In this series, Dostoevsky does not receive objections: he hands them out. Nietzsche would salute him in *Götzen-Dämmerung*, “Streifzüge” 45: Dostoevsky is “des einzigen Psychologen, anbei gesagt, von dem ich Etwas zu lernen hatte”, the only psychologist, incidentally, from whom I had something to learn. Nietzsche discovered Dostoevsky in February 1887, in a bookshop in Nice, through a French translation published under the title *L'Esprit souterrain* (trans. Halpérine-Kaminsky and Ch. Morice, Plon, 1886). He thus discovered Dostoevsky without the book in which the encounter would have been most evident; yet he would take up as genealogy what Dostoevsky had staged as drama.

> **En littérature.** Herman Melville, *Billy Budd* (1924) ; Victor Hugo, *Les Misérables* (1862)
>
> In *Billy Budd*, completed on the eve of Melville's death and published in 1924, the young foretopman Billy, falsely accused of mutiny by the master-at-arms Claggart, kills him with a blow of his fist, unable to speak. Captain Vere, who knows the innocence of his heart, cries out: “Struck dead by an angel of God. Yet the angel must hang!” He convenes a drumhead court, himself argues against the judges' pity in the name of the law of war and the order of the ship, and has Billy hanged at dawn; Billy dies blessing him [summary].
>
> Why here: Vere is the Grand Inquisitor in uniform, and Melville made him a good man. He knows that truth is on the side of the innocent, and he sacrifices him to the peace of the multitude, because, he says in substance, the men of the crew would not understand his being spared. This is the mechanism the essay describes with Girard and that the Inquisitor administers: a victim designated so that order may hold. Billy's blessing is Christ's kiss, a gesture that is not an argument.
>
> Javert, in *Les Misérables*, after Jean Valjean has spared him at the barricade and he in turn has let Valjean go, discovers two roads: “Il voyait devant lui deux routes également droites toutes deux, mais il en voyait deux ; et cela le terrifiait, lui qui n'avait jamais connu dans sa vie qu'une ligne droite”, he saw before him two roads, both equally straight, but he saw two; and that terrified him, he who had never in his life known anything but one straight line; he feels “que quelque chose d'horrible pénétrait dans son âme, l'admiration pour un forçat”, that something horrible was entering his soul, admiration for a convict, and throws himself into the Seine. This is the other face of the Inquisitor: the law as absolute, which does not survive the encounter with a grace it can neither punish nor understand. Smerdyakov, whom the essay reads with Bayle, has no such excuse.
