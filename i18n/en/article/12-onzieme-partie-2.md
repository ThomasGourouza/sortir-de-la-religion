### Voegelin (1901-1985) and Aron (1905-1983): secular religions

Schmitt and Löwith speak of concepts and of philosophies of history. Two authors, at almost the same moment and without having consulted each other, carried the question onto harsher ground: that of the regimes which, in the twentieth century, actually demanded of millions of people that they sacrifice themselves, or sacrifice others, to an earthly absolute. Eric Voegelin called them political religions, Raymond Aron secular religions. Both expressions name what this essay has been describing from the start, and that is why I place them at the heart of the secularization quarrel rather than in the chapter on totalitarianisms where they are usually filed.

Voegelin, born in Cologne and trained in Vienna under Hans Kelsen, was teaching political science at the University of Vienna when, in the spring of 1938, he had a short and dense essay printed, *Die politischen Religionen* (*The Political Religions*). The first edition, ready in April 1938, was suppressed by the National Socialist management imposed on the publisher Bermann-Fischer after the Anschluss; Voegelin, stripped of his licence to teach, left Vienna on 13 July, and the second edition appeared in Stockholm, with a preface dated Christmas 1938. The book's thesis is summed up by the *Neue Deutsche Biographie*: Voegelin interprets National Socialism as a form of innerworldly religiosity, “innerweltlicher Religiosität, die das rassisch determinierte Volk vergöttliche und mit einem eigenen Mythus der Erlösung verknüpfe”, a religiosity that deifies the racially determined people and joins to it a myth of redemption of its own. The central concept is that of *innerweltlich*, innerworldly, religion: a religion whose *realissimum*, its supreme object, is no longer beyond the world but within it, race, class, the State, and which nonetheless receives from it everything that religions received from their god, faith, worship, obedience, promise.

Fourteen years later, in the United States, Voegelin gave this intuition a genealogy in *The New Science of Politics* (Chicago, 1952), where it joins the quarrel between Löwith and Blumenberg. We must, he writes, “recognize the essence of modernity as the growth of gnosticism”. By Gnosticism Voegelin does not mean first of all the ancient sect, which he knows through Hans Jonas, but an attitude: the certainty of possessing a knowledge that makes it possible to transform the world and deliver it from its imperfection. Its point of origin is Joachim of Fiore, whom Löwith was encountering at the same moment at the bottom of his own genealogy, and its mechanism is immanentization: “The attempt at constructing an eidos of history will lead into the fallacious immanentization of the Christian eschaton”. The formula, which American conservatism later reduced to a slogan, says what Löwith said of Marx and of Comte: the salvation that Christianity awaited from the end of time is promised by human action within time. But Voegelin pushes the analysis as far as the political regime. Totalitarianism is not for him an accident of modernity, it is its outcome: “Totalitarianism, defined as the existential rule of Gnostic activists, is the end form of progressive civilization”. He gives the reason in terms this essay could adopt: modern Gnosticism sought to supply society with the civil theology that Christianity denied it, and the totalitarianism of our time must be understood, he writes, as “journey's end of the Gnostic search for a civil theology”.

One passage of the book bears closely on our first part. Voegelin there takes up, in connection with the Puritans, the old thesis of the *falāsifa* according to which the multitude must not touch philosophical questions, and he concludes, after evoking the murder of God that Nietzsche had announced, that one must admit that “Averroës had a point”. The connection is attested, and it throws light on Voegelin's leaning: his diagnosis of modernity is also a nostalgia for the medieval reserve, which kept speculation on the meaning of history out of “vulgar” hands. This is Averroes' hierarchical division, and, give or take a few nuances, the gesture of Leo Strauss.

Raymond Aron reached the same notion by another path, that of sociology and of war. Editor-in-chief of *La France libre* in London, he published there during the war two articles on secular religions, the better known of which, “L'avenir des religions séculières”, dates from 1944. The definition he proposes there has become classic; I give it in paraphrase, not having been able to verify its wording: Aron calls secular religions those doctrines which take, in the souls of his contemporaries, the place left by vanished faith, and which locate the salvation of humanity here below, in the distant future, in the form of a social order to be created. The definition contains three elements: a place left empty, that of faith, a vocabulary Blumenberg would rediscover more than twenty years later; a salvation, that of humanity; a displacement of this salvation here below, into a social order to be built. Aron does not say that communism is a religion because it resembles Christianity; he says that it is a religion because it fixes a last end and judges good and evil in relation to it. A commentator quotes in this connection a sentence of Aron's in English translation: such doctrines “set up an ultimate and quasisacred goal and define good and evil in relation to this ideal”.

Eleven years later, Aron drew from this analysis a polemical book, *The Opium of the Intellectuals* (1955), whose title turns Marx's formula against the Marxists: it is no longer religion that is the opium of the people, it is Marxism that has become the opium of those who think. This is the gesture this essay has seen recur from author to author, from Stirner to Camus. Aron formulates it as a sociologist, without Stirner's violence or Camus's lyricism, and that is his strength. He does not reproach intellectuals for hoping; he reproaches them for having turned a political hypothesis into a truth of salvation, and for having drawn from it the right to justify, in the name of the future, what they would have condemned in the present. The connection with Camus is constructed: *The Rebel* (1951) and *The Opium of the Intellectuals* are, four years apart, the two great French books against the deification of history, one written by a moralist, the other by a sociologist, and they earned their authors the same hostility from the intellectual left.

This civil theology also has ancestors we read much earlier. The expression comes from Varro, by way of Augustine, and Voegelin handles it as a connoisseur of *The City of God*; but the thing has antecedents closer to our purpose. Plato, in Book X of the *Laws*, had wanted a theology of the city armed with a penal law, and Rousseau, in the penultimate chapter of *The Social Contract* (IV, 8), a civil religion whose dogmas were guarded by banishment and death. Voegelin does not make this connection in the pages I have read; it is my own, and it throws light on the difference between the two epochs. Rousseau wanted a civil religion limited to a few dogmas, which left each person the rest of their conscience; Plato already went further, as far as forbidding private worship. The political religions of the twentieth century no longer leave anything over: they occupy the whole soul, because they lay claim at once to knowledge, to salvation and to government. What Voegelin calls Gnosticism is perhaps first of all this, the fusion of what Plato and Rousseau still kept apart, the opinion useful to the city and the truth that sets free.

The connection with Marcel Gauchet is of another order. Gauchet took up the notion of secular religion on his own account to describe the totalitarianisms, and his reading is closer to Aron's than to Voegelin's: he sees in them not a return of heretical Christianity, but an attempt to reconstitute, in a society that has left religion, the unity and hierarchy that religion once ensured. If this reading is right, secular religions do not prove that the exit from religion is impossible, but that it is dangerous, because it leaves behind it a nostalgia for the One that regimes can exploit: a conclusion much closer to the criterion than to Voegelin's genealogical thesis.

These two notions confirm this criterion, and they test it. They confirm it, because the feature that Voegelin and Aron each retain in their own way is that of the criterion: secular religion is a belief that authorizes sacrifice. The deified Volk of 1938 demands that one die for it and kill for it; the social order to come of 1944 justifies sacrificing present generations to those who will enjoy it. The sacrifice criterion, set out in the prologue and refined in connection with Feuerbach, finds here its most massive and bloodiest cases: after Voegelin and Aron, one can no longer say that refoundation is a matter for philosophers and positivist sacristies. It has had its police, its camps and its trials.

But they also test it, for the notion of secular religion runs the risk already recognized in the lazy form of the secularization thesis: seeing gods everywhere. The objection was formulated as early as the 1950s by Voegelin's own teacher. Hans Kelsen devoted to the notion a manuscript, *Secular Religion*, which he withdrew from the University of California Press in 1964 and which was not published until 2012; in it he attacks Voegelin and all those who see a theological structure in modern doctrines that postulate no belief in God, on the grounds that a theory capable of proving everything, even the religious character of irreligious theories, ends up proving nothing, and turns the concept of religion into an empty shell. This attested response is aimed at a former pupil. Blumenberg, for his part, never debated with Voegelin: the colloquium Jacob Taubes wanted to organize between them in Berlin in 1967 did not take place, and no direct exchange is known; but *Die Legitimität der Neuzeit* takes the word Gnosticism from Voegelin in order to reverse its meaning, since it makes modernity not a triumphant Gnosticism but a “second overcoming of Gnosticism”. The confrontation is therefore half attested: Blumenberg answers Voegelin's thesis in a book, he does not answer Voegelin.

I believe that Kelsen and Blumenberg are right against Voegelin's genealogical thesis, and wrong against Aron's functional thesis. Voegelin asserts a filiation: modernity *comes* from Gnosticism, through Joachim and the medieval sects, as from a heresy that ended up winning. This filiation is unverifiable in its generality, and it has the drawback of making the whole of modernity, liberalism and science included, one and the same evil, which Voegelin himself moreover toned down in his later work, according to his entry in the *Neue Deutsche Biographie*. Aron, for his part, asserts an analogy of function: a political doctrine becomes religious when it occupies the place of faith in souls and receives from it the power to demand. This analogy presupposes no inheritance; it is a matter of observation. It is therefore compatible with Blumenberg, for whom the empty places of theology call for reoccupations, and it makes precise what he leaves in the shadow: not all reoccupations are of equal worth. Some answer an inherited question with a modest hypothesis, others with a sacrificial absolute. Secular religion, in Aron's sense, is the name of the second kind, and it is indispensable to me. The notion of political religion, understood in Voegelin's genealogical sense, is in its turn a philosophy of history, one of decline, and it falls under the reproach it addresses to the others.

### Leo Strauss (1899-1973): Athens and Jerusalem

All the authors of this part argue about the way in which we left religion. Leo Strauss disputes that we left it at all. He disputes it not as a believer, which he never claimed to be, but as a philosopher, and that is why he is, I believe, the most serious adversary of the very thesis of the exit.

Strauss was born in 1899 in Kirchhain, a small rural town near Marburg, into an Orthodox Jewish family; he studied at Marburg, defended in 1921, under the supervision of Ernst Cassirer, a dissertation on Jacobi, and spent 1922 in Freiburg, where he attended Husserl's lectures and fell, like Levinas, Gadamer, Arendt and Karl Löwith, whose friend and correspondent he would remain, under the sway of Heidegger. His first book, written between 1925 and 1928 and published in 1930, is devoted to Spinoza's critique of religion, that is, to the very book that our third part made one of the founding acts of modern critique. In the preface to the English edition of 1965 he would present it as the work of a young Jew in Germany caught “in the grip of the theologico-political predicament”. This theologico-political problem, the question whether a society can be founded on reason alone or whether it rests on a revealed law, would be, he says elsewhere, “the theme of my studies”.

The book's thesis, as the 1965 preface restates it, reverses the story this essay has followed. Spinoza, and with him the whole Enlightenment critique, did not refute revelation. He could not, for belief in an omnipotent God who reveals himself when he wills and hides himself when he wills rests on a premise that, according to Strauss, can be refuted neither by experience nor by the principle of contradiction. And the indirect proof of this is the very means the critics used: “Spinoza and his like owed such success as they had in their fight against orthodoxy to laughter and mockery”; they tried to drive orthodoxy out by laughing at a position from which no proof drawn from Scripture or from reason could dislodge it. The modern critique of religion would thus not have won its case; it would have won a battle of opinion. And a genuine refutation would demand more than any philosopher has ever supplied: “The genuine refutation of orthodoxy would require the proof that the world and human life are perfectly intelligible without the assumption of a mysterious God”; it would require at the very least the completion of the philosophical system.

This sentence says that the exit from religion, in the strong sense in which the Enlightenment understood it, would have presupposed that reason become a total knowledge, master of the world and of life. Now that is what I have called a refoundation. The only possible refutation of revelation would be the installation of another absolute, completed reason, man the master of everything; and we know what secular religions made of that ambition. Strauss thus closes the door on both sides: one does not refute revelation without making oneself god, and if one gives up making oneself god, one does not refute it.

Conversely, and Strauss insists on this with the same rigour, revelation does not refute philosophy either. In *Natural Right and History* (1953), he formulates this impasse in the harshest terms for philosophy itself: “Philosophy has to grant that revelation is possible”, which amounts to granting that the philosophic life is not necessarily the right life; and further on, “the mere fact that philosophy and revelation cannot refute each other would constitute the refutation of philosophy by revelation”. For philosophy claims to be a life grounded on evidence; if it itself rests on a choice that no evidence grounds, it confirms what faith said of it. It is this conflict without an arbiter that Strauss calls Athens and Jerusalem, a title he gave to several lectures, in 1946, in 1950 and in 1967, the last published the same year in *Commentary*.

One recognizes here, displaced by eight centuries, the question of Averroes and of Maimonides, and it is no accident: it was by reading them that Strauss formed his thesis. *Persecution and the Art of Writing* (1952) maintains that the philosophers before modernity, under the threat of persecution, wrote in such a way as to convey one teaching to the majority of their readers and another to a few; Maimonides, Judah Halevi and Spinoza himself are said to be among these writers. Strauss took seriously the warning Maimonides places at the head of the *Guide*, not to explain his secret teaching, to the point of writing that no historian possessed of a sense of decency could lightly disregard the entreaty of a superior man such as Maimonides. We find again Averroes' division between those who demonstrate and those who are persuaded, which the first part set out; Strauss makes of it, rather than a prudence of circumstance, a permanent truth about the relation between philosophy and the city: the city lives on opinions, and in particular on opinions about the gods, which philosophy can neither share nor destroy without danger.

This thesis, hotly debated, seems to me partly fragile: it is unverifiable by construction, since the absence of signs of a hidden teaching can always be read as proof of its skill, and it has fostered among some disciples a way of reading texts in which suspicion takes the place of philology. One must also recall a letter Strauss wrote from Paris to Löwith in 1933: in it he asserted that one could protest against what he called the “shabby abomination” of the Hitler regime only on the basis of the principles of the right, “from fascist, authoritarian, and imperial principles”. The correspondence with Löwith, partly published under the title “Correspondence Concerning Modernity” (*Independent Journal of Philosophy*, 4, 1983, and 5/6, 1988, trans. G. E. Tucker), is one of the places where his position takes shape: the two former pupils of Heidegger, so close in their critique of historicism, diverge there over the possibility of a return to the Ancients.

His relations to the other protagonists complete his placing. As early as 1932, he wrote a review of Carl Schmitt's *The Concept of the Political*, in which he argued that Schmitt, despite all his efforts, remained a prisoner of the liberal horizon he was fighting, for want of being able to give reasons for his commitment to the political. The critique joins ours by another path: Strauss sees in a sovereign decision that cannot say in the name of what it decides the very symptom of the modernity Schmitt claimed to overcome. With Löwith he shares the rejection of progress and the gaze turned towards the Greeks; but where Löwith withdraws into the Stoic contemplation of nature, Strauss reopens the political question, that of the best regime, which classical philosophy posed and which modernity is said to have abandoned. With Blumenberg, finally, no dialogue is attested, but the disagreement is total: Blumenberg defends the legitimacy of the modern age as self-assertion, Strauss sees in it a break with ancient wisdom, of which Machiavelli is said to be the author and which the critique of religion is said to have made possible.

Strauss also strikes at our reading of Spinoza. The third part presented the *Theological-Political Treatise* as one of the acts by which modern philosophy emancipated itself from Scripture, and Spinoza as one of the rare exceptions in the series, the one who keeps the name of God without refounding a cult. Strauss does not dispute that Spinoza wanted to free philosophy; he disputes that he refuted what he was freeing it from. And he adds a suspicion our reading had not considered: that Spinoza himself wrote so as to be read in two ways, and that his apparent piety, the piety that made him a God-intoxicated man in the eyes of the Romantics, is in part an art of writing. I do not believe this suspicion undoes the reading of the *Ethics* that this essay has defended, for the intellectual love of God is demonstrated there, not suggested; but it obliges us to acknowledge that the Spinozist exception rests on a philosophical decision, that of holding the world to be intelligible without remainder, which Spinoza could no more prove than orthodoxy could prove revelation.

There remains from Strauss an objection I do not believe I can lift, and which I want to give in its best form. This essay's criterion says that one refounds a religion when one installs an absolute that demands sacrifice. Strauss accepts it, I believe, but he draws from it a more radical consequence. If the total exit from religion would presuppose the completion of reason, then every exit that believes itself total is already a refoundation, and the only honest position left is one that acknowledges that the question is not settled. It resembles that of the recognized exceptions, Spinoza, Hume, Nietzsche, Camus, in that it sacrifices no one to anything; it differs from them in that it does not claim to have found, in a spirituality of exercise or in a solidarity without a heaven, a peaceful way out. It keeps open, between Athens and Jerusalem, a tension it judges constitutive of the West and which it refuses to resolve.

> Asked by Saladin about the true religion, Nathan answers with the parable of the three rings: “Vor grauen Jahren lebt' ein Mann in Osten, / Der einen Ring von unschätzbarem Wert / Aus lieber Hand besaß”, in hoary years there lived a man in the East who possessed, from a beloved hand, a ring of priceless worth, which made whoever wore it in confidence pleasing to God and to men; the father, loving his three sons equally, had two copies made, and no one could any longer tell the true one. The judge to whom the dispute is brought sends the brothers away: “Es eifre jeder seiner unbestochnen / Von Vorurteilen freien Liebe nach!”, let each of them strive to emulate his father's love, uncorrupted and free of prejudice, and let the ring's virtue show itself in works, and in a thousand thousand years a wiser judge will speak.
>
> Why here: the parable states exactly the position Strauss holds against the idea of an exit, and with the same reservations. Neither revelation nor reason has refuted the other, and judgment is deferred to a judge who does not come; but Lessing, unlike Strauss, draws from it a moral of tolerance that is already an answer, and Strauss, who read Lessing all his life, reproached him precisely for having written so as to be heard by two audiences. The authentic ring is not lost, it is undecidable; this is the difference between Athens and Jerusalem made livable, and perhaps too livable.
