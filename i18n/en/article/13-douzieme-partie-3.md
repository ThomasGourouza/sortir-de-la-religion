### The Buddhism of the philosophers: a spirituality without God?

If one looks in the history of religions for a spirituality without a creator God, Buddhism is the obvious candidate; it has haunted this essay since Schopenhauer without ever being looked at squarely. This must be done with a precaution that is the very subject of this section: the Buddhism the European philosophers of the nineteenth century speak of is not, first of all, the Buddhism of Buddhist countries; it is a figure that Europe constructed, largely out of its own anxieties, and that it successively admired, feared and then adopted. Our question therefore arises twice: is Buddhism an exit from religion without refoundation? And did Europe, in taking it as a model, find an exit, or did it invent for itself a replacement religion?

Schopenhauer is one of the first great European philosophers to claim Buddhism as his own, and he does so in the name of atheism. In the critique of the Kantian philosophy that closes the first volume of *The World*, he rails against the habit of translating the Brahman of the Hindus or the Tien of the Chinese as “God”, and he lays down as an established fact that Buddhism knows nothing of the God of the monotheisms: “Daß namentlich der Buddhaismus, diese auf Erden am zahlreichsten vertretene Religion, durchaus keinen Theismus enthält, ja, ihn perhorrescirt, ist eine ganz ausgemachte Sache”, that Buddhism in particular, this religion with the greatest number of adherents on earth, contains absolutely no theism, and even abhors it, is a thoroughly settled matter. Just before, he had asked whether theism properly so called is not found only in the Jewish religion and in the two religions that came out of it. Buddhism is thus for him the historical proof that a religion can do without God, and that it is all the truer for doing without him.

What he finds there above all is the confirmation of his own doctrine of salvation. The fourth book of *The World*, whose final sentence on nothingness we read in Part Six, makes nirvana the mythical expression of the denial of the will to live. In § 63, Schopenhauer quotes the Buddhist promise in the form in which he had read it: “Du sollst Nirwana erlangen, d.i. einen Zustand, in welchem es vier Dinge nicht giebt: Geburt, Alter, Krankheit und Tod”, you shall attain nirvana, that is, a state in which there are not these four things: birth, old age, sickness and death. And he says of the Indian doctrine of transmigration, of which this promise is the end point, that no myth has ever come, or ever will come, closer to the philosophical truth accessible to so few. But at the end of the book, in § 71, he distances himself from the word itself. Before the nothingness that awaits at the end of all virtue and all holiness, he refuses to do as the Indians do, who evade it “durch Mythen und bedeutungsleere Worte, wie Resorbtion in das Brahm, oder Nirwana der Buddhaisten”, through myths and words empty of meaning, such as reabsorption into Brahman or the nirvana of the Buddhists. He prefers to admit that what remains after the abolition of the will is, for those who still will, nothingness. Schopenhauer admires Buddhism because he finds his pessimism in it, and he corrects it as soon as he finds a consolation in it. In 1856 he had a bronze statuette of the Buddha bought for him in Paris, had it gilded and installed it in his drawing room in Frankfurt.

Nietzsche inherits this reading and turns it around. In *The Antichrist* (1888), he takes care not to confuse the two religions, which he nonetheless ranks together among the nihilist religions, the religions of decadence. Buddhism comes out of the comparison enhanced: “Der Buddhismus ist hundertmal realistischer als das Christentum”, Buddhism is a hundred times more realistic than Christianity; it is heir to a long philosophical tradition, and when it appears, “der Begriff »Gott« ist bereits abgetan”, the concept “God” has already been disposed of. Nietzsche goes further: Buddhism is “die einzige eigentlich positivistische Religion, die uns die Geschichte zeigt”, the only properly positivist religion that history shows us, because it no longer says “Kampf gegen die Sünde”, struggle against sin, but “Kampf gegen das Leiden”, struggle against suffering. The Buddha, in this portrait, is a physician rather than a prophet: he treats a fatigue of civilization with a hygiene, life in the open air, temperance, wariness of the passions that heat the blood, and he excludes prayer as well as asceticism; Nietzsche even notes that there is no compulsion in the monastic community, “man kann wieder hinaus”, one can leave it again. The conclusion is aimed at Christianity: Buddhism is “eine Religion für späte Menschen”, a religion for late human beings, and “Europa ist noch lange nicht reif für ihn”, Europe is still far from ripe for it. And the difference, Nietzsche formulates a little further on: Buddhism does not promise, it delivers, whereas Christianity promises everything and delivers nothing.

Beyond their opposition, these two readings make Buddhism a religion without God and the mirror of their own philosophy: the Buddhism of renunciation for Schopenhauer, the Buddhism of hygiene for Nietzsche. Roger-Pol Droit's inquiry changes the perspective here. In *Le Culte du néant. Les philosophes et le Bouddha* (Seuil, 1997), one of the two books submitted for his doctorate, *La Découverte du bouddhisme et la philosophie européenne (1820-1890)*, defended in 1998 at Paris IV, Droit shows that nineteenth-century Europe long saw in Buddhism a cult of nothingness, a paradoxical and frightening religion that supposedly worshipped annihilation, and that this fear receded as the scholarly study of the texts developed. The scholar who founded this study in France, Eugène Burnouf, in his *Introduction à l'histoire du buddhisme indien* (1844), put the question in the very terms that would feed the fear. He recalls that the etymology of the word designates extinction, and he lists the possible interpretations up to the most radical: “en deux mots, le Nirvâna est-il le néant ?”, in a word, is Nirvana nothingness? Burnouf did not decide, and answered at once that the etymology settled nothing and that the usage of the different schools had to be examined; but the question had been asked, and the philosophers, from Hegel to the French spiritualists, turned it into an answer. Buddhism became, for Europe, the name of what it feared in itself: the nothingness it saw coming after God.

This detour sheds light on our question. When Nietzsche announces that Europe is not ripe for Buddhism, or when he wonders, as early as the preface to *On the Genealogy of Morality*, whether the morality of pity is not European culture's detour “zu einem neuen Buddhismus? zu einem Europäer-Buddhismus?”, towards a new Buddhism, towards a Buddhism of Europeans, he is not speaking first of Asia: he is speaking of the death of God and of what follows it. The Buddhism of the philosophers is the projection, in the sense Feuerbach gave the word, of the question Europe was asking itself: what remains when heaven is empty? Neither Schopenhauer nor Nietzsche describes real Buddhism.

For real Buddhism is not a spirituality without religion. It knows nothing of the creator and personal God of the monotheisms, and Schopenhauer was right on this point; but it is not without gods, who people its cosmology and are themselves caught in the cycle of rebirths, nor without the law of karma, nor rites, nor relics. Above all, it has an institution, the community of monks and nuns, the *saṅgha*, governed by a detailed discipline, the *vinaya*, with its rules, its expulsions and its ordinations. The refuge the faithful pronounce is threefold: the Buddha, the doctrine, the community. This is the point that matters for the criterion: a religion without God, Buddhism is not a religion without Law or without Church, and it shows that the absence of God is not enough to define an exit from religion, since everything else religion comprises, dogma, community, observance, can subsist without him. Conversely, it shows that the absolute of a religion is not necessarily a person: nirvana, the extinction of thirst, can hold the place that God holds elsewhere. Without deciding debates among Indologists that I am in no position to judge, I retain what all of them accept: that historical Buddhism is a complete religious tradition, and not a secular wisdom.

Does it demand a sacrifice? The answer must be nuanced. Monastic renunciation is voluntary, and Nietzsche was right to observe that one can leave the community. Buddhism has, in principle, no political theology, and non-violence is at the heart of its ethics. But history has known state Buddhisms, warrior monks and Buddhist nationalisms, and the fact that the doctrine demands no sacrifice has not prevented the institutions from demanding one. It thus confirms a lesson met with in Girard and in Gauchet: it is not doctrine alone that decides whether a religion sacrifices, it is the meeting of doctrine and power.

The Buddhism the West practises today is, for its part, a religious invention, perhaps the most durable since the religion of Humanity. It extracts from historical Buddhism a technique, meditation, and an ethics of compassion, leaving aside the cosmology, the rebirths, the gods and often monasticism. Comte-Sponville, whose spirituality of immanence gives a large place to the Eastern wisdoms, offers a philosophical version of it; the secular meditation of hospitals and companies is its technical version. This Buddhism is indeed a spirituality without God; but it is also a reinvention, which repeats the gesture of the nineteenth-century philosophers taking from Buddhism what they needed. It refounds no religion, since it demands nothing and installs no absolute. But it runs the opposite risk, the one Murdoch would have pointed out: an attention that no longer aims at anything real, an inner peace that is sufficient unto itself and can accommodate itself to any world whatever. Meditation taught to make employees more efficient is the opposite of what Nietzsche called a hygiene against the fatigue of civilization: it is its palliative care.

Historical Buddhism, I believe, is not the proof that one can leave religion by leaving God; it is the proof that religion is not defined by God. The Buddhism of the philosophers is not a description but a symptom: that of a Europe seeking, after the death of God, a form of salvation without a saviour. And contemporary Western Buddhism illustrates the thesis in its own way: one can keep spirituality while leaving religion, provided one knows what one is keeping and where one has it from. Those who meditate knowing that they are borrowing a technique from a tradition they do not share are in the position of Spinoza keeping the name of God; those who think they have found in meditation a wisdom without history and without presuppositions are in the position of the atheists of the marketplace, who do not see the shadows they carry.

### The spiritualities of the Universe: a dictated Christ, an enchanted physics

The wisdoms this part examines have authors, books and a history that can be argued over. What most of our contemporaries meet under the name of spirituality, outside the Churches, rarely has any of these: texts received from a higher plane, a Universe that answers our desires, a quantum physics invoked to guarantee that consciousness creates reality. They must nevertheless be looked at, for two reasons. It is in this form, far more than in that of Hadot or Murdoch, that spirituality without a Church exists today in bookshops and on screens; and these spiritualities put the criterion to a test that is the reverse of the one Buddhism set. Buddhism was a religion without God; these present themselves as spiritualities without religion, and we shall see that they keep what is most questionable in religion without keeping what is strongest in it. I take two cases, chosen because they are typical, not because they are important, and I warn that they are not, in the sense I give the phrase in the rest of this essay, spiritualities without God: the first keeps Christ, the second replaces God with the Universe, which is not the same thing as doing without him.

The *Christ Letters* (in French, *Les Lettres du Christ*) are nine letters presented as dictated by Christ himself to an anonymous South African woman who calls herself his “Recorder” (in the French edition, his “Porte-parole”), circulated online and then published as a book; in French under the title *Le Christ revient, révèle sa surprenante vérité* (Interkeltia, 2010), then as *Les Lettres du Christ* (Atlantes, 2012, then Pocket). In them Christ returns to correct the misinterpretations of the teaching he had given in Palestine under the name of Jesus. There is no sin against God; there is no Trinity; above all, there is no salvation through the cross. In the third letter, addressed to Good Friday, he asks that the drama attached to that day be abandoned: “I died[…] and that was, for me, a wondrous release”; and he demands that one turn away “from the old dogmas of ‘salvation by the blood of the lamb’, the Trinity, and other beliefs”. What replaces dogma is a metaphysics of universal Consciousness, of the ego as the source of all suffering, and of a creative energy that the letters describe in the vocabulary of electricity, magnetism and particles.

The tools of this essay are enough to place this text, and there is no need to invent others. Schweitzer had shown that every age makes itself the Jesus it needs: the Jesus of the *Letters* is a master of consciousness, without apocalypse, without the Jewish Law, without a redeeming cross, that is, the exact opposite of the prophet the historians reconstruct, and the exact portrait of the spirituality of personal development. Renan had his fifth gospel, which was a landscape; this is a sixth, which presents itself as a dictation, and that is the decisive point. Kierkegaard, Barth and Weil criticized religion in the name of faith by renouncing any possession of God; the *Letters* criticize religion by making him speak. They contest the authority of the Churches in the name of an authority higher than that of all the Churches, and they submit it to no control: the notice placed at the head of each letter asks the reader first to make silence within, because it is only in this “completely receptive” state that the letters will penetrate their thought with their reality. One recognizes the gesture of Otto asking the reader without experience not to read on; but Otto described an experience, he did not claim to transcribe it at the dictation of its object. The objection I addressed to Schleiermacher applies here without softening: the Recorder's experience proves that she felt something, it does not prove that this something was Christ. There remains James's criterion, judging by the fruits and not by the roots; it is the most charitable, and it is on this ground that the text must be judged.

On one point, and it is no small one, the fruits are good. The *Letters* refuse the sacrificial reading of the cross, the one Paul had installed by spiritualizing sacrifice instead of abolishing it, and which Girard, by an entirely different route, turned against the religion of sacrifice. A Christ who declares that he did not die to redeem anyone, and who asks that people stop mortifying themselves in his memory, stands on the side of the criterion. But the sacrifice driven out of the cross comes back by another door. God, say the *Letters*, cannot save man, because humanity, in ignorance of the laws of existence, will go on making the same mistakes, “thus creating his own sickness and misery”; the law of cause and effect is “imponderable”, and one can no more escape the law by which one reaps what one has sown in one's thoughts and feelings than the laws of electromagnetism, “for electromagnetism is the IMPULSE producing the Law of sowing and reaping”. The sixth letter specifies that the forms created by the ego's thoughts disturb the functioning of the body and are “the origin of physical discomforts or viruses or disease”. It is no longer a God who demands, it is a law; but the law makes the sick person the author of their sickness. The mechanism Girard described at the scale of the crowd is found here at the scale of the individual, in its gentlest and most insidious form: the victim is declared guilty of their own misfortune, and by no one, by the sole logic of a doctrine that leaves no room for chance. Paul's old sentence, whatever a man sows, that he will also reap, receives the guarantee of physics.

That guarantee is the second case. Quantum mysticism has a genealogy, which I summarize from the historians of the New Age without entering into the physics, which is beyond my competence. In 1952 Jung published with the physicist Wolfgang Pauli *Naturerklärung und Psyche*, in which he set out synchronicity, the coincidence of a psychic state and an external event linked not by a cause but by a meaning, with the example of the golden scarab that taps at the window while a patient is recounting her dream. In 1975 Fritjof Capra, a physicist by training, published *The Tao of Physics*, which brings quantum mechanics close to the Eastern mystics; in 1980 David Bohm, also a physicist, proposed in *Wholeness and the Implicate Order* an undivided totality that he would discuss with Krishnamurti; in 1989 Deepak Chopra made *Quantum Healing* a medicine of consciousness; the film *What the Bleep Do We Know!?* (2004) and then Rhonda Byrne's *The Secret* (2006) popularized the “law of attraction”, according to which our thoughts attract the events that resemble them. The physicists have answered, from Murray Gell-Mann, who spoke of “quantum flapdoodle”, to Victor Stenger (*The Unconscious Quantum*, 1995): the observer of quantum mechanics is a measuring apparatus, not a consciousness that creates the world, and the entanglement of particles does not bind souls together. I have nothing to add to their answer, except that it does not reach what interests this essay.

For the phenomenon is not new, and James had described it a century earlier. In lectures IV and V of the *Varieties*, he devotes long pages to what he calls the “Mind‐cure movement”, which its followers also call “New Thought” and of which the law of attraction is the direct heir. He takes it seriously, as “a genuine religious power”, and notes with irony that it has reached the stage where demand is strong enough for publishers to supply “insincere stuff, mechanically produced for the market”, which happens, he says, only to a religion well past its insecure beginnings. He also sees its limit. For Mrs. Eddy's Christian Science, the most radical branch of the movement, “evil is simply a *lie*, and any one who mentions it is a liar”; James sees in this “a bad speculative omission”. These are the once-born of his typology, and the sky-blue gospel he asked his hearers to turn their backs on in order to listen to the sick souls. Quantum mysticism changes nothing in this structure; it merely gives it a vocabulary more prestigious than that of Mesmer's magnetic fluid, which was its eighteenth-century version.

This vocabulary is nonetheless instructive. This essay has met science turned into an absolute several times: Freud's god Logos, the Reason of the new atheists, and Wittgenstein's remark that the moderns stop before the laws of nature as the ancients did before God and fate. Quantum mysticism makes the reverse and complementary movement: it does not sacralize science, it asks it to testify for the sacred. It is the regime of error turned inside out. Lucretius and d'Holbach set physics against the gods; here physics is called on to restore them, under the name of Consciousness, Energy or the Universe. It is also § 344 of *The Gay Science* in reverse: Nietzsche confessed that science's will to truth still rested on a faith, and drew from it a demand for lucidity; quantum mysticism borrows from science its prestige without its will to truth, that is, without the one thing that made it an ascesis. As for synchronicity, Boyer gives the most economical explanation of it: a human mind equipped to detect agents and intentions everywhere, and Hume before him, who saw faces in the moon. The meaningful coincidence is a world that beckons to me; and a world that beckons to me is a world that addresses me, that is, under another name, Providence.

It is here that wonder changes its nature. Wittgenstein said that the mystical is not how the world is, but that it is; synchronicity and the law of attraction look for the mystical, on the contrary, in the how, in the messages that events send me and in the answers the Universe gives my thoughts. One passes from wonder that there is a world to the conviction that the world answers me, and from there to the idea that it answers me according to what I deserve. I have maintained that to think being is not to owe it something; the law of attraction turns the fact into a gift, the gift into an answer and the answer into a debt, and the manuals that teach it prescribe, logically enough, daily exercises of gratitude towards the Universe.

Measured by the criterion, the verdict must be exact. Neither the *Christ Letters* nor quantum mysticism refounds a religion in the strict sense: they install no absolute in whose name the sacrifice of others could be demanded, they have no clergy, and criticizing them is not a sacrilege, only a mark of ego or of low vibration. They have merchants rather than priests, as James had seen, and a public of pilgrims without a lineage, in Hervieu-Léger's sense. I place them among the other transfers of the sacred: the sacred passes from God to the Universe, or to a Consciousness that has all his attributes except the name. Their fault is not sacrifice, it is consolation, and the indicator I drew from Weil applies to them to the letter. A spirituality in which every misfortune has its cause in the thoughts of the one who suffers it consoles for injustice by making it understandable; but it does worse than console, it suppresses the Protestation Marx heard in the sigh of the oppressed creature. Leibniz asked the individual to sacrifice their complaint for the sake of the harmony of the whole; the law of attraction makes the complaint itself a negative thought, which aggravates the evil it deplores. It is a theodicy without God, and it has the defect of all the theodicies Ivan refused: it is right in advance against the suffering child. And on the day when the belief that one creates one's own illness leads someone to give up treatment, or to have it refused to a child, the sacrifice ceases to be metaphorical; it is there, and not in the vocabulary, that the border runs between a harmless spirituality and a hold over people.

These spiritualities therefore confirm the thesis by a path I did not expect. They do not leave religion: they keep its revelation, its providence and its retribution, and give up only the Church, avowed dogma and the cross, that is, with the cross, the one place where Christianity looked the misfortune of the innocent in the face. They are atheists of the marketplace turned inside out: the men of § 125 did not know that God was dead; these do not know that their Universe is the old Providence in a lab coat. The answer this essay seeks is not to set science against them, since they already invoke it; it is to set against them what science, philosophy and the most demanding believers have in common, and what they have lost: lucidity about what one keeps and where one has it from, and the refusal to make the one who suffers evil bear the blame for it. Wonder that there is a world demands nothing of anyone; the Universe that answers me already demands that I think as I should.
