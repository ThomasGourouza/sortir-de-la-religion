## Part Twelve: spiritualities without God

The word “spirituality” has a shorter history than one might think, and that history is itself an argument. In Christian language, *spiritualitas* designated life according to the Spirit, as opposed to life according to the flesh, and then, in the modern period, the whole body of practices and schools of the inner life: people spoke of Ignatian, Carmelite, Salesian spirituality. Spirituality was a region of religion, the region of experience and exercise, distinct from doctrine and institution. What is new, since the second half of the twentieth century, is that the region has broken away from the continent. People now say “spiritual but not religious”, and they mean by this that they keep the experience while leaving the Church, the exercise while leaving dogma, the search for meaning while leaving the answer given in advance.

Part Eight showed how this detachment was prepared: James defined religion by the experience of individuals in their solitude, Bergson set the dynamic religion of the mystics against static religion, and Freud encountered, in Romain Rolland's oceanic feeling, an experience he admitted he could not find in himself. Spirituality without God inherits these three gestures, and it raises a question. If spiritual experience can be separated from instituted religion, the exit without refoundation is perhaps possible: one would leave religion while keeping spirituality, which, being neither a dogma nor a Church, would refound nothing. But spirituality might also be the subtlest form of refoundation, the one that keeps the sacred while excusing itself from answering for it.

Four French thinkers, who read, criticized and sometimes insulted one another, gave this question its most elaborate forms: Pierre Hadot and Michel Foucault, who open the part, make spirituality an exercise of the subject upon itself; André Comte-Sponville and Luc Ferry, further on, set a spirituality of immanence against a horizontal transcendence, and their quarrel is that of two wisdoms. Between the two pairs, four stages. Karl Jaspers, whom Camus took as a target, had as early as 1948 given the name of philosophical faith to a belief without revelation or Church. A question older than all religions, why there is something rather than nothing, serves as a test for every spirituality without God, in a constructed debate in which Leibniz, Hume, Kant, Schelling, Bergson, Heidegger, Carnap and Wittgenstein answer one another. Iris Murdoch keeps an absolute without God, the Good. Buddhism, as European philosophers have read it, finally offers the model of a religion without a creator God. After the quarrel of the two wisdoms, Mohammed Arkoun and Abdennour Bidar raise the question from within Islam, and the contemporary militant atheists show what becomes of the critique of religion when it ignores this whole path.

### Pierre Hadot (1922-2010): philosophy as spiritual exercise

Pierre Hadot was a Catholic priest before leaving the priesthood in 1952; he became one of the great historians of ancient philosophy, a specialist in Plotinus, Marius Victorinus and Marcus Aurelius, and professor at the Collège de France from 1982. His major work is not a treatise but a collection of articles, *Exercices spirituels et philosophie antique* (1981, enlarged several times up to 2002), complemented by a synthesis, *What Is Ancient Philosophy?* (1995), and by a book of interviews whose title sums everything up, *La Philosophie comme manière de vivre* (2001).

Hadot's thesis is first of all historical, and solid. In Antiquity, philosophy was not primarily a system of propositions about the world, to be adopted or refuted like a scientific theory, but a choice of life. To become an Epicurean, a Stoic, a Platonist, a Cynic was to convert to a way of existing, to enter a school, to adopt a daily discipline. Philosophical discourse, treatises, demonstrations, commentaries, served this conversion: it transformed the existence of the one who studied it, instead of constituting a knowledge detached from him. Hadot readily contrasted this conception with what he called the mere accumulation of knowledge. The Epicureans recalled to mind the fourfold remedy; the Stoics practised the view from above, the premeditation of evils, attention to the present; the Platonists practised dying, in the sense of the *Phaedo*, detaching the soul from the body through thought. These practices Hadot calls spiritual exercises.

Why “spiritual”? The word comes straight from the Christian tradition: the *Spiritual Exercises* of Ignatius of Loyola (1548) are the model of the method. But the debt is reversed, and that is the meaning of all Hadot's work: it was the Christians who inherited the philosophical exercises of Antiquity, when Christianity presented itself, from the second century onwards, as the true philosophy. Monastic Christianity took over the Stoics' attention to oneself, the Pythagoreans' examination of conscience, the Platonists' meditation on death. Philosophy therefore does not borrow the word “spiritual” from religion; it was religion that had borrowed the thing from philosophy. I have not found Hadot's justification for his choice of the word in a primary text; a summary indicates that he saw no difficulty in the expression, and Ossola surmises that Barthes's *Sade, Fourier, Loyola* (1971) had made it available.

Hadot thus makes available, to a modern reader who no longer believes, a model of spiritual life that owes nothing to faith in the Christian sense. One can practise Marcus Aurelius's view from above without believing in Stoic providence; one can train oneself, with Epicurus, not to fear death, without believing in atoms. Spirituality becomes a practice, not a belief: it asks for no dogma, no Church, no sacrifice, but for work on oneself.

Hadot does not, however, stop at the self, and it is on this point that he opposed Michel Foucault, in an attested response. Foucault, who had read Hadot and made use of him in his lectures of the 1980s, turned Antiquity into the site of a “culture of the self”, a “care of the self”, in which the subject constitutes itself as a work. Hadot, in two texts written after Foucault's death, expressed both his esteem and his disagreement. He regrets that this death interrupted a dialogue, “dans lequel nous aurions pu sans doute profiter mutuellement de nos accords, mais aussi et surtout de nos désaccords”, in which we could no doubt have profited from each other's agreements, but also, and above all, from our disagreements. Testa sums up the disagreement in three points. Foucault recentres ancient spirituality on the self, whereas it aimed at a going beyond the self towards the universal, nature and the human community. He confuses Stoic joy, *gaudium*, which is found in the best part of oneself, with pleasure. Finally, he makes the culture of the self an aesthetics of existence, which risks, Hadot writes in the English version of his text, being “too aesthetic” and becoming “a new form of Dandysm”.

This disagreement is more than a quarrel among historians: it bears on the criterion. A spirituality that aims only at the construction of the self refounds no absolute, but it risks making the self itself the absolute: Stirner's Unique turned into a work of art. Hadot replies that there is something other than the self: the awareness of being part of the Whole, the cosmic feeling, the demand for justice towards other human beings. The question then shifts: this Whole to which the sage rises, this nature of which he feels himself a part, is it not a new name for the divine? The Stoics, for their part, said so without embarrassment: nature was God, the *logos* was Zeus. Hadot keeps the exercise while setting aside Stoic theology. One can, I believe, keep the one without the other, on one condition: that the Whole be not an object of adoration but a horizon of lucidity, and that the exercise demand not that one sacrifice oneself to it, but that one see more truly within it. This is what Spinoza already called knowing things under the aspect of eternity, and Hadot is, without being a Spinozist, one of the contemporary thinkers closest to Spinoza (a constructed comparison).

### Michel Foucault (1926-1984): philosophy and spirituality

Michel Foucault never wrote a book on religion, and one must not make him say more than he said. But his lecture course at the Collège de France in 1981-1982, published in 2001 under the title *The Hermeneutics of the Subject*, contains a definition of spirituality that is precise and useful to our question. In the lecture of 6 January 1982, he calls spirituality “la recherche, la pratique, l'expérience par lesquelles le sujet opère sur lui-même les transformations nécessaires pour avoir accès à la vérité”, the search, the practice, the experience through which the subject carries out on itself the transformations necessary for gaining access to the truth.

This definition has one merit: it says nothing about God. Spirituality here is neither belief in the supernatural, nor experience of the sacred, nor relation to a transcendent being, but a thesis on the relation between the subject and truth: truth does not give itself to the subject as he is; he must transform himself in order to gain access to it. Conversion, ascesis, purification, exercise are not ornaments of the philosophical life, but the very conditions of knowledge. In Antiquity, Foucault says, philosophy and spirituality were not separate: the Delphic precept “know thyself”, γνῶθι σεαυτόν (*gnôthi seauton*), was subordinate to another, more fundamental one, the care of the self, ἐπιμέλεια ἑαυτοῦ (*epimeleia heautou*). One could know the truth only by taking care of oneself, that is, by transforming oneself.

Then came what Foucault calls the “Cartesian moment”. I do not quote his formula, which I have not found on a reliable page; I paraphrase it. From Descartes onwards, access to truth comes through knowledge alone: a subject who reasons well and follows the method is enough to reach the truth, whatever the way he lives. The subject no longer has to be transformed, only to be correctly directed. Modern philosophy then separates from spirituality; it becomes knowledge, and spirituality, driven out of philosophy, takes refuge in religion, where it merges with faith. Foucault does not say that this moment is a catastrophe; he says that it took place, and that it made what the ancient philosophers were doing almost incomprehensible to us.

This framework sheds light on the debate of this essay. It explains why the modern critique of religion has almost always targeted religion as *belief*, as a set of true or false propositions: since the Cartesian moment, truth has been a matter of propositions. Spinoza is the exception: the *Ethics* is a book of geometrical demonstrations, but its title and its end are an ethics, that is, a transformation of life leading to beatitude, and Foucault could have seen in it the last great treatise in which philosophy and spirituality remain united. Nietzsche, for his part, never stops asking what truth does to the one who seeks it, and what type of human being the will to truth presupposes; against the Cartesian moment, he recovers the question of spirituality. These two comparisons are constructed confrontations; Foucault does not make them in the passages I have been able to read.

But Foucault's definition has a limit, which Hadot saw: centred on the subject, it says that he must transform himself in order to reach the truth, not what this truth is, nor whether it surpasses him. If truth is only a historical construction, as the Foucault of the 1970s tended to think, then spirituality risks closing in on the aesthetics of the self for which Hadot reproached it. If truth surpasses the subject, one must say how, and the question of the Whole returns. Foucault died in June 1984 without having settled this alternative, and the dialogue with Hadot broke off on this point.

### Jaspers (1883-1969): philosophical faith, or transcendence without revelation

Karl Jaspers came to philosophy through psychiatry. His *Allgemeine Psychopathologie* (*General Psychopathology*, 1913) is a classic of the discipline; the *Psychologie der Weltanschauungen* (*Psychology of Worldviews*, 1919) marks the transition; the three volumes of *Philosophie* (1932) form his major work. In 1910 he married Gertrud Mayer, from a devout Jewish family; he was removed from university administration after 1933, dismissed from his chair at Heidelberg in 1937, banned from publishing, and he learned indirectly that a plan to deport him, together with his wife, was in place in the spring of 1945. Settled in Basel in 1948, he gave there the lectures published under the title *Der philosophische Glaube* (*Philosophical Faith*, 1948), the book that brings him into this debate.

Hadot and Foucault proposed a spirituality of exercise, which asks one to believe in nothing. Jaspers answers the simplest question that the man without a Church asks today: can one *believe* without belonging, believe in something without believing in a dogma? His answer is yes, and it bears a deliberately paradoxical name, philosophical faith. Since Kant, faith and knowledge have divided the ground between them; since Kierkegaard, faith has been the opposite of philosophy; since Heidegger, a Christian philosophy has been a wooden iron. Jaspers maintains that there is a faith which is not that of the Church and which is nonetheless the very condition of philosophy.

To understand it, one must start from the limit situations, the *Grenzsituationen*, which the *Psychologie der Weltanschauungen* introduced and the *Philosophie* developed: death, suffering, struggle, guilt. One can neither change nor evade them, and in them consciousness meets its walls. The Stanford Encyclopedia describes them as moments, usually accompanied by dread, guilt or acute anxiety, in which the mind experiences the narrowness of its forms and becomes disposed to go beyond them. In his *Einführung in die Philosophie* (*Introduction to Philosophy*, 1950), Jaspers drew from them a genealogy of philosophy with three sources: wonder, which he takes from Plato and Aristotle, doubt, which he takes from Descartes, and the shaking of the human being before limit situations, which is his own contribution. The constructed debate that follows starts from the first; Jaspers holds it together with the third.

At the edge of its limits, consciousness does not find an object. Everything we know, we know in a relation of subject to object, within a horizon; and each horizon is itself enveloped by a vaster horizon, which never becomes an object. This ultimate envelope Jaspers calls *das Umgreifende*, the Encompassing, a notion he sets out above all in *Von der Wahrheit* (*On Truth*, 1947). Transcendence is not a supreme being, in the sense in which Heidegger reproached metaphysics for making it one; it is the Encompassing of all encompassings, what can be neither known nor denied. It gives itself only in *ciphers*, *Chiffern*: nature, the work of art, the religious symbol, metaphysics itself are ciphers of transcendence, which designate it by withdrawing. The peculiarity of the cipher is that it refuses knowledge at the very moment it points; and the attitude that answers it is not possession, but failure, *Scheitern*, in which consciousness experiences its absolute insufficiency.

This philosophy does not refute religions, it translates them. Dogmas, myths, rites are ciphers; they are not false, they become false when they are taken for knowledge. Philosophical faith therefore holds between two refusals: that of orthodoxy, which freezes the cipher into doctrine and demands obedience, and that of dogmatic atheism, which takes the refusal of dogma for a knowledge of the absence of all transcendence. The Stanford Encyclopedia sums up the notion in three senses: authentic philosophy is guided by a faith in the transcendent origin of existence; it cannot for all that abdicate rationality before revealed contents; and the certainties of faith remain paradoxical and uncertain, so that whoever seeks them must accept a philosophical relativism and the exchange of reasons. Jaspers adds to this framework a word that sets him apart from all the mystics of this essay: *Kommunikation*. The truth of philosophical faith is tested in discussion, and is lost as soon as it dispenses with it.

This programme underwent two great public tests, both attested. The first is the demythologization controversy. Rudolf Bultmann had proposed, as early as 1941, to free the kerygma of the New Testament from its mythical envelope, so as to keep its existential core. Jaspers was expected to approve; he attacked him in *Die Frage der Entmythologisierung* (*The Question of Demythologization*, 1954), the small book that gathers the documents of the debate. He reproaches him with a false rationalism, an arbitrary contempt for the spiritual experiences laid down in the myths of ancient times, and, under cover of liberalism, the imposition of a new orthodoxy that reduces all transcendence to a single scheme. The reversal is clear: the philosopher without a Church defends myth against the theologian. Myth, for him, is not an error of physics to be corrected, as it is for Lucretius or d'Holbach; it is a cipher, and to demythologize it is to reduce it to silence. The second test is the book of 1962, *Der philosophische Glaube angesichts der Offenbarung* (*Philosophical Faith in the Face of Revelation*). In it Jaspers rejects a revelation's claim to be exclusive, that is, to make a historical event, the Incarnation, the single truth binding on all. He comes to read the Trinity itself as a set of ciphers of human possibilities, and Christ as the cipher of the human possibility of inner turning; the Stanford Encyclopedia notes that this reading echoes Feuerbach's anthropological analysis. Karl Barth denounced in Jaspers a transcendence without any objective content. Barth was right about the fact and wrong in the judgement: the absence of objective content is precisely what Jaspers lays claim to.

The third item in the file, a historical one, gives Jaspers his place in an essay that seeks to move beyond Christianity alone. In *Vom Ursprung und Ziel der Geschichte* (*The Origin and Goal of History*, 1949), Jaspers proposes the notion of the axial period, *Achsenzeit*. Between roughly 800 and 200 before our era, and above all around 500 BCE, comparable spiritual events occur independently of one another in China, India, Iran, Palestine and Greece: Confucius and Laozi, the Upanishads and the Buddha, Zarathustra, the prophets of Israel, the Greek philosophers. Everywhere human beings become aware of being as a whole, of themselves and of their limits, and the categories with which we still think are born. The thesis runs counter to a Christian conception, which Hegel had taken up, according to which the appearance of Christ is the axis of universal history. For Jaspers, the axis is not a particular revelation, but a common breakthrough of humanity towards reflection and transcendence. If spirituality has a birthplace, it is neither Sinai nor Golgotha, but this constellation of five centres, none of which holds a monopoly. Philosophical faith is the modern form of this breakthrough, freed from all the orthodoxies that emerged from it.

It remains to let the adversaries speak. The sharpest is Camus, and the response is attested. In *The Myth of Sisyphus* (1942), Jaspers is cited from the first chapter, as one of those who recognized “l'impossibilité de constituer le monde en unité”, the impossibility of constituting the world as a unity, and Camus immediately adds: “combien pressés d'en sortir !”, how eager to get out of it! The chapter “Le suicide philosophique” (“Philosophical suicide”) makes him the textbook case: “Jaspers va nous fournir, poussé jusqu'à la caricature, un exemple type de cette attitude”, Jaspers will provide us, pushed to the point of caricature, with a typical example of this attitude. Camus then quotes a sentence of Jaspers: “L'échec ne montre-t-il pas, au-delà de toute explication et de toute interprétation possible, non le néant mais l'être de la transcendance”, does not failure reveal, beyond every possible explanation and interpretation, not nothingness but the being of transcendence? And he concludes: “Ainsi l'absurde devient dieu (dans le sens le plus large de ce mot) et cette impuissance à comprendre, l'être qui illumine tout. Rien n'amène en logique ce raisonnement. Je puis l'appeler un saut”, thus the absurd becomes god (in the broadest sense of the word) and this inability to understand becomes the being that illuminates everything. Nothing logically leads to this reasoning. I can call it a leap. Camus calls Jaspers “cet apôtre de la pensée humiliée”, this apostle of humiliated thought.

The objection hits home. Jaspers starts from the failure of reason, from *Scheitern*, and draws from it the being of transcendence; between the two there is no inference, only a trust. Camus, who refuses the leap, asks that one hold to the failure. But the objection leaves something intact. Jaspers's leap ends in no Church, no dogma, no commandment; the transcendence it reaches has no objective content, as Barth objected. Kierkegaard's leap ended in Christ, and in the obedience of Abraham; Jaspers's ends in a cipher, which it is forbidden to take for knowledge. Camus's objection therefore holds in logic, not with regard to the criterion: a leap that ends in no obedience refounds nothing.

With Heidegger, the second adversary, the debate is first of all biographical. The two men were friends in the 1920s, and they were long classed together under the label of the philosophy of existence, which both of them rejected. Heidegger had written, already in the years of their friendship, a critical review of the *Psychologie der Weltanschauungen*, in which he reproached Jaspers for remaining caught in the metaphysics of subjectivity; the break came with the commitment of 1933, which Jaspers, married to a Jewish woman, experienced as a personal threat; and, after the war, consulted by the Freiburg denazification commission, Jaspers recommended that Heidegger be suspended from teaching. On the substance, Heidegger awaits a god without saying which; Jaspers believes in a transcendence without saying which. The structure is similar, and the objection I addressed to Heidegger, that of an empty absolute available to whoever wishes to occupy it, could be turned against Jaspers. A single word protects him from it: communication. Heideggerian waiting is solitary, reserved for the thinker and the poet; Jaspersian faith is subject to the discussion of all with all, and it denies itself as soon as it claims to impose itself. That is why Jaspers, as early as 1946, published *Die Schuldfrage* (*The Question of German Guilt*) and addressed his compatriots publicly on the responsibility of each.

In the Groningen lectures published under the title *Vernunft und Existenz* (*Reason and Existence*, 1935), Jaspers placed Kierkegaard and Nietzsche at the threshold of contemporary philosophy, as two exceptions who shattered systematic reason and of whom no one can be the disciple. He wrote a *Nietzsche* (1936). The one is the believer, the other the impious one; Jaspers holds them together because both lived a limit situation through to the end. His own position is to remain between them, without a leap towards Christ and without proclaiming the death of God.

With regard to the criterion, Jaspers does not refound: his transcendence demands no sacrifice, no obedience, no belonging, and it forbids anyone to put it at the service of a historical cause. But neither does he leave, nor does he claim to. He deliberately keeps transcendence, as Spinoza kept the name of God, and so belongs to the family of exceptions who keep something of the religious knowingly; and he brings to it what Spinoza lacked, a positive recognition of the plurality of ciphers, Chinese, Indian, Jewish, Greek, Christian, which shifts the question of religion towards that of human spirituality. My reservation is Camus's, displaced: Jaspers's danger is not the leap, it is inflation. A faith that welcomes every cipher and holds on to none risks no longer saying anything, and the infinite tolerance of the Encompassing can become an elegant way of never deciding. Jaspers answered in advance through his practice: he decided, in 1933, in 1945, in 1946, each time history demanded it, and his philosophical faith was never confused with indifference.
