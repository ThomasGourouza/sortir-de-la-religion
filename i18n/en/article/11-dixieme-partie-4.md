### Levinas (1906-1995): ethics as an optics of the divine

With Emmanuel Levinas, the critique of onto-theology changes its source. Heidegger had diagnosed onto-theology and sought the way out in another thinking of being. Levinas, one of the first to introduce Husserl and Heidegger into France, starts from the same diagnosis and draws the opposite conclusion: it is not the forgetting of being that disfigured God, it is being itself. The God of the philosophical tradition was thought of as a supreme being, then as the being of beings; in both cases he was caught up in ontology, and that was his undoing. One must therefore look for a God uncontaminated by being, in the phrase of the foreword to *Of God Who Comes to Mind* (1982). The title of the great book of 1974, *Otherwise than Being or Beyond Essence*, states the programme: not to be otherwise, but otherwise than being.

This God is found neither in a mystical experience nor in a supernatural revelation, but in the most everyday relation there is, the relation to the other person. *Totality and Infinity* (1961) starts from a text we read in Part Three, Descartes's third *Meditation*. There Descartes discovers in himself an idea he could not have produced, the idea of the infinite, which exceeds him; and he concludes that there is in him, in a certain way, the perception of the infinite, that is, of God, before that of the finite, that is, of himself (the Latin sentence is quoted in Part Three). Levinas keeps the structure and changes the content. The idea of the infinite is a thought that thinks more than it thinks, a content that overflows its container. And this structure is encountered not first in the proof of God, but in the face of the other, which always exceeds the idea I form of it.

The face is the central notion, and it must be understood in the strictest sense: not a form one sees, but a nakedness that speaks. In a text of 1952, “Ethics and Spirit”, collected in *Difficult Freedom* (1963), Levinas writes that the face is inviolable, that these eyes, absolutely without protection, oppose possession with an absolute resistance, and that the temptation of murder is inscribed in this resistance; and he concludes: to see a face is already to hear “Tu ne tueras point”, “Thou shalt not kill”. The commandment is not added to the face by a law, it is the face itself; and it is only there that God can be thought: everything I can hear coming from God or going towards him, God who is invisible, must have come to me through this unique voice.

Hence the formula that condenses all of Levinas, and which is a precise answer to our question: “Ethics is an optic”. Ethics is not the corollary of the vision of God, it is that very vision, so that everything I know of God must find an ethical expression. Levinas adds an example that directly concerns Maimonides, whom we read in Part One: the knowledge of God we can have is expressed, according to Maimonides, in the form of negative attributes; it receives a positive meaning from the moral statement “God is merciful”, which means “be merciful like him”. The attributes of God are given not in the indicative, but in the imperative. A text of 1961, published in *L'Arche*, says it more sharply: ethics is an optics of the divine; no relation with God is direct or immediate; the divine can be manifested only through the neighbour; and for a Jew, incarnation is neither possible nor necessary.

Feuerbach, for whom the truth of theology is anthropology, added, in the *Grundsätze der Philosophie der Zukunft* (1843), that the unity of I and Thou is God (§ 60, quoted in Part Six), and that “die wahre Dialektik ist kein Monolog des einsamen Denkers mit sich selbst, sie ist ein Dialog zwischen Ich und Du”, true dialectic is not a monologue of the solitary thinker with himself, it is a dialogue between I and Thou (§ 62). Levinas seems to say the same thing, and says the opposite. For Feuerbach, the Thou refers me to humanity, to the species, to the common essence we share; the relation is symmetrical, and God dissolves into it. For Levinas, the face of the other refers me to a height I do not share, to an irreducible asymmetry: the other is not another self, he is situated, Levinas says, in a dimension of height, in the ideal, in the divine. Martin Buber, in *Ich und Du* (1923), had already thought of the relation as the place where the Eternal lets himself be encountered; Levinas always acknowledged this priority, and always reproached him for the reciprocity of the relation, which erases height. The confrontation with Feuerbach is constructed, the one with Buber attested in Levinas's work; both show that the same word, the Thou, can found an exit from religion or a new religion depending on whether it is thought of as symmetrical or asymmetrical.

This thought has a name in *Difficult Freedom*: “a religion for adults”, the title of an essay of 1957. There Levinas joins, unexpectedly, the most radical critiques this essay has encountered. Judaism, he writes, disenchanted the world, by contesting the idea that religions arose from enthusiasm and the sacred. Enthusiasm is possession by a god; the numinous, the sacred in Otto's sense, envelops and transports man beyond his powers, and true freedom is offended by this uncontrollable surplus. The sentence is uncompromising: the sacred that envelops and transports me is a violence. One recognizes Weber's vocabulary, and one hears a direct reply to Rudolf Otto: the *mysterium tremendum*, far from being the heart of religion, is what true religion must free itself from.

Levinas pushes the paradox to its limit. Monotheism, measured against the divine that the mythical gods embodied, is nothing but atheism. The destruction of the numinous sacred carries the risk of atheism, and this risk must be run: it is a great glory for the Creator to have set on its feet a being who affirms him after having contested and denied him. Monotheism surpasses and integrates atheism, but it is impossible unless one has reached the age of doubt, solitude and revolt. The critique of religion is therefore not the enemy of faith, but its condition. One also understands why Levinas, in the same text, finds Simone Weil as an adversary, since she sought in pagan myths the forms of the true God. The same essay rejects the Plotinian exit: against the philosophy that proclaims, with Plotinus, that the soul will go towards nothing other than itself, Judaism teaches a real transcendence, a relation with Him without whom the soul cannot stand. Plotinus, whom we read in Part One, is targeted here by name: this is an attested response.

In 1961, the year of *Totality and Infinity*, Levinas published a short occasional piece, “Heidegger, Gagarin and Us”, collected in *Difficult Freedom*. In it he targets the prestigious current of modern thought that came from Germany, which would have man rediscover the world, rootedness, landscape, place. Technology, on the contrary, tears us away from the Heideggerian world and from the superstitions of Place; what matters most in Gagarin's feat is that he left Place, and that for an hour a man existed outside any horizon. Judaism did not sublimate idols, it demanded that they be destroyed; like technology, it demystified the universe. The confrontation is attested, and of a violence that the urbanity of the tone does not hide: for Levinas, the sacred of place, of soil and of blood is the very essence of paganism, and the thinking of being is its last learned form. Levinas, a prisoner of war while his family was being murdered in Lithuania, had admired *Sein und Zeit* and seen its author join the Nazi party in 1933.

The criterion makes Levinas hard to classify. He seems to escape refoundation without difficulty: he installs neither Church, nor cult, nor dogma, and his religion for adults is defined precisely against the enthusiasm that carries the individual outside himself. But he demands something else, which resembles a sacrifice. In a radio commentary of 1955, “Loving the Torah More than God”, devoted to Zvi Kolitz's text attributed to Yossel Rakover, a fighter of the Warsaw ghetto, he writes that to love the Torah more than God means precisely to have access to a personal God against whom one can rebel, that is, for whom one can die. And *Otherwise than Being* pushes responsibility for the other as far as substitution, as far as the hostage: I am responsible for the other to the point of answering for his fault, without reciprocity.

Is this an absolute in whose name the sacrifice of the individual can be demanded? I do not think so, without being entirely sure. To die for a God against whom one rebels is a self-offering, which the criterion does not reach. The reason lies in the nature of this absolute: neither an idea, nor a nation, nor a class, nor a Church, but the singular face of the other human being, this one, now. The sacrifice the criterion denounces consists in immolating individuals to an abstraction; Levinasian responsibility consists in never sacrificing an individual to an abstraction, even that of the common good. One may judge this demand crushing, and I believe it is: an infinite responsibility, without reciprocity, is a burden no life can carry, and Levinas knows it, since he introduces the “third party”, justice and the State to measure it. But it does not refound a religion in the sense in which Comte founded one. Levinas offers a spirituality that has mourned the sacred, and that finds the divine in the prohibition of murder. It is, with Ricoeur, a demanding form of faith that has passed through critique; and it is, against Heidegger and against Marion, an exit from onto-theology that passes not through being but through the other.

### Marion (b. 1946): the idol and the icon

Jean-Luc Marion closes this part with a reversal. A Catholic philosopher, a phenomenologist, a pupil of Beaufret in the preparatory class for the École normale supérieure and of Derrida at the École itself, in the rue d'Ulm, a specialist in Descartes, he published in 1982 *God Without Being*, which takes the Heideggerian critique of onto-theology seriously and turns it in favour of faith. If the God of metaphysics is only an idol, faith has lost nothing by his death: it can at last think of God otherwise than as the supreme being.

The central distinction of the book is that of the idol and the icon. The idol is not a false god in the ordinary sense; it is a way of seeing. It stops the gaze and sends back to it its own measure, like an invisible mirror. The gaze that produces the idol is filled: it sees what it can see, it sees God at its own height. The icon, on the contrary, does not let itself be seen: it looks at the one who looks at it, and decentres him. Marion applies this distinction to concepts: “Quand une pensée philosophique énonce, de ce qu'elle nomme alors ‘Dieu’, un concept, ce concept fonctionne exactement comme une idole”, when a philosophical thought states a concept of what it then calls “God”, this concept functions exactly like an idol. The God of the philosophers is a conceptual idol, and Marion defines it in Heidegger's very terms: “l'idole conceptuelle a un site, la métaphysique, une fonction, la théologie dans l'onto-théo-logie, et une définition, la causa sui”, the conceptual idol has a site, metaphysics, a function, theology in onto-theo-logy, and a definition, the causa sui.

Marion nevertheless goes further than Heidegger, and it is here that the attested response takes shape. Heidegger, in denouncing onto-theology, did not free God from being; he only changed the order of subordination. For him, God can appear only within the horizon of being, as the divine that shows itself to meditative thinking; he remains subject to the question of being. Marion sees in this a second idolatry, subtler than the first. Hence his thesis: “Dieu ne se dit pas d'abord et à partir de l'Être”, God is not said first of all and on the basis of Being. God is said on the basis of love, of *agapè*, of the gift, which precedes being and cannot be reduced to it. According to an academic thesis, Marion even crosses out the word “God”, to signify that the name does not name a being.

The Zurich seminar here takes on its full significance. Marion relies on Heidegger's declaration that a theology, if he were to write one, should not contain the word “being”. He takes Heidegger at his word: since Heidegger himself admits that faith has no need of being, let us write the theology without being that he never wrote. The response is attested, and skilful: it turns against Heidegger the most sincere concession he ever made.

Marion has been challenged from two sides. The Thomists reproached him for making Thomas Aquinas an onto-theologian, whereas the Thomist *esse*, the act of being, is not a supreme being but the pure act that surpasses all essence; J.-D. Robert defended Thomas against Marion as early as 1983. Marion himself qualified his position in an article of 1995, “Saint Thomas d'Aquin et l'onto-théo-logie”, published in the *Revue thomiste*, often read as a partial retraction. On the other side, Heideggerian and unbelieving readers reproached him for remaining onto-theological in spite of himself: to think of God as love or as gift is still to give him a first name, a founding attribute, and therefore to place him at the summit of a hierarchy.

Marion proves, by example, that the critique of onto-theology is not a critique of faith, and that it can even serve it. This is what Barth had done with Feuerbach, Bonhoeffer with modern autonomy, Weil with the Marxist critique of consolation. Twentieth-century Christianity systematically turned the critiques of religion into purifications of faith, and this reversal is, I believe, both a victory and a capitulation. A victory, because faith thus purified escapes the objections of Feuerbach, Marx, Nietzsche and Heidegger: it no longer claims to know anything of God, it no longer consoles, it no longer founds anything. A capitulation, because to escape them it has had to give up almost everything that made it a religion: a knowledge, a consolation, a foundation, an institution. What remains is a spirituality, rigorous, demanding, but all the more exposed to the opposite objection: of being no more than the pious name of an experience that atheists such as Bataille, Camus or, as we shall see, Comte-Sponville, also claim.

### Assessment of Part Ten: the critique of religion changes sides

In the nineteenth century, the critique of religion was the work of unbelievers, and believers endured it. In the twentieth century, it is shared. Heidegger criticizes the God of the philosophers in the name of a divine god; Barth criticizes religion in the name of revelation; Bonhoeffer announces the end of religion in the name of Christ; Weil makes atheism a purification in the name of love; Levinas seeks the trace of God in the face of the other; Marion turns Heidegger around in the name of charity. Facing them, Wittgenstein keeps silent about what cannot be said, Bataille seeks ecstasy without God, Sartre takes atheism as his starting point, Camus refuses all divinity in the name of measure. And these two camps meet on one point: the refusal of the available God, the God who consoles, guarantees and founds. Heidegger writes that god-less thinking is perhaps closer to the divine god; Bonhoeffer, that the world become godless is perhaps closer to God; Weil, that the one who denies God is perhaps the closest to him. Three times the same “perhaps”, three times the same closeness of the godless and God, in three authors of whom it cannot be established that they read one another on this point. I do not claim that this convergence proves anything; I say that it redefines the question.

The question can no longer be: can one leave religion? It becomes: *what* does one leave when one leaves religion? If one leaves the God-as-foundation, the God-as-consolation, the stopgap God, the most demanding believers of the century left them too, and are none the worse for it. What remains common to both, an attention, an orientation, a way of standing before what cannot be possessed, is perhaps what can be called, in the strong sense, a spirituality. And the question shifts once more: is this common spirituality the truth of religion, at last freed from its idols, or its last refuge, the form in which religion survives its own critique?

It is also historical: what happened, in the real history of the West, when religion ceased to structure societies? The authors of the next part asked it as historians, jurists and sociologists, and they gave a name to what happened: secularization. But this name, far from closing the debate, reopened it. It is this trial of secularization that must now be conducted.
