### Ricoeur (1913-2005): critique passed through

Paul Ricoeur comes last, by design: he is the first reader of this part. The gesture that made him famous consists in looking together at three authors we have met separately, Marx, Nietzsche and Freud, and giving them a common name. Ricoeur places them in a single school, the school of suspicion, and tradition has retained, on the basis of his book, the phrase “maîtres du soupçon” (masters of suspicion). The book is *De l'interprétation. Essai sur Freud*, published by Seuil in 1965. His biographical position explains nothing but situates everything: Ricoeur is a Protestant, trained in the French Reformed tradition and a reader of Barth; his five years of captivity in Pomerania are years of reading, of Jaspers and of Husserl; he would later write the preface to the French translation of Bultmann, and the “Préface à Bultmann” (Preface to Bultmann) is reprinted in *Le Conflit des interprétations* (1969). A believer, then, who chose to read the hardest atheists not as adversaries to be refuted, but as masters whose school must be followed.

What do these three masters do? All three practise what Ricoeur calls interpretation as an exercise of suspicion. Their common gesture is to doubt, no longer the thing, as Descartes did, but consciousness itself: what consciousness believes it sees, believes, wills, is not what is at play within it. Religion, for Marx, expresses the world upside down; for Nietzsche, it expresses weakness under the name of virtue; for Freud, it expresses the child's desire under the name of Providence. In all three cases, the manifest meaning is the effect of a latent meaning that must be deciphered, and understanding goes by way of a detour. Ricoeur calls this common discipline a “science médiate du sens” (mediate science of meaning), which he opposes to the “conscience immédiate du sens” (immediate consciousness of meaning).

This grouping puts our own map to the test. We have distinguished three regimes where Ricoeur sees one school: the symptom in Marx, value in Nietzsche, illusion in Freud. Is he right to merge them? I believe he is right as to method and wrong as to object. As to method, the unity is real: all three treat belief as a doctored text whose hidden author must be found, the class, the weak will, the infantile desire. As to object, the distinction holds: Marx does not want to cure the individual, he wants to change the world that needs opium; Freud does not want to change the world, he wants the individual to stop being a child; Nietzsche wants neither, he wants new values to be created. Ricoeur's grouping shows what the three share against religion; our regimes show what separates them in the manner of leaving it, and that is where refoundation is decided.

But the heart of the book is a conflict. Opposite the hermeneutics of suspicion, he sets another hermeneutics, that of the restoration of meaning, whose model he finds in the phenomenology of religion, in Van der Leeuw and in Eliade: an interpretation that listens to the symbol instead of unmasking it, and that lets itself be told by it something it did not know. Ricoeur thus describes two opposed dispositions, a willingness to listen and a willingness to suspect. Both are legitimate, and neither can absorb the other. The sentence that condenses this conflict says that we are “ces hommes qui n'ont pas fini de faire mourir les idoles et qui commencent à peine d'entendre les symboles”, those men who have not finished putting the idols to death and who are barely beginning to hear the symbols.

This is where the notion that made Ricoeur's fortune comes in, though he rarely uses it: the “seconde naïveté” (second naïveté). It appears for the first time at the end of *La Symbolique du mal* (1960), in a concluding chapter whose title is already a thesis, “Le symbole donne à penser” (the symbol gives rise to thought). The starting diagnosis is the forgetting of the sacred peculiar to modernity: the first naïveté, that of the believer who lives in the symbol without reflecting on it, has become impossible for us. But Ricoeur refuses to conclude that the symbol is dead. Not having consulted the French text of 1960, I quote the English translation by Emerson Buchanan: “we can, we modern men, aim at a second naïveté in and through criticism”, and it is “by interpreting that we can hear again”; “beyond the desert of criticism, we wish to be called again”; and this second naïveté is meant to be the postcritical equivalent of the precritical hierophany.

What Ricoeur proposes, then, is a faith that has passed through critique, not a faith that has avoided it. The distinction bears directly on our question. Kierkegaard too wanted a faith that would owe nothing to historical criticism; Barth too wanted a faith that would not be a religion. But both placed themselves out of reach of suspicion, by the leap or by Revelation. Ricoeur agrees to remain exposed to it. He does not say to Freud: you are wrong about religion. He says to him: you are right about one religion, that of the protecting father and the judge, and it is precisely this religion that must die. In the lecture “Religion, athéisme, foi” (Religion, atheism, faith), reprinted in *Le Conflit des interprétations*, he distinguishes, I summarize without quoting, two cores of religion that modern atheism rightly destroys: the god of accusation, whom Nietzsche unmasked as an invention of ressentiment, and the god of consolation, whom Freud unmasked as a desire for protection. What remains after this double destruction, if anything remains, is neither the moral God nor the providential God. It is a word one listens to, a call that promises nothing.

Freud defined illusion by its motivation, the belief in which the fulfilment of a wish comes to the fore, and he loyally added that this does not settle its truth. Ricoeur takes Freud at his word: if the Freudian critique bears on the motivation of belief and not on its object, it does not close the question of the object, it purifies it. A belief that has renounced the protecting father, that has mourned consolation, escapes Freudian suspicion by definition, since the infantile wish no longer comes to the fore in it. Freud showed that the religion of the child is an illusion; he did not show, and did not claim to show, that there is no other. Ricoeur's reading of Freud is attested; the argument I draw from it here is a reconstruction, but it rests on the very letter of *The Future of an Illusion* and turns Freud's own loyalty against him.

The same reply can be made to the regime of the symptom. Marx said that religion is the protest against real distress at the same time as its expression; Ricoeur hears in the second half of the sentence what Marxism wanted to forget there. If religion is also a protest, then its critique cannot consist solely in dissolving it; it must also hear what it protests against. The hermeneutics of suspicion, pushed to the end, destroys the content of the symbol; the hermeneutics of listening gathers what the symbol said and suspicion could not hear. Our essay has practised this double gesture from the beginning without naming it: we have dismantled the refoundations, but we have also taken seriously what each of them was seeking.

There is, however, an objection, and it is a strong one. Is the second naïveté not a first naïveté that gives itself a good conscience? One passes through critique, says Ricoeur; but one comes out of it with the same symbols, fault, defilement, exile, promise, and reinvests them with a new meaning. Is this not what Hegel did, whom we reproached for translating religion into concept the better to preserve it? Is it not what Strauss did, who discovered in myth a truth of humanity? Ricoeur knows the objection, since he knows Hegel closely. His reply, I believe, comes down to one word: he does not claim that the second naïveté is a form of knowledge. Hegel completed religion in absolute knowledge; Ricoeur leaves it incomplete in interpretation, which has no end. The symbol gives rise to thought; it does not give rise to knowledge. And this difference is the one that separates, in our essay, refoundation from the exception.

For that is indeed where Ricoeur must be classed. He does not leave religion: he remained a believer, and he never hid that his faith preceded his philosophy, although he always took care not to bring it in as an argument. But neither does he refound a religion in the sense of the criterion. He installs no absolute in whose name the sacrifice of the individual could be demanded; on the contrary, he installs the individual in an infinite task of interpretation in which no absolute lets itself be possessed. Having entered the school of his adversaries, he never entirely left it: a rare position, which I believe more honest than that of most apologists as well as most critics. It has its price, which is instability: the second naïveté is always in danger of falling back into the first, or of dissolving into suspicion. Ricoeur accepts this, and this acceptance makes him, at the end of this part, a fair interlocutor of the three masters he named.

Ricoeur leaves a question for what follows. If the critique of religion can be passed through, if listening is possible after suspicion, then the exit from religion is not an event, but a path that can be travelled in both directions. Heidegger, Barth, Weil, Levinas, Marion all attempted, each in his or her own way, this return beyond critique. The question will each time be the same: is this return a second naïveté that knows what it is doing, or a refoundation that forgets it?

### Assessment of Part Eight: suspicion and experience

The landscape has changed profoundly. The sciences of religion have not confirmed the expectation of the Enlightenment, which hoped to see religion dissolve under the light of knowledge. Durkheim showed that one does not leave the sacred; Weber, that the gods return in the plural; Freud, that God is replaced by a god Logos; Otto, that the numinous is a category of the mind; James and Bergson, that mystical experience outlives all Churches; and Ricoeur gave this landscape its name, the school of suspicion, by showing that one can pass through it without going back or settling in it. The debate no longer bears on the existence of God, but on two new questions: that of the social function of the sacred, which the political religions of the twentieth century will make tragic, and that of spiritual experience, which theology and philosophy will each take up in its own way. These sciences make inevitable a question that Part Nine takes up in its own right: why do people believe? Part Ten will then open in this double context; Camus, in particular, will measure there what the divinization of history costs, akin to that sacralization of society which Durkheim had described without fearing it. The twentieth century thus begins with the discovery that man without God is not man without religion.
