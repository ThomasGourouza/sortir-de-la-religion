## Part Six: the Hegelian left and its adversaries

In 1840 Frederick William III of Prussia died; his son, Frederick William IV, a pietist romantic, brought Schelling to Berlin to combat “die Drachensaat des Hegelschen Pantheismus”, the dragon's seed of Hegelian pantheism. The Young Hegelians lost their posts, their journals were banned, and the critique of religion became for them the necessary form of political critique in a state that called itself Christian. This is the context of the German texts in this part, published between 1841 and 1845 in a climate of censorship and exile. At the same moment, in Copenhagen and in Paris, two authors who could not be more opposed, Kierkegaard and Comte, answered the same question by opposite routes, one by returning to faith against Christendom, the other by founding a religion without God. And in Frankfurt an old philosopher ignored for thirty years, Schopenhauer, proposed a mysticism without God that would shape Nietzsche's youth.

### Ludwig Feuerbach (1804-1872): the secret of theology

This whole debate pivots on Feuerbach, who must be followed at greater length than is usually done. He is reduced to a formula, theology is anthropology, and to a book, *Das Wesen des Christentums*. But his thinking about religion extends over twenty years, from 1830 to 1851, and it moves: it starts from death, passes through Christianity, takes on Hegel, defends itself against Stirner and ends in nature. At every stage our question returns: did the man who dissolved God into man abolish religion, or did he displace it?

The first book, published anonymously, cost its author his career. The *Gedanken über Tod und Unsterblichkeit* (*Thoughts on Death and Immortality*) appeared in Nuremberg in 1830. Feuerbach, then a *Privatdozent* at Erlangen, denies personal immortality in it: the individual is finite, and what is infinite in him, reason, love, spirit, does not belong to him as his own; death is the act by which the individual returns to the infinite what was not his. The author was quickly identified, and he would never be appointed professor. This starting point sheds light on everything else: the first object of Feuerbach's critique is not God but the beyond, and his first gesture is not to deny the infinite but to take it away from the individual and give it to the species. The young Hegelian of 1830 is still a pantheist of spirit, and it will take him ten years to make the species no longer Spirit, but man.

*Das Wesen des Christentums* (*The Essence of Christianity*) appeared in Leipzig, published by Otto Wigand, in 1841. It struck a whole generation as a liberation, and Engels still remembered it in 1886 in his *Ludwig Feuerbach und der Ausgang der klassischen deutschen Philosophie*: “Die Begeisterung war allgemein: Wir waren alle momentan Feuerbachianer”, the enthusiasm was general: we were all Feuerbachians for the moment. The words “for the moment” already tell what followed.

The thesis is stated in the preface to the first edition: the book means to show “daß das Geheimniß der Theologie die Anthropologie ist”, that the secret of theology is anthropology. The abridged formula of the textbooks, “die Theologie ist Anthropologie”, is not in the first edition in that form, and the difference is no small one: the word *Geheimnis* says that theology *hides* what it is and must be deciphered. George Eliot, in her 1854 translation made from the second German edition, keeps the word: “the secret of theology is anthropology”.

The deciphering rests on a theory of consciousness. Man differs from the animal because he is conscious not only of himself as an individual but of his species, his *Gattung*, that is, of the infinity of his essence: reason, will, love. This infinite essence the finite individual cannot recognize as his own: he projects it outside himself and worships it under the name of God. Hence the central formula: “Das Bewußtsein Gottes ist das Selbstbewußtsein des Menschen”, the consciousness of God is the self-consciousness of man. The mechanism is precise: man, and this for Feuerbach is the secret of religion, objectifies his essence, then makes himself the object of this objectified essence transformed into a subject; he thinks of himself as an object, but as the object of an object, of another being. All the divine predicates are human predicates raised to infinity: God is love, and it is human love that is worshipped without knowing it.

The book tests the thesis dogma by dogma, and it was this testing, more than the thesis, that made its success. The first part, entitled “Die Religion in ihrer Uebereinstimmung mit dem Wesen des Menschen”, religion in its agreement with the essence of man, reads each mystery as the secret of a human reality. The incarnation is the secret of love: God becomes man because love drives him to it, and the love that commands God is higher than he is, which Feuerbach writes with a boldness that announces the conclusion: “Die Liebe ist Gott selbst und außer ihr ist kein Gott”, love is God himself and apart from it there is no God. Prayer is the secret of the heart that knows no limits: “Das Gebet ist die Selbsttheilung des Menschen in zwei Wesen”, prayer is the self-division of man into two beings, a dialogue of man with himself, with his heart. The miracle is the secret of desire: “Das Wunder ist eine Creatio ex nihilo”, the miracle is a creation out of nothing, for whoever makes wine from water makes wine from nothing; and its truth is simple: “Aber was ist das Wunder? Ein realisirter supranaturalistischer Wunsch”, but what is the miracle? A supernaturalist wish fulfilled.

The second part reverses the lighting: “Die Religion in ihrem Widerspruch mit dem Wesen des Menschen”, religion in its contradiction with the essence of man. For the projection is not innocent: placed outside man, his essence escapes his criticism and turns against him. The central chapter, the last before the conclusion, is “Der Widerspruch von Glaube und Liebe”. Love, says Feuerbach, is universal and unites all men; faith is particular and separates those who believe from those who do not. “Der Glaube ist also wesentlich parteiisch”, faith is therefore essentially partisan; “Der Glaube kennt nur Feinde oder Freunde, keine Unparteilichkeit”; and the consequence is necessary: “Der Glaube ist wesentlich intolerant”, faith is essentially intolerant. The flames of hell, he adds, are only the sparks of the furious glance that faith casts on unbelievers. This chapter is the real point of the book: Feuerbach does not reproach Christianity for being false, but for betraying its own heart, love, for the sake of faith, from which love must be wrested.

The second edition, in 1843, adds a preface containing Feuerbach's most quoted sentence in the twentieth century, because Guy Debord made it the epigraph of *The Society of the Spectacle* (1967). I give it in Eliot's translation, made from that edition: the present age “prefers the sign to the thing signified, the copy to the original, fancy to reality, the appearance to the essence”; and for that age, the criticism that dispels illusion is a profanation, “for in these days illusion only is sacred, truth profane”. The sentence says that Feuerbach knew himself to be a profaner and held profanation to be a duty of truth.

Feuerbach owes to Strauss the transfer of the predicates to the species, but he does away with the Hegelian Spirit of which Strauss's humanity was the realization: there is only man, sensuous and natural. He owes to Schleiermacher the idea that religion has its seat in feeling, but where Schleiermacher saw the place where the infinite gives itself, he sees the place where man gives himself the infinite. The reduction Hegel feared is accomplished.

There remained Hegel, with whom Feuerbach settles accounts in two short texts that are perhaps more important than the great book. The *Vorläufige Thesen zur Reform der Philosophie* (*Provisional Theses for the Reform of Philosophy*), written in 1842 and published in 1843 in Switzerland, because of Prussian censorship, in Arnold Ruge's *Anekdota*, open with a sentence that extends the diagnosis of 1841: “Das Geheimnis der Theologie ist die Anthropologie, das Geheimnis aber der spekulativen Philosophie die Theologie”, the secret of theology is anthropology, but the secret of speculative philosophy is theology. Hegel did not go beyond religion, he rationalized it. “Die Hegelsche Philosophie ist der letzte Zufluchtsort, die letzte rationelle Stütze der Theologie”, Hegelian philosophy is the last refuge, the last rational support of theology, and Feuerbach adds with biting irony that, just as Catholic theologians once became Aristotelians to fight Protestantism, Protestant theologians will have to become Hegelians to fight atheism. Hence the method, which fits in one line: “Wir dürfen nur immer das Prädikat zum Subjekt, und so als Subjekt zum Objekt und Prinzip machen”, we need only ever make the predicate the subject, and thus, as subject, the object and the principle. Hegel says: Spirit thinks; Feuerbach: man thinks, and Spirit is only his thought. This is the inversion that Marx, thirty years later, would still claim in the afterword to *Capital*, by setting back on its feet a dialectic that was standing on its head.

The *Grundsätze der Philosophie der Zukunft* (*Principles of the Philosophy of the Future*), of 1843, give the positive version. The first paragraph states the meaning of the whole modern age: “Die Aufgabe der neueren Zeit war die Verwirklichung und Vermenschlichung Gottes”, the task of the modern age was the realization and humanization of God. The philosophy of the future will be sensualist: real being is sensuous being, and truth lies not in the thinking self but in the relation between two sensuous beings, the I and the Thou. “Der natürliche Standpunkt des Menschen, der Standpunkt der Unterscheidung in Ich und Du, Subjekt und Objekt ist der wahre, der absolute Standpunkt”. Here one recognizes, eighty years before Buber's *Ich und Du* (1923), the philosophy of dialogue; Buber acknowledged this debt, which I note without quoting it, not having checked the passage.

Paragraph 60 states plainly what the book of 1841 implied. “Einsamkeit ist Endlichkeit und Beschränktheit, Gemeinschaftlichkeit ist Freiheit und Unendlichkeit”, solitude is finitude and limitation, community is freedom and infinity; and Feuerbach concludes: man by himself is man, in the ordinary sense; man with man, “die Einheit von Ich und Du ist Gott”, the unity of I and Thou is God. This is not a metaphor. The *Theses* had said it of the new philosophy: it has made the attribute the substance, the predicate the subject, and thereby “die neue Philosophie ist die realisierte Idee, die Wahrheit des Christentums”, the new philosophy is the realized idea, the truth of Christianity; and Feuerbach specifies: “Aber eben weil sie das Wesen des Christentums in sich hat, gibt sie den Namen des Christentums auf”, but precisely because it has the essence of Christianity within it, it gives up the name of Christianity. This sentence is the centre of our question: Feuerbach does not claim to abolish Christianity, but to keep its essence and give up its name.

Criticism therefore has a positive task: not to destroy religion, but to *retranslate* it, to give back to man what he has alienated. The necessary turning point of history, says the last chapter of 1841, is the avowal “daß das Bewußtsein Gottes nichts andres ist als das Bewußtsein der Gattung”, that the consciousness of God is nothing other than the consciousness of the species. And he draws the practical rule from it: if the essence of man is the supreme being of man, then the first and highest law must be the love of man for man. “Homo homini Deus est”, man is a God to man, “dieß ist der oberste praktische Grundsatz”, this is the supreme practical principle, and it is the “Wendepunkt der Weltgeschichte”, the turning point of world history. The formula reverses the *homo homini lupus* of the Hobbesian tradition.

Hasty readers miss the essential point: Feuerbach does not merely say that human relations are good, he says that they are *holy*. The relations between child and parents, between spouses, between brothers, between friends, in a word moral relations, are “per se wahrhaft religiöse Verhältnisse”, in themselves truly religious relations; life is, in its essential relations, “durchaus göttlicher Natur”, of a wholly divine nature; it does not receive its consecration from the priest's blessing. Marriage, as the free union of love, is “durch sich selbst” holy. Religion is not abolished but moved from the church to the home, from heaven to the relation between I and Thou. Feuerbach does not desacralize the world, he resacralizes the whole of it.

There is a greatness in this undertaking that what came after has caused to be forgotten. Feuerbach is one of the first to have taken religion seriously *as human truth*: Spinoza put it in its place, Feuerbach listens to it, not as a web of errors but as the deepest expression of man, misunderstood by man himself. Hence, in *Das Wesen des Christentums*, pages of almost religious fervour on love, suffering, the incarnation: Feuerbach does not refute Christianity, he *realizes* it, and theologians have been able to read him as an ally. Karl Barth, in 1927, would declare him entirely right in his interpretation of *religion*, with one restriction: in so far as it bears on religion as the experience of evil and mortal man; and this would be the better to withdraw *revelation* from his critique (see Part Ten).

The first objection, which came almost at once, forced Feuerbach to define himself. In 1844 Stirner accused him, in *Der Einzige und sein Eigentum*, of having achieved no more than a theological liberation from theology: he had done away with the subject, God, but left intact the predicates, love, reason, Man, before which one must still kneel. Feuerbach replied in 1845, in *Wigands Vierteljahrsschrift*, with an article entitled “Über das *Wesen des Christentums* in Beziehung auf den *Einzigen und sein Eigentum*”. The response is attested, and stronger than is usually said.

First, on the predicates: to let them remain is not to let them remain divine. “Werden sie aus Gott in den Menschen versetzt, so verlieren sie eben den Charakter der Göttlichkeit”, transferred from God into man, they lose precisely the character of divinity. A political image: the majesty of the king rests only on the opinion that he is a being apart; let me see him as a man among others, and it vanishes; the same holds for heavenly majesty. Then he turns Stirner's own weapon against him: the Unique One declares that he has founded his cause on nothing, but nothing too is a predicate of God, as mysticism has said a hundred times. “So hat also der »Egoist« doch auch noch seine Sache auf Gott gestellt! So gehört also auch er noch zu den »frommen Atheisten!«”, so the egoist too has founded his cause on God after all! so he too still belongs among the pious atheists! Stirner's formula comes straight back in his face.

Next, on substance, the formula “man is the God of man” deifies nothing. “Der Satz: der Mensch ist der Gott, das höchste Wesen des Menschen, ist daher identisch mit dem Satz: es ist kein Gott, kein höchstes Wesen im Sinne der Theologie”, the proposition ‘man is the God, the supreme being of man’ is therefore identical with the proposition ‘there is no God, no supreme being in the sense of theology’; the first is only the positive, practical, religious expression of what the second expresses negatively. He had written just before, for anyone who might think to take him at his word: “Religion ist Affekt, ist Poesie; voilà tout”, religion is affect, it is poetry, that is all. Finally, he rejects the charge of having sacrificed the individual to the species, since his book makes the sensuous individual, who eats and drinks, the last word of the mystery, and he refuses the heavenly Unique One as well as the earthly one: “Schlage Dir den »Einzigen« im Himmel, aber schlage Dir auch den »Einzigen« auf Erden aus dem Kopfe!”, get the Unique One in heaven out of your head, but get the Unique One on earth out of your head too! The article closes on a self-definition that would become famous: Feuerbach is neither materialist, nor idealist, nor philosopher of identity; he is a man, or rather, since he places the essence of man in community, “Gemeinmensch, Kommunist”, a communal man, a communist. Stirner replied once more the same year, and the quarrel ended there.

Who prevails? Feuerbach's reply seems to me right on one point and weak on the other. Right when it shows that the Unique One, founded on nothing, rejoins negative theology: this is the objection I make to Stirner myself, and Feuerbach saw it as early as 1845. Weak when it claims that the proposition “man is God to man” is purely negative. If it were, why say it in the language of religion? Why call human relations “divine”, marriage “holy”, the unity of I and Thou “God”? It is, Feuerbach replies, the positive, practical, religious way of saying the same thing. But that is the whole question: whoever keeps only the positive expression of a negation keeps religion. His distinction between the name and the thing turns against him: he gives up the name of God, but keeps the thing, that is, worship.

The last works, the ones the textbooks ignore most, confirm this. In 1846 *Das Wesen der Religion* (*The Essence of Religion*) appeared; then, having given thirty public lectures in Heidelberg during the winter of 1848-1849, at the students' invitation and in the fever of revolution, Feuerbach published them in 1851 under the title *Vorlesungen über das Wesen der Religion*. The shift is twofold. Feuerbach widens the object: Christianity was only a religion of man, and there is an older religion, which worships nature. And he changes the principle of explanation, through an avowed borrowing from Schleiermacher. “Das Abhängigkeitsgefühl des Menschen ist der Grund der Religion; der Gegenstand dieses Abhängigkeitsgefühles, Das, wovon der Mensch abhängig ist und abhängig sich fühlt, ist aber ursprünglich nichts andres, als die Natur”, man's feeling of dependence is the ground of religion; but the object of this feeling, that on which man depends and feels himself dependent, is originally nothing other than nature.

Feuerbach takes up Schleiermacher's *schlechthinniges Abhängigkeitsgefühl* and himself recalls, in the *Vorlesungen*, Hegel's gibe: the expression has had a bad reputation among speculative philosophers ever since Hegel, against Schleiermacher, made the witticism that the dog too ought to have a religion, since it feels dependent on its master. He takes the side of feeling against the concept, but turns this feeling towards nature: what man depends on is not a personal God, it is air, water, light, earth, and religion is the affective and imaginary translation of this real relation. The response to Schleiermacher is attested: Feuerbach does not deny the phenomenon described, he shifts its object.

*Das Wesen der Religion* even touches, without knowing it, on the criterion of this essay. In paragraph 29 Feuerbach writes: “Im Opfer versinnlicht und concentrirt sich das ganze Wesen der Religion”, in sacrifice the whole essence of religion is made sensible and concentrated; the ground of sacrifice is the feeling of dependence, fear, uncertainty; but its result and its aim are self-feeling, courage, enjoyment. Feuerbach thus sees that religion is concentrated in sacrifice; he concludes that man sacrifices to reassure himself, I conclude that a religion is recognized by its demand that sacrifices be made to it. It remains to be seen whether his religion of humanity escapes this demand.

The last Heidelberg lecture answers unambiguously. Feuerbach calls on his listeners to will a better life rather than believe in one, and to will it together. But for this, he says, “müssen wir an die Stelle der Gottesliebe die Menschenliebe als die einzige, wahre Religion setzen”, we must put in place of the love of God the love of man as the only, the true religion, and in place of faith in God man's faith in himself, the faith that the destiny of humanity depends on humanity alone, that “der einzige Teufel des Menschen der Mensch[…], aber auch der einzige Gott des Menschen der Mensch selbst ist”, that the only devil of man is man, but also that the only God of man is man himself. Then, by way of farewell, the aim of all his teaching: to turn his listeners “aus Gottesfreunden zu Menschenfreunden, aus Gläubigen zu Denkern, aus Betern zu Arbeitern, aus Candidaten des Jenseits zu Studenten des Diesseits”, from friends of God into friends of men, from believers into thinkers, from people who pray into workers, from candidates for the beyond into students of the here and now. The first lecture had announced the same programme, in almost the same terms: to turn men from theologians into anthropologists, and from theophiles into philanthropists.

I admire this programme, but it must be read to the end: Feuerbach's last word is not that religion is an illusion to be dispelled, it is that the love of man is “the only, the true religion”. He himself supplied Engels with the material for his objection.

Engels formulated the objection in 1886, in what is in my view the best page of his book. Feuerbach, he writes, “will die Religion keineswegs abschaffen, er will sie vollenden. Die Philosophie selbst soll aufgehn in Religion”, by no means wants to abolish religion, he wants to complete it; philosophy itself is to be absorbed into religion. Engels describes the mechanism as I have done from the start: Feuerbach does not accept that love, friendship, compassion, sacrifice are worth something in themselves; he asserts that they reach their full value only if they are given a higher consecration by being called religion. “Sie sollen für voll gelten, erst wenn sie religiös abgestempelt sind”, they are to count fully only once they have been stamped as religious. Then comes an etymological remark that takes us back to the prologue: “Religion kommt her von religare und heißt ursprünglich Verbindung. Also ist jede Verbindung zweier Menschen eine Religion”, religion comes from *religare* and originally means bond; therefore every bond between two people is a religion. Engels mocks: “Solche etymologische Kunststücke bilden das letzte Auskunftsmittel der idealistischen Philosophie”, such etymological tricks are the last resort of idealist philosophy. To this he adds a historical argument: from 1793 to 1798 the Christian religion had so thoroughly disappeared from France that Napoleon had difficulty restoring it, without there appearing, in that interval, the slightest need for a substitute in Feuerbach's sense. And, more harshly, a social remark: in Feuerbach, “die Liebe ist überall und immer der Zaubergott, der bei Feuerbach über alle Schwierigkeiten des praktischen Lebens hinweghelfen soll”, love is everywhere and always the magic god who is supposed to help one over all the difficulties of practical life, and this in a society divided into classes with diametrically opposed interests.

The argument is very powerful. Engels does not reproach Feuerbach for loving human beings, but for needing to call this love “religion”. Refoundation does not lie in the content of what one loves, but in the need to *sacralize* it, which sharpens the criterion set out in the prologue: one refounds a religion not when one preserves values that religion carried, but when one feels the need to consecrate them as absolute and to give them the name of sacred. From here on two degrees must be distinguished: soft refoundation, which consecrates without demanding, and refoundation in the strict sense, which installs an absolute in whose name the sacrifice of the individual can be demanded, and criticism of which becomes sacrilege; self-offering freely consented to belongs to neither.

Must Feuerbach then be ranked among the refounders, alongside Rousseau and Comte? I believe so, with a nuance that the comparison brings out. Rousseau sacralizes the city, and his civil religion has dogmas, a Sovereign who fixes them, a penalty for whoever betrays them: it demands sacrifice and says so. Comte would sacralize Humanity, with a calendar, temples and a high priest. Feuerbach, for his part, sacralizes the relation: love, marriage, friendship, the unity of I and Thou. His religion has neither dogmas, nor priests, nor penalties, and demands of no one that he immolate himself: it belongs to soft refoundation, the need to consecrate, and not to refoundation in the strict sense, the absolute that can demand the sacrifice of the individual. It is the softest of refoundations, and that is why it has been so fertile: it nourished the “true socialism” that Engels mocked, the religion of humanity of George Eliot, who translated it and made it the morality of her novels, and the philosophy of dialogue in the twentieth century. But it also opened the way to less gentle refoundations: for if man is God to man, someone will end up asking which man, and in the name of which humanity. Stirner asked the question as early as 1844, and the answers of Marx, of Comte and of the political religions of the twentieth century would show that Man, once installed in the place of God, can demand as much as he did.

Feuerbach is thus the textbook case of the need to consecrate. It remains to hear Stirner's objection in its original form, the one given it in 1844 by a former regular of the “Freien”, the circle of Berlin Young Hegelians, with a brutality that Feuerbach's reply did not disarm.
