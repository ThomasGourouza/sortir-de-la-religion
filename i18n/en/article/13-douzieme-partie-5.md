### Islam before the exit from religion: Arkoun and Bidar

This essay has taken up the question of the exit from religion where it was formulated, in Christian and post-Christian Europe, and Gauchet gave that choice its justification: if Christianity is “la religion de la sortie de la religion”, the religion of the exit from religion, the question itself is Christian. Part One nonetheless showed that it had been raised elsewhere, and earlier: in Cordoba, Averroes maintained that the Law commands us to philosophize, and reserved for the learned an interpretation the multitude could not bear. What of it today, from within Islam? Two French thinkers of Muslim culture, very different in age and method, allow us to ask: Mohammed Arkoun, historian of Islamic thought, who sought to reopen what orthodoxy had closed, and Abdennour Bidar, philosopher, who sought to think an exit from religion proper to Islam. I have read none of their books, which are under copyright; I know them through studies and reviews that I name each time, and what follows must be read with that reservation.

Mohammed Arkoun (1928-2010), born in Kabylia, in the village of Taourit-Mimoun, trained in Algiers and then at the Sorbonne, where he prepared the agrégation in Arabic, taught the history of Islamic thought at the Sorbonne Nouvelle (Paris III). His work is contained in a programmatic title, *Pour une critique de la raison islamique* (1984), and in a project he named “islamologie appliquée”, applied Islamology. Classical Islamology, that of the Orientalists, reconstructed the history of Islamic civilization and offered a philological critique of its great texts. Arkoun wants, according to a phrase from *Humanisme et islam* (2005) reported by Leïla Tauil, to move the question of Islam from “son cadre polémique et théologique dogmatique vers l'espace ouvert de l'histoire et de l'anthropologie critiques”, its polemical and dogmatic theological framework towards the open space of critical history and anthropology. The same passage assigns to this discipline the task of making up for “aux oublis de la pensée islamique qui continue de refouler toute idée d'historicité dans la genèse et les fonctions concrètes de la croyance religieuse”, the omissions of Islamic thought, which continues to repress any idea of historicity in the genesis and concrete functions of religious belief. The aim is to submit the Qur'an, the prophetic tradition and the doctrinal discourses born of them to historical criticism, to linguistics, to the analysis of the conditions under which meaning is produced. *Lectures du Coran* (1982) applies this method to the text itself.

The heart of the undertaking is a history of closure, with a before and an after. Between the ninth and the eleventh centuries, the Islamic world knew an intense doctrinal pluralism: grammarians, jurists, philosophers, Sufis and rationalist theologians clashed there. The Mu'tazilites, supported by the caliph al-Ma'mun, defended free will and the thesis of the created Qur'an, that is, of a divine word inscribed in time and in a human language; Arkoun stresses “la portée humaniste de thèses comme celles du Coran créé et du libre arbitre (qadar)”, the humanist scope of theses such as those of the created Qur'an and of free will (qadar). Then power changed sides: al-Mutawakkil, according to Tauil, made the literalist doctrine of Ibn Hanbal the official doctrine of the state, and, at the beginning of the eleventh century, the caliph al-Qadir had read out in the mosques of Baghdad a profession of faith which, according to Tauil, ordered the execution of anyone who professed that the Qur'an is created. Arkoun calls this process the “clôture dogmatique”, the dogmatic closure. It produces not only a body of doctrine, but a limit to thought: it imposed, his commentator sums up, cognitive frontiers not to be crossed, and “tout un ‘impensable’ s'est constitué”, a whole “unthinkable” took shape. The discontinuities of Islamic history, the lost debates, the abandoned possibilities then disappear into what Arkoun calls the “sphère de l'impensé et de l'impensable”, the sphere of the unthought and the unthinkable. The distinction is subtle: the unthought is what has not yet been thought, the unthinkable what an orthodoxy has made impossible to think.

The vocabulary of this history comes largely from the French thought of its time, and the kinship sheds light on the project. Arkoun speaks of the passage from an “épistémè ‘médiévale’ à une épistémè moderne”, a “medieval” episteme to a modern episteme, and his commentator describes his method as an “archéologie des connaissances”, an archaeology of knowledge, of a deconstructive kind. Episteme and archaeology are Foucault's words, deconstruction Derrida's; and the question of a meaning that survives the critique of its conditions of production is the one Ricoeur put to the masters of suspicion. This comparison is mine: I have not consulted any text in which Arkoun discusses these authors, and I do not claim to establish a filiation. But it says the essential thing: the critique of Islamic reason is not the importation into Islam of Enlightenment atheism, that regime of error; it belongs rather to what Ricoeur called the school of suspicion, applied not to religion as such but to the orthodoxy that imposed itself in its name. Arkoun does not ask whether God exists, but how a tradition decided what could be thought of him. His Gifford Lectures, given in Edinburgh in 2001, concluded, according to the summary given on the Gifford Lectures website, that all aspects of Islamic knowledge and practice should be submitted to critical analysis.

Arkoun touches the criterion in two ways. First, the dogmatic closure he describes falls under it in the strict sense: a state profession of faith that declares lawful the blood of those who profess a theological thesis installs an absolute in whose name the sacrifice of the individual can be demanded, and al-Qadir's law joins the law of Plato's *Laws* and Rousseau's civil religion in the gallery of armed orthodoxies. Second, and this is the more interesting point, Arkoun does not propose to leave it by leaving Islam, but to reopen Islam to what it was before the closure: the pluralism of the ninth century, the falsafa, the Mu'tazilites, Averroes. He envisages an exit not from religion, but from closure. It resembles Feuerbach less than Spinoza reading Scripture as a historian, or Ricoeur seeking a faith that has passed through critique. It escapes, I believe, the sacrifice criterion, since it installs no new absolute; but it leaves Gauchet's question open. Does a religion restored to its historicity remain a religion in the strong sense, an institution of the social by a law that comes from elsewhere? Arkoun does not say, at least in what I have learned from his commentators, and the confrontation I construct between him and Gauchet remains open on this point.

Abdennour Bidar, b. 1971, philosopher and agrégé, takes the question head on, and this time the debate with Gauchet is attested. Between 2004 and 2012 he published five books that form a progression: *Un islam pour notre temps* (2004), *Self islam* (2006), *L'islam sans soumission* (2008), *L'islam face à la mort de Dieu* (2010), devoted to the Indian philosopher Muhammad Iqbal, and finally *Comment sortir de la religion* (2012), whose title almost overlaps with that of this essay. I know this last book only through this review, from which all the quotations that follow come. According to Demorgon, Bidar wants to leave religion, and therefore Islam, as well as atheism, in one and the same exit. And he expressly criticizes Gauchet's formula according to which Christianity would be the religion of the exit from religion. His objection is twofold. The Western exit has failed: “Deux siècles n'ont pas suffi pour évacuer du monde un religieux qui le fondait depuis des millénaires”, two centuries were not enough to clear from the world a religious element that had founded it for millennia; and it rests on the error of treating religious humanity as a humanity in its childhood, which adulthood ought to leave behind.

Hence the central thesis: religion must not be destroyed but replaced, and it can be replaced only by something equal to it. “L'effort pour maintenir en vie la religion relève de l'intuition qu'elle ne doit pas être détruite avant d'être réellement remplacée”, the effort to keep religion alive stems from the intuition that it must not be destroyed before it is really replaced; and again: “Renoncer aux dieux reste un sacrifice psychiquement insupportable pour l'humanité et un pas évolutif impossible à franchir si la compensation offerte n'est pas à la hauteur de ce dont il faut se séparer”, renouncing the gods remains a psychically unbearable sacrifice for humanity and an evolutionary step impossible to take if the compensation offered is not equal to what must be parted with. The exit Bidar calls for is therefore not against religion; it is a religious exit from religion, in the formula Demorgon uses to sum it up, which must pass through all the traditions, and which takes as its programme to bring to light “la sortie de la religion comme finalité du religieux”, the exit from religion as the purpose of the religious. Islam, in his view, has a resource of its own here. Bidar rereads the Qur'an from the figure of man as *khalîf*, God's lieutenant or heir on earth, and from the command given to the angels to prostrate themselves before Adam (Qur'an II, 34). The Islamic exit from religion would not be, as in Nietzsche, a murder of God, but a voluntary effacement of God in favour of the man to whom he has handed on his power.

I believe Bidar is right against a naive version of the exit, and that on this point he joins Durkheim and Hervieu-Léger: one does not leave a religious element that founds the social bond and the memory of a society as one corrects an error, and the formula of the unbearable sacrifice says what the proponents of subtraction underestimated. But his answer falls, I believe, under the criterion, in a way that readers of Feuerbach will recognize. An exit that consists in transferring to man the omnipotence of a God who effaces himself, and that makes this power the purpose of history, is Feuerbach's gesture of returning God's predicates to man, and it is also, pushed to the limit, Kirillov's dream. Demorgon reports, moreover, that Bidar speaks of a human “surpuissance”, a superpower, and that he sees in the inequalities of capitalism an obstacle to the “saut évolutif que l'espèce est en train d'effectuer”, the evolutionary leap the species is in the process of making. A humanity that is God's heir, engaged in an evolutionary leap, called to a kind of infinitization of its power, is an absolute, and it is not certain that it demands no sacrifice. This objection is a constructed confrontation, which I present with the caution imposed by a second-hand reading; the book itself may forestall it.

Raised from within Islam, then, the question takes two forms we have already met. Arkoun is on the side of Spinoza and Ricoeur: he does not leave the tradition, he leaves its closure, and he does so through history. Bidar is on the side of Feuerbach and Comte: he wants an exit that preserves the grandeur of what it leaves, and he risks, as they did, making a new religion of it. Neither fully confirms Gauchet's thesis: they show that critical thought is possible from within Islam, as in Cordoba in the twelfth century, not that it leads to the same place as the Christian exit. The question of the exit from religion is first of all Christian; it is not only Christian, and each tradition raises it with its own resources.

### Contemporary militant atheists: Onfray and the “new atheists”

A last atheism occupied public debate at the beginning of the twenty-first century without belonging to the same category as the preceding authors. Neither Michel Onfray (b. 1959) in France nor the “new atheists” of the English-speaking world are the peers of Spinoza or Nietzsche. But they form a useful test case: they show what becomes of the critique of religion when it ignores this whole path. Their books are under copyright; I quote from them only what is published by the publisher or by the author, and I indicate what is known only through third parties.

The *Traité d'athéologie. Physique de la métaphysique* (Grasset, 2005) belongs to the first regime, that of error, in the version of the militant Enlightenment. The back cover, signed by the author, sums up the thesis: “Les trois monothéismes, animés par une même pulsion de mort généalogique, partagent une série de mépris identiques : haine de la raison et de l'intelligence ; haine de la liberté ; haine de tous les livres au nom d'un seul ; haine de la vie ; haine de la sexualité, des femmes et du plaisir ; haine du féminin ; haine des corps, des désirs, des pulsions.”, the three monotheisms, driven by one and the same genealogical death drive, share a series of identical contempts: hatred of reason and intelligence; hatred of freedom; hatred of all books in the name of one alone; hatred of life; hatred of sexuality, of women and of pleasure; hatred of the feminine; hatred of bodies, of desires, of drives. Religion here is neither a misunderstood human truth, as in Feuerbach, nor a symptom, as in Marx, but a harmful lie. According to quotations reported by third parties, Onfray writes there that he despairs that believers “préfèrent les fictions apaisantes des enfants aux certitudes cruelles des adultes”, prefer the soothing fictions of children to the cruel certainties of adults (p. 27). Engels's objection, formulated in 1882, struck him in advance: one does not get rid of a religion that subdued the Roman Empire by declaring it a tissue of absurdities forged by impostors; explanation by hatred of life is hardly any better. A historian of antiquity, Jaime Alvar, entitled his review “Un tratado fracasado: la ateología como discurso del ateísmo cristiano”, a failed treatise: atheology as the discourse of Christian atheism. The word atheology Onfray takes from Bataille, reversing its meaning: where the *Somme athéologique* sought an experience of the sacred without God, the *Traité* critiques religion through knowledge, which is what Bataille fought against. The mythicist thesis, which the *Traité* sketches and *Théorie de Jésus* (2023) radicalizes, is examined in Part Two.

With *Décadence. Vie et mort du judéo-christianisme* (Flammarion, 2017), the regime changes. The book traces the history of Judeo-Christian civilization as that of an organism, birth, vigour, exhaustion, and its central thesis is that “c'est la religion qui produit une civilisation”, it is religion that produces a civilization (p. 575). Religion is no longer an error, but a power, and Onfray claims the neutrality of the diagnosis: “Mon propos n'est ni optimiste ni pessimiste, ni conservateur ni réactionnaire”, my purpose is neither optimistic nor pessimistic, neither conservative nor reactionary. Then the position shifts. In 2020, the magazine he founded, *Front populaire*, placed itself on the ground of the “défense de la civilisation judéo-chrétienne”, the defence of Judeo-Christian civilization. On 18 July 2021, he wrote in *Le Figaro*: “Je suis athée, on le sait, mais la vie de l'Église catholique m'intéresse parce qu'elle donne le pouls de notre civilisation judéo-chrétienne bien mal en point. Car si Dieu n'est pas de mon monde, mon monde est celui qu'a rendu possible le Dieu des chrétiens.”, I am an atheist, as is well known, but the life of the Catholic Church interests me because it takes the pulse of our Judeo-Christian civilization, which is in a very poor state. For if God is not of my world, my world is the one that the God of the Christians made possible.

The diagnosis of *Décadence* makes Onfray an inverted Marx. Marx wanted to abolish the need for opium by abolishing the misery that made it necessary; he thought religion would disappear of its own accord when the world no longer needed that sigh. *Décadence* observes that the weaning has taken place, that the West no longer believes, and this observation, far from being an emancipation, is called decline. Marx's prognosis is verified, his evaluation reversed: the end of religion is no longer the liberation of men, it is the death of a civilization. Gauchet's reading, which describes the same event as a fulfilment, seems to me more accurate; but Onfray has the merit of making visible what the madman of § 125 announced to the atheists of the marketplace: the death of God is not good news to be celebrated, it is an event whose weight we have not finished measuring.

This is usually seen as a contradiction. I see in it rather the passage from one regime to the other without the first ever being refuted. Religion remains false, and it is necessary. Now this conjunction has a name in our corpus: it is the position of the Grand Inquisitor, for whom faith is a lie men cannot do without. Onfray does not govern a Church, but he defends the Latin Mass without believing in God, in the name of what it holds together: in the same column, against the motu proprio *Traditionis custodes*, he makes it a “patrimoine liturgique”, a liturgical heritage. The irony is complete if one recalls the reproach he is said to have addressed to Comte-Sponville in 2005: he has in turn become a Christian atheist, but differently. Comte-Sponville keeps from Christianity an inner fidelity, a spirituality without a Church; Onfray keeps from it an outer civilization, a Church without spirituality. By the criterion, the first refounds nothing, since he demands no sacrifice; the second, by making the defence of a civilization the ground of his combat, refounds a collective absolute. It is a contemporary case of Stirner's law, not its culmination.

The new atheists of the English-speaking world offer its other side. Richard Dawkins (b. 1941), a biologist, published *The God Delusion* in 2006; Sam Harris (b. 1967), *The End of Faith* in 2004; Daniel Dennett (1942-2024), *Breaking the Spell* in 2006; Christopher Hitchens (1949-2011), *God Is Not Great* in 2007. I do not quote them; their common position is simple. Religion is a hypothesis about the world, the God hypothesis, false or indemonstrable, and harmful, since it justifies violence, hinders science and oppresses consciences. It must therefore be fought with reason and with science.

This current does not deserve the contempt sometimes shown it in Europe: it had the courage to say in public what many thought in private, in societies where unbelief remained suspect. Dennett, in particular, proposed an explanation of religion grounded in evolutionary biology, a serious research hypothesis. But for our question they bring nothing new: they return to the regime of error, in its poorest form, the one Spinoza had already gone beyond in 1670 by asking not whether superstition was false, but why men clung to it. They ignore Feuerbach, who had shown that religion speaks a human truth; Marx, who had shown that it is a symptom; Nietzsche, who had shown that science itself rests on a faith; Durkheim, who had shown that the sacred is social; Taylor, who would soon show that unbelief is not a subtraction. They refute the God hypothesis like a physical theory, which neither Feuerbach nor Nietzsche had judged sufficient, and they fall back, without knowing it, under the objection Stirner addressed to the atheists of 1844: they fight religion in the name of Reason and Science, essences to which the individual must submit, and their crusading tone has something pious about it. This comparison is constructed.

Onfray and the new atheists thus fail in two symmetrical ways. The latter remain in error; the former has left it by the high road, towards a civilization to be defended. The new atheists are the men of the marketplace of § 125, who did not believe in God and laughed at the madman: they are right about the existence of God, and have not understood what his death means. Onfray has understood it, and concluded that the Church must be kept without God.

### Towards the conclusion

The landscape has become clearer: there are those who refound deliberately, those who refound without meaning to and whom their successor unmasks, those who refute without understanding, and a few figures who seem to escape the trap because they knowingly keep something of what they criticize, without demanding of anyone that he sacrifice himself to it. It remains to be seen whether these exceptions form a position or only a family of attitudes, and whether they answer the question Marx's *Protestation* left open: what becomes of the complaint of the oppressed creature when it no longer has a heaven to address? That is for the conclusion to say.
