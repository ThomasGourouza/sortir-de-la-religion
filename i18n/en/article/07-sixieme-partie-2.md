### Max Stirner (1806-1856): Man is a ghost

*Der Einzige und sein Eigentum* (*The Ego and Its Own*) appeared from the same publisher as Feuerbach, Otto Wigand, in the autumn of 1844, dated 1845. Its author, Johann Caspar Schmidt, a teacher at a school for young ladies in Berlin, signed himself Max Stirner. The book opens and closes on the same line, borrowed from Goethe: “Ich hab' mein' Sach' auf Nichts gestellt”, I have set my cause on nothing. Everything lies in this framing: the Unique One rests on no cause that surpasses him, neither God, nor Man, nor Truth, nor the People.

The preface sets out the argument with an irony of great precision. Everyone wants me to serve a cause: the cause of God, of humanity, of truth, of freedom, of the fatherland. But God serves only his own cause, he is perfectly egoistic, and so is humanity. Hence the conclusion: “Gott und die Menschheit haben ihre Sache auf Nichts gestellt, auf nichts als auf Sich. Stelle Ich denn meine Sache gleichfalls auf Mich, der Ich so gut wie Gott das Nichts von allem Andern, der Ich mein Alles, der Ich der Einzige bin”, God and humanity have set their cause on nothing, on nothing but themselves. Let me then likewise set my cause on myself, I who am, just as much as God, the nothing of everything else, I who am my all, I who am the Unique One. And Stirner at once specifies that this nothing is not a void: “Ich bin Nichts im Sinne der Leerheit, sondern das schöpferische Nichts, das Nichts, aus welchem Ich selbst als Schöpfer Alles schaffe”, I am nothing, not in the sense of emptiness, but the creative nothing, the nothing out of which I myself, as creator, create everything.

Stirner's thought generalizes the critique of projection. Feuerbach had shown that God was the alienated human essence. Stirner shows that the human essence is *itself* an alienation. Every instance one places above oneself and to which one must sacrifice oneself is a ghost, a *Spuk*, and whoever believes in it has “einen Sparren zu viel”, a screw loose: “Mensch, es spukt in Deinem Kopfe; Du hast einen Sparren zu viel!” Political liberalism, socialism, the humanism of Feuerbach and of Bruno Bauer are stages of this haunting.

From the first pages of the first part, Stirner sets side by side the two formulas of the moment: “Der Mensch ist dem Menschen das höchste Wesen, sagt Feuerbach. / Der Mensch ist nun erst gefunden, sagt Bruno Bauer”. He concludes, in the second part, that humanism has merely changed the object of fear: “Menschenfurcht” is “bloß eine veränderte Gestalt der Gottesfurcht”, the fear of Man is only a modified form of the fear of God. And then comes the sentence that sums up the whole book and that I have taken as the thread of this essay: “Unsere Atheisten sind fromme Leute”, our atheists are pious people. The feudal analogy follows: in the feudal age, we held everything from God as a fief; in the liberal age, the same relation of vassalage exists with Man; God was the lord, now it is Man; God was the mediator, now it is Man; God was the Spirit, now it is Man. The rights of man themselves are a power received from a superior, which for that reason we call “right” and not “might”.

Stirner goes further still, to the point of turning the language of Christian demonology against humanism: “Der Mensch ist der letzte böse Geist oder Spuk, der täuschendste oder vertrauteste, der schlaueste Lügner mit ehrlicher Miene, der Vater der Lügen”, Man is the last evil spirit or ghost, the most deceptive or the most familiar, the craftiest liar with an honest face, the father of lies. The “father of lies” is the name of the devil in the Gospel of John (8, 44): Feuerbach's Man, the idol of the humanists, is the egoist's devil. And in its place Stirner claims what he calls an “Entheiligung”, a desecration without measure: “Nichts ist ihm heilig!”, nothing is sacred to him.

Stirner formulated as a programme a complete desecration, and he saw that all the critiques of religion of his time were “theologische Insurrectionen”, theological insurrections: revolts against God waged with the weapons and within the categories of theology. The diagnosis is astonishingly accurate.

The objection that strikes him comes from two sides, and it is a sign of his strength that his two main adversaries, Marx and Nietzsche, are also those who profited most from him. On Marx's side, *Die deutsche Ideologie* (written in 1845-1846 with Engels, published in 1932) devotes its longest section to “Saint Max”, a commentary of ferocious irony. The argument fits in a line: the Unique One is an abstraction, who believes he has freed himself from all essences but ignores the material conditions that make him what he is, and his “ego” is the ideological reflection of the isolated individual of commodity society. That the shock of Stirner was decisive in Marx's break with Feuerbach is a widespread interpretation; it is plausible, it is not proven.

On Nietzsche's side, the objection is more subterranean, and I believe it is deeper. Nietzsche may not have read Stirner, we have only indirect testimony of it, but his critique of the subject strikes the Unique One head on. The “I” is for him a grammatical fiction, supposed behind the action because language imposes a subject on every verb; the Unique One, a fixed point that consumes everything without being consumed, is still a substratum, a substance. Worse, Stirner's preface says so itself: the Ego rests on nothing “so gut wie Gott”, just as much as God. The Unique One has not abolished the place of God, he has settled into it. This is the objection Camus will take up in *The Rebel* (1951), one chapter of which is precisely devoted to “The Unique One”: revolt that refuses every limit ends by deifying itself (see Part Ten). Stirner stated the law of the whole series without escaping it himself.

### Karl Marx (1818-1883) and Friedrich Engels (1820-1895): religion as symptom

The introduction to *Zur Kritik der Hegelschen Rechtsphilosophie* (*Critique of Hegel's Philosophy of Right*) appeared in Paris in the *Deutsch-Französische Jahrbücher* of 1844. It opens with an observation that is at the same time a farewell: “Für Deutschland ist die Kritik der Religion im wesentlichen beendigt, und die Kritik der Religion ist die Voraussetzung aller Kritik”, for Germany, the critique of religion is essentially complete, and the critique of religion is the premise of all critique. Marx does not extend Feuerbach, he closes him: the work of translation is done; what remains is to ask why men needed this upside-down translation.

Marx's most famous text on religion is also the most badly quoted: only its end is remembered, read as an insult. Here it is in full: “Das religiöse Elend ist in einem der Ausdruck des wirklichen Elendes und in einem die Protestation gegen das wirkliche Elend. Die Religion ist der Seufzer der bedrängten Kreatur, das Gemüt einer herzlosen Welt, wie sie der Geist geistloser Zustände ist. Sie ist das Opium des Volkes.” Religious misery is at once the expression of real misery and the protest against real misery; religion is the sigh of the oppressed creature, the heart of a heartless world, as it is the spirit of spiritless conditions; it is the opium of the people.

Everything hangs on this word *Protestation*, which hurried readers forget: religion is not only a consolation that lulls, it is also a complaint that accuses. And opium, in 1844, is not first of all the drug of the underworld: it is the remedy, the painkiller. Marx does not despise the believer: he takes his pain and his revolt seriously, and says only that they mistake their object. Hence the consequence, which absolutely distinguishes the regime of the symptom from the regime of error: “Die Forderung, die Illusionen über seinen Zustand aufzugeben, ist die Forderung, einen Zustand aufzugeben, der der Illusionen bedarf”, to call on the people to give up their illusions about their condition is to call on them to give up a condition that requires illusions. One does not refute a symptom, one treats the disease. Marx has an image of great beauty to say so: criticism has plucked the imaginary flowers from the chain, “nicht damit der Mensch die phantasielose, trostlose Kette trage, sondern damit er die Kette abwerfe und die lebendige Blume breche”, not so that man may wear the chain without fantasy and without consolation, but so that he may cast off the chain and pluck the living flower. And again: “Die Religion ist nur die illusorische Sonne, die sich um den Menschen bewegt, solange er sich nicht um sich selbst bewegt”, religion is only the illusory sun that revolves around man as long as he does not revolve around himself. The programme follows: “Die Kritik des Himmels verwandelt sich damit in die Kritik der Erde, die Kritik der Religion in die Kritik des Rechts, die Kritik der Theologie in die Kritik der Politik”.

The image of the sun betrays something. Religion is the illusory sun; man must revolve around himself, around “seine wirkliche Sonne”, his real sun. The centre changes, but the structure remains: there is still a centre, and it is now man. Marx, in 1844, is still a Feuerbachian; he breaks a year later.

The *Thesen über Feuerbach* (*Theses on Feuerbach*), jotted down in the spring of 1845 and published by Engels in 1888 in a revised version (I quote here Marx's manuscript, MEW 3, p. 5-7), give this shift its theoretical form. The fourth thesis is its heart: Feuerbach dissolves the religious world into its secular basis, but the fact that this basis “sich von sich selbst abhebt und sich ein selbständiges Reich in den Wolken fixiert”, detaches itself from itself and establishes an independent realm in the clouds, “ist nur aus der Selbstzerrissenheit und Sichselbstwidersprechen dieser weltlichen Grundlage zu erklären”, can only be explained by the inner cleavage and self-contradiction of this secular basis. The sixth thesis turns Feuerbach's own concept of species against him: Feuerbach dissolves the religious essence into the human essence, but the human essence “ist kein dem einzelnen Individuum inwohnendes Abstraktum. In seiner Wirklichkeit ist es das ensemble der gesellschaftlichen Verhältnisse”, is no abstraction inherent in the single individual; in its reality, it is the ensemble of social relations. The seventh adds that the “religiöse Gemüt” is “selbst ein gesellschaftliches Produkt”. And the eleventh draws the conclusion for philosophy as a whole: “Die Philosophen haben die Welt nur verschieden interpretiert, es kömmt drauf an, sie zu verändern”.

The sixth thesis is Marx's attested response to Feuerbach, and it bears on the point isolated above. Feuerbach's Man, the species, the *Gattung*, is an abstraction, a mute “Allgemeinheit”, a generality that binds individuals together naturally. This is Stirner's objection turned around: for Stirner, Man is a ghost and only the Ego is real; for Marx, Man and the Ego are two abstractions, and only social relations are real. Marx took from Stirner his critique of Man in order to turn it against him, and one understands why *Die deutsche Ideologie* devotes so many pages to “Saint Max”: Stirner was the most dangerous adversary, because he had seen before Marx what Marx also saw.

Twenty years later, *Das Kapital* (1867) rediscovers the religious structure where one no longer expected it: to understand the fetishism of the commodity, “müssen wir in die Nebelregion der religiösen Welt flüchten”, we must take flight into the misty region of the religious world, where the products of the human head appear endowed with a life of their own. The first chapter goes further and establishes a correspondence between economic form and religious form. For a society of commodity producers, who relate their private labours to one another as abstract human labour, “ist das Christenthum, mit seinem Kultus des abstrakten Menschen, namentlich in seiner bürgerlichen Entwicklung, dem Protestantismus, Deismus u. s. w., die entsprechendste Religionsform”, Christianity, with its cult of abstract man, especially in its bourgeois development, Protestantism, Deism and so on, is the most fitting form of religion. The remark reaches far: the “cult of abstract man” is what Feuerbach proposed as the religion of the future, and Marx suggests that Feuerbachian humanism is not the exit from Christianity, but its purest bourgeois form. And he announces the condition of its real disappearance: “Der religiöse Wiederschein der wirklichen Welt kann nur verschwinden, sobald die Verhältnisse des praktischen Werkeltagslebens den Menschen tagtäglich durchsichtig vernünftige Beziehungen zu einander und zur Natur darstellen”, the religious reflection of the real world can only vanish when the relations of practical everyday life present to men, day after day, transparently rational relations with one another and with nature. The process of social life will strip off its “mystischen Nebelschleier”, its mystical veil of mist, only when it is the product of freely associated men, under their conscious and planned control.

Engels extends Marx in a direction that tends to be neglected. We have seen that, in connection with Bauer, he set aside the explanation by imposture (Part Five). In “Zur Geschichte des Urchristentums” (1894-1895), he goes further and likens early Christianity to the workers' movement: “Wie diese, war das Christentum im Ursprung eine Bewegung Unterdrückter: es trat zuerst auf als Religion der Sklaven und Freigelassenen, der Armen und Rechtlosen, der von Rom unterjochten oder zersprengten Völker”, like the latter, Christianity was originally a movement of the oppressed: it first appeared as the religion of slaves and freedmen, of the poor and those without rights, of the peoples subjugated or dispersed by Rome.

Hence the objection that Marxism invites, and that has been made to it a hundred times without always being well formulated. It is not that communism is “a religion” in the vague sense in which this is said of any enthusiasm; it is more precise. If religion is the protest of the oppressed against a misery whose cause he does not see, and if Engels himself recognizes in early Christianity a movement of the oppressed analogous to the workers' movement, then revolutionary emancipation inherits the *function* of religion. It promises the end of suffering, the reconciliation of man with his essence, a history that has a meaning and an end, a “transparent” society in which nothing will any longer be hidden. Marx can abolish the need for opium only by promising a world in which that need will have disappeared, and this promise has the form of salvation. This is the thesis Karl Löwith will develop in 1949 and Hans Blumenberg will contest in 1966; I discuss it in Part Eleven, because Blumenberg raises against it a serious objection that forces one to specify what it means to “inherit” from a religion.

Marx must be given his best defence. He does not deify humanity in Feuerbach's sense, since he precisely refuses abstract Man; he founds no cult, asks for no veneration, and mocks the “true socialists” who made a religion of love. The critique Engels addresses to Feuerbach holds against all sacralization. If Marxism produced cults, it was his heirs who produced them. I find this defence admissible as far as the texts are concerned, insufficient as far as the structure is concerned. The real sun of 1844 around which man must revolve, the transparent society of 1867, keep the form of an eschatological promise. Marx dismissed the sacred; he did not dismiss salvation.

### Søren Kierkegaard (1813-1855): Christendom against Christianity

While the Hegelian left criticizes religion in the name of man, a Dane criticizes it in the name of God. A contemporary of Feuerbach and Marx, Kierkegaard heard Schelling in Berlin in 1841-1842, in the same lecture halls as Bakunin and Engels, and he read Feuerbach. His conclusion, opposed to theirs, strangely joins their diagnosis: the Christianity of his time is not Christianity.

Two moments must be distinguished. The first is philosophical, and it culminates in the *Afsluttende uvidenskabelig Efterskrift til de philosophiske Smuler* (*Concluding Unscientific Postscript to Philosophical Fragments*, 1846), published under the pseudonym Johannes Climacus. Against Hegel, who claims to understand Christianity by sublating it into the concept, Kierkegaard maintains that Christianity is not a doctrine to be understood but an existence to be lived, and that Christian truth is not objective but subjective. The formula is famous: “Subjektiviteten er Sandheden”, subjectivity is truth. It does not mean that everyone has his own truth, but that religious truth is possessed only in the passionate relation of the existing individual to what surpasses him. Kierkegaard defines faith with paradoxical precision: “Tro er netop Modsigelsen mellem Inderlighedens uendelige Lidenskab og den objektive Uvished”, faith is precisely the contradiction between the infinite passion of inwardness and objective uncertainty. And again: inwardness is so great because it embraces objective uncertainty “med Uendelighedens hele Lidenskab”, with the whole passion of the infinite.

This is a radical response to Hegel: Christianity is a paradox that offends reason, and every attempt to understand it destroys it. It is also a response to Schleiermacher, faith being not a feeling for the universe but a passionate decision before a paradox. And it is a response to Feuerbach. Kierkegaard read *The Essence of Christianity* and, according to a study devoted to this reading, found in Feuerbach a paradoxically useful witness: in his papers of 1844, he judges him consistent, and illuminating by contrast; elsewhere, he says that he sets out Christianity so honestly that it is a pleasure to read him. It is also reported that he said Christianity needed more “traitors” of this kind; I have found this formula only in English translation in an anthology, without a Danish original, and I regard it as unsourced. The logic of this esteem is clear: Feuerbach saw that the Christianity of Christendom is a human religion, all too human; he was merely wrong to believe that it was Christianity.

The second moment, polemical, occupies the last months of Kierkegaard's life. From May to September 1855, he published at his own expense a series of pamphlets, *Øieblikket* (*The Moment*), in which he attacked the official Church of Denmark with unprecedented violence. The thesis is simple: Christendom has abolished Christianity. Kierkegaard says so in a parable about the cunning of mankind: unable to reject Christianity by force, it found a ruse, “vi ere alle Christne, saa er eo ipso Christendommen afskaffet”, we are all Christians, so Christianity is eo ipso abolished. And elsewhere: it is perhaps without analogy in history that a religion should have been abolished in this way, “ved at florere”, by flourishing; the religion of suffering has become the religion of the joy of living, “men har beholdt Navnet uforandret”, but has kept its name unchanged. Kierkegaard piles up the sarcasms: the two thousand churches of the kingdom are, Christianly speaking, a swindle, and so are the thousand priests in velvet and silk.

Kierkegaard's contribution comes down to this. He demonstrates from inside faith what Stirner demonstrated from outside, that established religion is not what it claims to be, but he reverses the conclusion. For Stirner, humanism is a religion in disguise; for Kierkegaard, established religion is a humanism in disguise, a “paganism” living under the name of Christianity: “og saa leve vi (under Navn af Christendom) Hedenskabet”, and so we live (under the name of Christianity) paganism. Both see a sacralization of the human under a divine name; the one concludes that all the sacred must be abolished, the other that true transcendence must be recovered. And Kierkegaard adds an idea that Barth and Bonhoeffer will take up in the twentieth century: authentic Christianity is inversely related to numbers; a single true Christian is enough for Christianity to exist, and when all have become Christians, the concept of Christian is abolished.

Kierkegaard is therefore a critic of religion, and one of the most radical, if by religion one means the institution, the socially reassuring cult, the piety that grounds order; he is not one if one means faith. His position takes up, against established Christendom, the exit *from above* that Pascal had opened: leaving religion in the name of a faith that religion betrays. We shall find it again in Barth (“Religion ist Unglaube”), in Bonhoeffer (a Christianity without religion), in Simone Weil (atheism as purification). But the objection is immediate, and Hegel would have made it: a faith defined by the infinite passion of inwardness before objective uncertainty has, no more than Schleiermacher's feeling, an internal criterion for distinguishing the true God from the idol. Kierkegaard knows it and accepts it: it is the risk of faith. But this risk makes his position a limit case, which one may admire without making it a general answer.
