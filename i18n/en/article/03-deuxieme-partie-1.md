## Part Two: the birth of Christianity, between Athens and Jerusalem

The origin of Christianity is not a matter for historians that lies outside our question. Every critique of the Christian religion presupposes a thesis about its birth: Feuerbach thinks it is the projection of the human essence, Nietzsche that it is the work of Paul, Strauss and Bauer that it is a myth, Engels that it is the uprising of the oppressed of the Empire, Onfray that it is a literary fiction. And every refoundation takes up something of this birth, since it was there that the bond was tied between a God of the Law, who came from Jerusalem, and a God of reason, who came from Athens. Tertullian, c. 200, gave the opposition its most famous form: “Quid ergo Athenis et Hierosolymis? quid academiae et ecclesiae?”, what then has Athens to do with Jerusalem, the Academy with the Church? (*De praescriptione haereticorum*, 7, 9). And he adds, against those who have produced “a Stoic, Platonic and dialectical Christianity” (“Stoicum et Platonicum et dialecticum christianismum”), that after Jesus Christ curiosity is no longer needed (*ibid.*, 7, 11-12). But the question is rhetorical in a sense he did not intend: Athens and Jerusalem already had a great deal in common. Nietzsche, seventeen centuries later, reversed the reproach while keeping it: “Christenthum ist Platonismus für's "Volk"”, Christianity is Platonism for the “people” (*Jenseits von Gut und Böse*, preface). The formula is polemical, but it points to a fact that historians do not dispute: Christianity thought itself in the categories of Greek philosophy, beginning with that of the Logos. We shall follow this passage from Philo of Alexandria, who reads Genesis with the *Timaeus*, to Augustine, who turns the God of the Platonists into a God to whom one prays, by way of Celsus and Porphyry, whose critiques, Platonist in character, already contained almost all the arguments of the Enlightenment.

### Philo of Alexandria (c. 20 BCE-c. 45/50 CE): Moses read with the *Timaeus*

Philo is a Jew of Alexandria, an exact contemporary of Jesus, born into one of the wealthiest families of the community, and in 39-40 a member of the embassy that pleaded the cause of his fellow Jews before the emperor Caligula. He writes in Greek, reads the Bible in the Greek translation of the Septuagint, and comments on it in an immense body of work, almost entirely allegorical. Nothing suggests that he ever heard of Jesus, and none of his treatises mentions the Christian movement. Yet he supplied nascent Christianity with the tool that allowed it to think its God as a philosopher would.

His project comes down to one assertion, set out at the beginning of the *De opificio mundi*, his treatise on the creation of the world: Moses is a philosopher, and indeed the first. Moses, he writes, had reached the summits of philosophy very early, and had learned from the oracles of God the most numerous and most important principles of nature (*De opificio mundi*, 8). Genesis and the *Timaeus* therefore say the same thing, and when they seem to diverge, it is because the letter of Genesis hides a meaning that allegory must uncover.

The heart of the doctrine is the Logos. Reading Genesis with the *Timaeus* requires solving a difficulty that the *Timaeus* itself leaves open: how can the transcendent God, who has nothing in common with matter, shape the world? Plato placed the Ideas, eternal models, between the demiurge and the world. Philo places them in the mind of God, and he calls this mind in act the Logos. If one wished to speak plainly, he says, one would not say that the intelligible world is anything other than the Logos of God already engaged in creating the world, just as the intelligible city is nothing other than the thought of the architect who plans to build it; and he immediately adds that this is the doctrine of Moses, not his own (*De opificio mundi*, 24-25). The Logos is thus at once the place of the Ideas, the instrument of creation and the model of man, since man is made “in the image of God”. Philo goes so far as to call it, in a passage of the *Questions on Genesis* whose Greek original is lost but which Eusebius of Caesarea preserved, the “second God”: nothing mortal could be made in the likeness of the supreme God and Father of the universe, but only in the likeness of the second God, who is his Logos (*Quaestiones in Genesim* II, 62, quoted by Eusebius, *Preparation for the Gospel* VII, 13).

A Jew faithful to the Law, who recited every day that God is one, thinks the world on the basis of a divine intermediary who is not God himself and whom he nonetheless names “second God”. In Philo this is not a person, still less a man: the Logos is a function of divine thought, which Philo describes now as a power, now as an archangel, now as the high priest of the world, without ever fixing the metaphor. But a few decades later the prologue of the Gospel of John would write: “Ἐν ἀρχῇ ἦν ὁ λόγος, καὶ ὁ λόγος ἦν πρὸς τὸν θεόν, καὶ θεὸς ἦν ὁ λόγος”, in the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God (Jn 1, 1), and then what Philo could never have written: “Καὶ ὁ λόγος σὰρξ ἐγένετο”, and the Word became flesh (Jn 1, 14). Whether or not Philo directly influenced John, a disputed question, the concept was available, and it was through it that Christianity entered philosophy.

The allegorical method is the common ancestor of almost all the religious hermeneutics of the West. Philo starts from a principle he holds to be self-evident: God has neither hands, nor anger, nor repentance, and when Scripture ascribes them to him, it speaks the language of men in order to be understood by them. Every narrative therefore has two senses, the literal, which serves the multitude, and the figurative, addressed to those who know how to read. The garden of Eden becomes virtue, the patriarchs become dispositions of the soul, the exodus from Egypt becomes the soul's departure from the body. Philo does not for all that despise the letter: he expressly reproaches the radical allegorists of his time for neglecting the observance of the Law on the pretext of having understood its meaning. The two levels of reading are thus a way of holding together the community and philosophy, the practice of all and the contemplation of a few. One recognizes the division that Averroes would theorize eleven centuries later.

Philo also left, in the *De vita contemplativa*, the description of a community of men and women, the Therapeutae, who lived near Lake Mareotis in prayer, the study of the Scriptures and song, withdrawn from the city. Reality or ideal, it is the first image of a spiritual life that is neither the worship of the Temple nor the school of the philosophers, but a form of life governed by contemplation. The Christian monks of Egypt, two centuries later, recognized their ancestors in it, and Eusebius even believed that the Therapeutae were Christians. The confusion is instructive: the form of spiritual life preceded the doctrine that would appropriate it.

The most instructive fact of his history is his posterity: rabbinic Judaism did not hand him down; the tradition that kept him is Christian. It was Christians of Alexandria, Clement and then Origen, who read him, quoted him, imitated him and copied his manuscripts; it was Christians, such as Eusebius, who preserved for us what we have lost of his Greek text. This is the consensus of historians. Judaism, which was to rebuild itself after the destruction of the Temple in 70 around the Law and its commentary, had no need of a Logos; Christianity, which had to explain how a crucified man could be the son of a single God, needed one vitally. Philo supplied a religion that was not his own with the philosophy it was looking for.

Philo belongs to the regime of allegory, prior to all the regimes of critique: he does not criticize religion, he translates it into philosophy. This translation has two faces: it shields the Law from the rationalist objection, but it installs in biblical religion a philosopher's God, without passion and without a face. Philo is thus, before Christianity, the inventor of what Heidegger would call onto-theology, applied to the Bible. His allegorical reading also has a price, which Spinoza would make Maimonides, his distant heir, pay (Part One): if one can always read beneath the letter the meaning that reason demands, reason no longer learns anything from Scripture. With regard to the criterion, finally, Philo refounds nothing and demands no new sacrifice; he makes Greek philosophy religious, and biblical religion philosophical.

### Paul of Tarsus (d. c. 64/67): Christ crucified, folly to the Greeks

If Philo supplied the concept, Paul supplied the scandal. Still, one must know which Paul one is speaking of. Of the thirteen letters the New Testament attributes to him, historical criticism holds only seven to be authentic: the First Letter to the Thessalonians, the Letter to the Galatians, the two Letters to the Corinthians, the Letter to the Romans, the Letter to the Philippians and the note to Philemon. This is the dominant interpretation, almost a consensus; the others are called deutero-Pauline, written in his school after his death. These seven letters, composed between about 50 and 60, are the oldest Christian texts we have, earlier than all the Gospels. The Acts of the Apostles, which tell his life, are later by at least a generation, composed by the author of the Gospel of Luke with an ancient historian's freedom that calls for caution.

Paul presents himself as a Jew, and even as an elite Jew. He reminds the Galatians of what his conduct had been “in Judaism”, how he persecuted the Church, and how he “advanced in Judaism beyond many of [his] own age” (Ga 1, 13-14). He does not say that he left Judaism for a new religion; he says that it pleased God “to reveal his Son in me, that I might proclaim him among the nations” (Ga 1, 16). Scholarship in the second half of the twentieth century insisted on this point: the “new perspective on Paul”, inaugurated by E. P. Sanders (*Paul and Palestinian Judaism*, 1977), challenged the Lutheran image of a Paul who set Christian grace against a Judaism of legal performance: the Judaism of his time, Sanders shows, was already a religion of the covenant received by grace, and Paul does not leave Judaism, he opens its borders to the nations. Without settling this debate among exegetes, we may note that the presumed founder of Christianity did not think of himself as the founder of a religion.

What he founds, however, is unheard of, and he knows it. The First Letter to the Corinthians contains the sentence that sums up the encounter of Athens and Jerusalem, and makes it impossible: “ἐπειδὴ καὶ Ἰουδαῖοι σημεῖα αἰτοῦσιν καὶ Ἕλληνες σοφίαν ζητοῦσιν, ἡμεῖς δὲ κηρύσσομεν Χριστὸν ἐσταυρωμένον, Ἰουδαίοις μὲν σκάνδαλον, ἔθνεσιν δὲ μωρίαν”, Jews demand signs and Greeks seek wisdom, but we proclaim Christ crucified, a scandal to Jews and folly to the Gentiles (1 Co 1, 22-23). Paul does not offer a wisdom better than that of the philosophers; he offers a folly, a God who manifests himself in the punishment reserved for slaves. Philo's Logos was the place of the Ideas; Paul's Christ is a condemned man.

Acts nevertheless tells of an attempt at reconciliation, the scene in which Athens and Jerusalem physically meet. Paul, in Athens, debates with Epicurean and Stoic philosophers, who take him to the Areopagus. There he delivers a speech that starts from an altar “to an unknown god”, quotes a Greek poet, “for we are also his offspring”, and declares that God “does not dwell in temples made with hands”; then he announces the judgement and the resurrection of a man, and at that point, says the narrative, “some mocked, and others said: We will hear you again about this” (Ac 17, 22-32). This speech is, according to the dominant interpretation among exegetes, a composition by Luke rather than a transcript, but it rightly shows where the frontier lies. A creator God who does not dwell in temples, the Stoics could understand, and Plato had almost said as much; it is the resurrection of a body that makes the Areopagus laugh. Christianity can speak Greek as long as it speaks of God; it ceases to be Greek as soon as it speaks of the flesh.

The oldest testimony to the content of the faith is in Paul, and it is a received account rather than a doctrine: the formula of 1 Co 15, 3-5, which we shall meet again in connection with the existence of Jesus, hands down the death of Christ for our sins according to the Scriptures, his burial, his resurrection on the third day and his appearances to Cephas, then to the twelve. Paul writes c. 54; he says he is handing on what he received, probably in Damascus or Jerusalem, in the thirties. And in the Letter to the Galatians he mentions his meeting with Cephas, that is, Peter, and with James, the brother of the Lord (Ga 1, 19). This verse will weigh heavily in the debate on the historical existence of Jesus.

Two features of Paul's thought matter for our debate. The first is his universalism. “There is neither Jew nor Greek, there is neither slave nor free, there is no male and female; for you are all one in Christ Jesus” (Ga 3, 28). The sentence does not promise the abolition of slavery or the civil equality of the sexes, and elsewhere Paul sends the slave back to his master; it declares that these distinctions no longer count before God. That is little from the point of view of politics, and it is immense from the point of view of ancient religion, which was always the religion of a people, a city or a station in life. The second feature is his expectation of the end. The oldest letters assume that Christ will return within the lifetime of those to whom they are addressed. The dominant interpretation among historians draws from this a consequence that theologians long refused: Pauline Christianity is first of all an eschatology, an expectation of the imminent end of the world, and not a morality or a metaphysics. Albert Schweitzer, at the beginning of the twentieth century, would make it the key to all research on Jesus.

Engels, who had read Bruno Bauer closely, drew from these two features an analogy that has enjoyed lasting success. “Die Geschichte des Urchristentums bietet merkwürdige Berührungspunkte mit der modernen Arbeiterbewegung”, the history of early Christianity offers curious points of contact with the modern labour movement: like it, Christianity was originally a movement of the oppressed, and both proclaim an imminent deliverance from bondage and misery (*Zur Geschichte des Urchristentums*, 1894). Engels adds that Christianity places this deliverance in the beyond and socialism in this world. The analogy is illuminating and dangerous: it illuminates the sociology of the first communities, and announces without meaning to what Löwith would reproach Marxism with, namely that it is a transposed eschatology. Celsus, as we shall see, was already saying with contempt what Engels says with sympathy.

Did Paul invent Christianity? That is Nietzsche's thesis, for whom Paul is the bearer of bad tidings (Part Seven): Paul “verlegte einfach das Schwergewicht jenes ganzen Daseins hinter dies Dasein”, simply shifted the centre of gravity of that whole existence beyond this existence, into the lie of the risen Jesus, and his need was power (*Der Antichrist*, § 42). Renan, in his *Saint Paul* (1869), had already set the Galilean Jesus of the parables against the theologian of Tarsus, with less violence and more melancholy. The comparison is constructed, since Nietzsche does not quote Renan on this point; the two readings converge on a Jesus without doctrine and a Paul without Jesus.

I believe this thesis is half right. Right, in that Paul made a theology out of an execution, and that the letters take almost no interest in the life of Jesus, in his words, in his deeds: all that counts is the cross and what follows it. Wrong, in that it makes Paul a solitary inventor. Paul says he “received” the formula of 1 Co 15, he keeps company with Peter and James, he quarrels with them over whether converted Gentiles must observe the Law; he joins a movement that existed before him and already believed Jesus risen. And Nietzsche, like Renan, is unaware of what Sanders has shown: for Paul the cross is not the rejection of Judaism, it is its fulfilment for the nations. With regard to the criterion, finally, a phrase from the Letter to the Romans deserves attention: “I appeal to you therefore, brothers, by the mercies of God, to present your bodies as a living sacrifice, holy and acceptable to God, which is your reasonable worship” (Rm 12, 1). Blood sacrifice is replaced by self-offering, and this worship is called *logikê*, in keeping with the Logos. Paul spiritualizes sacrifice instead of abolishing it. It is an immense advance over the burnt offerings, and it is at the same time the birth certificate of a religion that will be able to demand that the individual offer himself entirely.

### Justin Martyr (c. 100-c. 165): the Logos sown in all human beings

Born in Samaria into a pagan family, Justin sought the truth among the philosophers before finding it, he says, among the prophets. Converted c. 130, he opened a school in Rome where he taught Christianity as a philosophy, wearing the philosopher's cloak, and he died a martyr there c. 165. His two *Apologies*, addressed c. 150-155 to the emperor Antoninus and to the senate, are the first systematic effort to present the Christian faith to the Greco-Roman world in its own language. They rest on an idea that Philo had prepared and that Justin carries to its conclusion: the Logos that became flesh in Jesus was already present, in part, in all human beings.

The thesis is stated with a clarity that is still astonishing. Christ, Justin writes, is the Logos in which the whole human race has had a share, and those who lived according to the Logos are Christians, even if they were held to be atheists: among the Greeks, Socrates and Heraclitus and those like them; among the barbarians, Abraham, Elijah and many others (*1 Apology*, 46). The word “atheists” carries all the weight here. Christians were accused of atheism because they refused the gods of the city; Socrates had been accused for the same reason, and the trial of Socrates becomes the precedent for Christian martyrdom. Justin says so expressly in the *Second Apology*: Socrates was accused of the same crimes as we are, of introducing new divinities and of not recognizing the gods the city recognized (*2 Apology*, 10). This is the very crime that the law of the *Laws* punished: at its birth, Christianity presents itself as the religion of the impious according to Plato and of the just according to Socrates.

The same chapter contains a quotation over which Celsus and Origen would argue. Justin writes that Socrates urged men to come to know the God unknown to them through the inquiry of reason, saying that it is neither easy to find the Father and maker of all things nor safe, having found him, to declare him to all (*2 Apology*, 10). This is the *Timaeus*, which Justin attributes to Socrates: “τὸν μὲν οὖν ποιητὴν καὶ πατέρα τοῦδε τοῦ παντὸς εὑρεῖν τε ἔργον καὶ εὑρόντα εἰς πάντας ἀδύνατον λέγειν”, to find the maker and father of this universe is a task, and having found him, it is impossible to declare him to all (*Timaeus* 28c). For Justin, Christ did what Plato declared impossible: the Father, inaccessible to the philosophers, made himself known to all.

The doctrine that gave its name to Justin's thought is that of the Logos *spermatikos*, the seminal reason. Each of the philosophers, he writes, spoke well insofar as he had a share in the seminal Logos, seeing what was akin to it; and whatever has been well said among all men belongs to us Christians (*2 Apology*, 13). The formula is double, and its two faces have had two opposite posterities. On the one hand, it is an opening of rare generosity: truth is not the preserve of the baptized, it is sown in all, and one can be a Christian without knowing it. On the other, it is an annexation: everything that is true in others is ours. Justin adds that he professes Christianity not because Plato's teachings are foreign to those of Christ, but because they are not in every respect similar to them (*ibid.*). Plato saw a part; Christ is the whole.

Justin also wrote, c. 160, a *Dialogue with Trypho*, in which he discusses the interpretation of the Scriptures with a cultivated Jew. The book opens with the account of his conversion: he had gone through the schools, Stoic, Peripatetic, Pythagorean, and had settled on Platonism, which promised him the sight of God, when an old man met by the seashore showed him that the prophets knew more than the philosophers. The account is no doubt stylized, but it says the essential: Christianity is not the opposite of philosophy, it is its fulfilment, the only one that keeps its promises. Hegel would take up this scheme by inverting it, since for him it is philosophy that fulfils religion by raising it to the concept; the comparison, which is constructed, shows that the question of which fulfils the other has never ceased to be asked.

The *Dialogue with Trypho* also has a dark side: it is one of the first texts in which separation from Judaism becomes a polemic against it: the Law is presented there as a discipline imposed on the Jews because of the hardness of their hearts, and the Church as the true Israel. The doctrine of the seminal Logos, so welcoming towards the Greeks, is much less so towards the Jews, whom it reproaches for having had the Word before their eyes without recognizing it. Christian universalism thus begins with an exclusion, and the long history of Christian anti-Judaism finds one of its roots there.

An argument that now seems naive has had a long history. Justin asserts that Plato borrowed from Moses his doctrine of creation from formless matter, and even that the passage of the *Timaeus* in which the world soul is arranged “in the shape of a chi”, crossed within the universe, comes from the bronze serpent that Moses raised in the desert, read by Plato as a cross he did not understand (*1 Apology*, 59-60). This is the thesis of the “theft of the Greeks”, taken up by Clement of Alexandria and by Eusebius: Greek wisdom is later than Moses and derived from him. Historically false, it answers a polemical necessity: in a world where antiquity confers authority, a religion born under Tiberius had to prove that it was older than Plato.

Justin made Christianity a philosophy, and it is as a philosopher that he dies, refusing to sacrifice to the gods of the Empire. Martyrdom here is not the sacrificial absolute that the criterion denounces: it is the Roman state, not Justin's doctrine, that demands the sacrifice, and Justin dies for having refused an act of worship. But the doctrine of the seminal Logos contains in germ the other danger: if everything that is true belongs to Christians, those who deny it are not merely in error, they are withholding what does not belong to them. Justin's openness and the intolerance of the following centuries come out of the same sentence.
