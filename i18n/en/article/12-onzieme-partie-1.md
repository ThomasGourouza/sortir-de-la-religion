## Part Eleven: secularization on trial

Marion left us before a paradox: a theologian, in order to free God from metaphysics, takes up the very gesture of the modern critique of religion and denounces the God of the philosophers as an idol. The critique of the idol thus crosses the border between believers and unbelievers, and that border is perhaps not where we thought it was. Until now, we have asked each author whether he had left religion or refounded one. Yet this question assumes that we know what it means, for a modern idea, to *come* from religion. To say that Comte refounds a cult, that Marx inherits eschatology, that Heidegger awaits a god, is to assert a filiation; and the filiation between Christianity and modernity is the subject of one of the great controversies of the twentieth century, the secularization debate. Between 1922 and 1976 it set against one another a Catholic jurist, a philosopher of Jewish origin, baptized a Protestant and returned to Stoic scepticism, and a philosopher who made modernity the opposite of a debt. This debate puts my thesis on trial: if Blumenberg is right, most of the “refoundations” this essay believed it saw are optical illusions.

### Carl Schmitt (1888-1985): political theology as the truth of modernity

*Politische Theologie. Vier Kapitel zur Lehre von der Souveränität* (*Political Theology: Four Chapters on the Concept of Sovereignty*) appeared in 1922, then in a second edition in 1934. Its third chapter opens with a sentence that became the watchword of the whole discussion: “Alle prägnanten Begriffe der modernen Staatslehre sind säkularisierte theologische Begriffe”, all significant concepts of the modern theory of the state are secularized theological concepts.

The sentence can be read at two levels, which Schmitt holds together. The first is historical: the concepts have migrated. The omnipotent God of theology became the omnipotent lawgiver of the modern state; sovereignty, a divine attribute, passed to the prince, then to the people. The second, the one that interests Schmitt, is systematic: political concepts do not merely have the same *origin* as theological concepts, they have the same *structure*, and one understands a political epoch only by understanding the metaphysical image of the world it gives itself. The definition that opens the book, according to which sovereign is he who decides on the state of exception, finds its foundation here. The exception, which suspends the legal order, occupies in jurisprudence the place that the miracle occupies in theology; I do not quote Schmitt's sentence, which I have not read on a reliable page, but the analogy is the heart of the chapter. Hence the parallel that, in his view, organizes all of modern history: deism, which excludes the miracle and makes God the clockmaker of a world governed by its own laws, corresponds to the liberal state under the rule of law, which excludes the exception and claims that the norm suffices for everything. The theology of the Counter-Revolution, that of Bonald, de Maistre and above all Donoso Cortés, saw what liberalism refused to see: that a decider is needed, and that the decision cannot be deduced from any norm.

People often mistake Schmitt's tone. They read his formula as a denunciation in the manner of Feuerbach: modern politics would be nothing but disguised theology, and it would have to be unmasked. Hebekus has shown that the reverse is true. Schmitt does not reproach modernity for having stolen its concepts from theology, but for having forgotten it, and he wants the politics of decision to raise itself, of its own accord, to the theological height from which it descends. Secularization is not for him an illegitimacy to be corrected, it is a truth to be embraced, and decisionism is the politics that embraces it.

Schmitt is thus, with Rousseau and Comte, one of the few authors in the corpus who *claim* refoundation; he is the only one to claim it as the truth of modernity as a whole. The others mostly undergo it without knowing it, and it is the next critic who reveals it to them: Stirner to Feuerbach, Marx to Stirner, Heidegger to Nietzsche. Schmitt, for his part, wants it. The sovereign who decides on the exception is a mortal god, and Schmitt, who wrote its genealogy, knows it. That this jurist put his talent at the service of the National Socialist regime in 1933, and that in 1934 he justified the murders of the “Night of the Long Knives” in an article whose title made the Führer the protector of the law, “Der Führer schützt das Recht”, is not a biographical accident unconnected with the thesis: it is what becomes of a political theology that sacralizes decision without saying in the name of which God. I write this without polemical pleasure: Schmitt is the limit case of refoundation, the one in which the sacred returns without even the mask of Humanity, in the sole form of authority.

### Karl Löwith (1897-1973): the philosophy of history as disguised eschatology

Karl Löwith, a student of Heidegger before becoming one of his harshest critics, driven out of Germany by the racial laws, taught in Japan and then in the United States. It was there that he published *Meaning in History. The Theological Implications of the Philosophy of History* (Chicago, 1949), translated and reworked in German under the title *Weltgeschichte und Heilsgeschehen* (*World History and Salvation History*, 1953). The book goes back in time: it starts from Burckhardt and moves backwards through Marx, Hegel, Proudhon, Comte, Condorcet and Turgot, Voltaire, Vico, Bossuet, Joachim of Fiore, Augustine and Orosius, as far as the Bible.

The thesis is simple. The idea that universal history has a knowable meaning, directed towards a fulfilment, is neither Greek nor natural. The Greeks thought of time as a cycle; they did not seek the meaning of history, but the *logos* of the cosmos. Oriented history comes from Hebrew and Christian faith, from the expectation of a final fulfilment, the eschaton, which gives meaning to everything that precedes it. The modern philosophies of history, of progress as of revolution, were born when this schema was transposed into immanence: fulfilment is no longer expected from God at the end of time, it is produced by man within time. The philosophy of history is Christian eschatology minus God: it keeps the form, it changes the subject.

The chapter on Marx is the most famous. Löwith reads the *Manifesto* there as a prophetic document disguised as science: the proletariat is the chosen people, the revolution is the judgement, the classless society is the kingdom, and the whole of history is a history of salvation transposed into economics. The formula is plain: “It is the old Jewish messianism and prophetism”, Löwith continues, speaking of Marx. This is the objection I addressed to Marx in Part Six: a revolution that promises the end of suffering and the meaning of history takes the form of salvation. Löwith formulated it as early as 1949, with an erudition I do not claim to equal.

The chapter on Comte, less often cited, also sheds light on Part Six. Löwith sees in it the same transposition, in a more naive and more explicit form. The law of the three stages, which moves humanity from the theological age to the metaphysical age and then to the positive age, is a history of salvation in three ages, and Löwith recalls that it has a precise medieval ancestor: the division of history into three reigns, that of the Father, of the Son and of the Spirit, in Joachim of Fiore, the twelfth-century Calabrian abbot whom the Franciscan Spirituals had made the prophet of a new age. Positivism, which believed it had dismissed theology, reproduced its most daring schema, the advent of a third age on earth. And Comte, unlike Marx, drew the consequence himself by founding the Religion of Humanity, with its Great Being, its sacraments and its calendar. Comte's refoundation was therefore not an eccentricity of old age, as his scientistic disciples claimed, but the logical outcome of the law of the three stages: whoever believes that history is heading towards a fulfilment must sooner or later celebrate its cult. The link between Comte and Löwith's thesis is attested by the very structure of the book; the remark on religious foundation as a necessary consequence is mine.

But Löwith's conclusion is not the one we expect. He asks neither that we return to the Christian faith, the original of which modernity would be the degraded copy, nor that we complete secularization by purifying progress of its theological remnants. The Christian faith, according to him, was never a historical expectation: the Christian awaits the kingdom, he does not await it *from history*. And critical reason, for its part, finds no meaning in history: “To the critical mind, neither a providential design nor a natural law of progressive development is discernible in the tragic human comedy of all times.” What Löwith proposes, at the end of the book, is a withdrawal: the Greek view of nature, the Stoic wisdom that accepts the world without asking it for meaning. Habermas, as early as 1963, gave this gesture its critical name in an article in *Merkur*: “Karl Löwiths stoischer Rückzug vom historischen Bewusstsein”, Karl Löwith's Stoic withdrawal from historical consciousness.

Löwith is thus an ambiguous ally. He gives my thesis its best historical support: the philosophies of history refound a religion of salvation without God, and that is why they were able to demand sacrifices that only a religion obtains. But his conclusion joins, by another path, the exceptions we have identified. His Stoicism is an exit without refoundation: it promises nothing, sacralizes nothing, demands of the individual no sacrifice to the future. It may be compared with Spinoza, and with the *amor fati* of Nietzsche, whom he knew closely, since in 1935 he had devoted a book to the Nietzschean philosophy of eternal recurrence, *Nietzsches Philosophie der ewigen Wiederkunft des Gleichen*. It remains to be seen whether one can live in this withdrawal. Blumenberg, Taylor and Habermas will answer, each in his own way, that modernity does not live there.

>
> The second epilogue of *War and Peace* is no longer a novel: it is a treatise on history, which denies great men and Providence the power to explain it, and which ends with an analogy with Copernicus. Just as we had to renounce the immobility of the earth that we feel in order to admit a movement that we do not feel, “in the present case it is similarly necessary to renounce a freedom that does not exist, and to recognize a dependence of which we are not conscious”.
>

But Tolstoy draws from it a dependence that Löwith called Stoic and that the essay judges to be without a way of life; both have dismissed eschatology, and both remain before a necessity that must, after all, be inhabited.
>
> Flaubert, the same year, wrote the other version of the disenchantment of history. *Sentimental Education* passes through 1848 with Frédéric Moreau, who watches the sack of the Tuileries, the shooting and the coup d'état as he watches his life, without entering into it; the novel ends with two friends agreeing that the best thing they ever had was a botched visit to a brothel, twenty years earlier. This is 1848 as farce, the essay says with Marx; it is above all the end of all historical expectation, and Flaubert's novel does what Löwith's treatise observes: history no longer has a meaning, and neither do the characters.
>

### Hans Blumenberg (1920-1996): the legitimacy of the modern age

One of the two serious objections announced in the prologue did not come from a believer. It came from Hans Blumenberg, in *Die Legitimität der Neuzeit* (*The Legitimacy of the Modern Age*, 1966), a book he reworked in depth between 1973 and 1976 in response to his critics, and whose first part, devoted to secularization, is a methodical refutation of Löwith and Schmitt. I owe this objection its best form, for it forces me to make the thesis more precise.

Blumenberg starts from a semantic observation. The word “secularization” comes from law: it first designates the passing of Church property into lay hands, in particular during the confiscations of the Napoleonic era. To say that progress is secularized eschatology is therefore to use a concept that implies an *expropriation*: a property that legitimately belonged to someone has been taken from them. Blumenberg shows that secularization, so understood, functions as a “Kategorie des geschichtlichen Unrechts”, a category of historical injustice; in the 1962 lecture that prepared the book, he already spoke of a “Kategorie historischer Illegitimität”, a category of historical illegitimacy, a formula that gives the lecture its subtitle. Whoever says “secularized” insinuates that modernity lives on stolen property and is not legitimate in its own right.

For this reproach to hold, Blumenberg continues, three conditions would have to be met: the transferred property would have to be identifiable as the same property before and after; the first owner would have to have been its legitimate possessor; the transfer would have to have been unilateral. Yet none is met in Löwith's case. Christian eschatology awaits an end that breaks into history from outside, that judges it and interrupts it; modern progress is an immanent principle, realized in history through the accumulation of human knowledge and power. These two contents are not the same thing in two garbs: they are heterogeneous. What creates the illusion of their identity, Blumenberg states in a sentence I give in full, for it contains his whole thesis: “die historische Identität und methodische Identifizierbarkeit der als säkularisiert vermeinten Vorstellungen ist ein Schein, der entsteht durch die Identität der Funktion, die ganz heterogene Gehalte an bestimmten Stellen des Systems der Welt- und Selbstdeutung annehmen können”, the historical identity and methodological identifiability of the ideas supposed to be secularized are an appearance, which arises from the identity of the function that quite heterogeneous contents can assume at certain places in the system of interpretation of the world and of the self.

Here is the decisive concept: *Umbesetzung*, reoccupation. Christianity opened certain questions, it hollowed out certain places in the interpretation man gives himself of himself and of the world: the question of the meaning of history as a whole, that of the totality of the real, that of an end. When Christianity ceased to answer them credibly, the questions remained, and modernity was summoned to answer them in its turn, with contents that had nothing Christian about them. Progress is not disguised eschatology; it is the modern answer to a question that eschatology had posed, and which it had to take up, so to speak, in spite of itself. The identity of the place has been mistaken for the identity of the occupant. Blumenberg even sees here the origin of the excesses of the philosophies of history: they overloaded the concept of progress because they believed themselves bound to answer an immoderate question, that of the totality of history, which modernity had not posed itself.

As for the legitimacy of modernity, it does not come from an inheritance, but from a *Selbstbehauptung*, a self-assertion. Blumenberg dates it precisely: it is man's response to the theological absolutism of the late Middle Ages, that of nominalism, which had made God an arbitrary and all-powerful will, before which the world no longer offered any guarantee of order. Against a hidden and unpredictable God, man had to take charge of himself, to know and master nature in order to find his bearings in it. Modernity is not a Christianity that has lost its faith; it is a response to an internal crisis of Christian theology, a response that owes it nothing other than its provocation.

The exchanges are attested. Blumenberg built his criteria on the example of Löwith. Löwith answered him with a review, dated 1968 in his *Sämtliche Schriften* (vol. II, pp. 452-459); in it, according to Weidner, he reproaches Blumenberg for demanding a substantial identity as proof of secularization while denying that such an identity could ever exist, that is, for imposing on his adversary an impossible burden of proof. Schmitt, for his part, replied in 1970 in the last chapter of *Politische Theologie II*, reproaching Blumenberg for already presupposing, in his demonstration, the standpoint of the modernity he claims to legitimize. Blumenberg replied in the revised edition: Schmitt's formula is in his eyes “die stärkste Form des Säkularisierungstheorems”, the strongest form of the secularization theorem, but he proposes to reverse it. Modern political concepts are not secularized theological concepts; they are *theologized* concepts, to which an absoluteness they do not have is attributed, because one expects from politics what one expected from God. The two men then corresponded, from 1971 to 1978, at Blumenberg's initiative; the correspondence was published in 2007.

This last inversion is, I believe, the key: it makes it possible to answer Blumenberg without proving him wrong. What does he say, in sum? That modernity does not inherit Christian contents, but finds itself before empty places that it is tempted to occupy, and that, when it occupies them, it overloads its own concepts with an absoluteness that does not belong to them: it theologizes. This is what I call a refoundation. I must, however, concede an important point. If one takes the essay's thesis in its extensive version, the one which says that *every* modern structure resembling a Christian structure is a disguised religion, then Blumenberg is right against it: that thesis proves too much. It makes religion unfalsifiable, since it will be found wherever one finds a hope, a community, an ideal or a history. It belongs to what Blumenberg calls a metaphysics of substance, which assumes that contents persist beneath metamorphoses: it is the laziness of the historian who sees shadows everywhere.

The criterion set out in the prologue finds its justification here; it owes more to Stirner than to Löwith. A thought refounds a religion not when it occupies a place left empty by Christianity, which is inevitable, but when it installs in that place an absolute *in the name of which the sacrifice of the individual can be demanded*, and whose critique becomes a sacrilege. Progress as a hypothesis about the accumulation of knowledge is not a religion; Progress as the end of history, in the name of which the death of entire generations can be demanded, is one. Society as an object of study is not a religion; Comte's Great Being, before which one kneels, is one. The sovereign as an organ of decision is not a religion; the sovereign whose decision makes the law and whom no one judges is one. The criterion is not resemblance of structure, which Blumenberg is right to reject, but the sacrifice demanded: Stirner's question, that of the fear of Man which is only a modified form of the fear of God. Thus armed, the thesis becomes falsifiable. It can be false for a given author: it is, I believe, for Blumenberg himself, whose *Selbstbehauptung* demands no sacrifice and promises no salvation, which makes him an unexpected ally of the thesis. It remains true for the others, Comte, state Marxism, Schmitt, and perhaps Heidegger, whom Blumenberg no longer forbids me to accuse, since his own concept of theologization says what I reproach them with.

>
> Goethe's Prometheus speaks to Zeus like a proprietor: “Ich kenne nichts Aermeres / Unter der Sonn', als euch, Götter! / Ihr nähret kümmerlich / Von Opfersteuern / Und Gebetshauch / Eure Majestät”, I know nothing poorer under the sun than you, gods: you meagrely nourish your majesty on sacrificial tributes and the breath of prayer; and he ends: “Hier sitz' ich, forme Menschen / Nach meinem Bilde, / Ein Geschlecht, das mir gleich sey, / Zu leiden, zu weinen, / Zu genießen und zu freuen sich, / Und dein nicht zu achten, / Wie ich!”, here I sit, forming men in my own image, a race that shall be like me, to suffer, to weep, to enjoy and to rejoice, and to pay no heed to you, as I do.

The gods are poor in it because they live on sacrifices: this is the essay's criterion read backwards, a divinity that has being only through what is immolated to it, and the man who stops immolating starves it to death. The poem does not occupy the place of God, it leaves it empty and works beside it.
>
> Mary Shelley subtitled *Frankenstein* “the Modern Prometheus”, and her Victor warns: “Learn from me, if not by my precepts, at least by my example, how dangerous is the acquirement of knowledge”; the creature answers him: “I ought to be thy Adam, but I am rather the fallen angel, whom thou drivest from joy for no misdeed.” The novel shows what reoccupation costs: the man who takes the creator's place also inherits the creature's questions, and he has no answer. Kafka, finally, summed up in four contradictory legends what is known of Prometheus, the last saying that everyone grew weary, the gods, the eagles, the wound; “Blieb das unerklärliche Felsgebirge. Da sie aus einem Wahrheitsgrund kommt, muß sie wieder im Unerklärlichen enden”, there remained the inexplicable mass of rock; the legend tries to explain the inexplicable; since it comes out of a ground of truth, it must end again in the inexplicable. This is the last page of *Arbeit am Mythos*, and Blumenberg chose it.
