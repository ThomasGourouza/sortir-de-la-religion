### André Comte-Sponville (b. 1952): a spirituality of immanence

In 2006 André Comte-Sponville published *L'Esprit de l'athéisme. Introduction à une spiritualité sans Dieu*. The book is built around three questions: can we do without religion? Does God exist? What spirituality for atheists? To the first two he answers as an atheist; to the third, as a mystic, and therein lies his originality.

His first answer is measured. One can do without religion, in the sense that one can live without a Church, without dogma, without worship; one cannot do without communion, fidelity or love. The paraphrase comes from a review, and it renders the movement of the book well: the best of what religion provided, the bond between human beings, attachment to transmitted values, the capacity to love, can survive faith. Comte-Sponville calls himself a faithful atheist: he has lost faith, not fidelity to what the Christian tradition taught him of justice, compassion and love. The second is no less measured: atheism is for him a belief, not a knowledge; he does not claim to prove that God does not exist, he says as a philosopher why he does not believe, without contempt for believers.

It is the third that interests us. Comte-Sponville maintains there that spirituality does not belong to the religions. It is the life of the mind insofar as it relates to the infinite, to eternity, to the absolute; and this absolute the atheist does not deny, he denies only its transcendence. The atheist's spirituality is a mysticism of immanence. He gives a formula for it that condenses the whole book: “Un Dieu ? Pour quoi faire ? L'univers suffit. Une Eglise ? Inutile. Le monde suffit. Une foi ? A quoi bon ? L'expérience suffit”, a God? What for? The universe is enough. A Church? Useless. The world is enough. A faith? What good is it? Experience is enough. And elsewhere: “A quoi bon rêver d'un paradis ? Le Royaume, c'est ici et maintenant”, what good is dreaming of a paradise? The Kingdom is here and now. And the last sentence of the book says it again: “Nous sommes déjà dans le Royaume : l'éternité, c'est maintenant”, we are already in the Kingdom: eternity is now. The philosophical formula that grounds this mysticism is Spinozist: “Tout est immanent au Tout”, everything is immanent to the All.

The heart of this spirituality is an experience, and Comte-Sponville calls it by the very name Romain Rolland had proposed to Freud: the oceanic feeling. He defines it as “l'expérience d'une intériorité (mais qui me contient, et que je ne contiens pas), d'une immanence, d'une unité, d'une immersion, d'un dedans”, the experience of an interiority (but one that contains me, and that I do not contain), of an immanence, a unity, an immersion, an inside. He adds that this experience, which he says he has known himself, culminates in acceptance: “dire oui à tout ce qui est, à tout ce qui arrive”, saying yes to everything that is, to everything that happens. He sees in it the common ground of the mystics of every tradition, Christian, Buddhist, Taoist, an experience that is sufficient unto itself and does not need God in order to be lived. And he does not want to leave it to the fanatics: “la spiritualité est une chose trop importante pour qu'on l'abandonne aux fondamentalistes”, spirituality is too important a thing to be abandoned to the fundamentalists.

The confrontation with Part Eight is constructed. In 1930, Freud could not discover in himself Rolland's oceanic feeling; he made it the survival of a primitive state of the ego, prior to its separation from the external world, and refused to see in it the source of religion, born in his view of infantile helplessness and the need for a father. In 2006, an atheist philosopher makes this feeling the heart of a spirituality without God. Rolland was therefore right against Freud on one point: the experience exists and does not need the personal God in order to be expressed. Freud was right against Rolland on another: nothing obliges us to see a revelation in it. Comte-Sponville holds both ends, Rolland's experience and Freud's atheism.

The objections came from two sides, and one of them is attested. On the atheist side, Michel Onfray places Comte-Sponville in what he calls Christian atheism, and is said to have called him a “chrétien athée”, an atheist Christian. In Onfray's mouth this is an accusation: the faithful atheist keeps Christian morality, compassion, humility, love of neighbour, abandoning only God; he is still one of those pious atheists Stirner denounced. I have found no reply by Comte-Sponville to this formula. On the philosophical side, Hausemer made a graver objection, carried by the very title of his review, “André Comte-Sponville ou La disparition du mal”, André Comte-Sponville or the disappearance of evil: the Spinozist acceptance of everything that happens makes evil disappear. If I say yes to everything that is, I also say yes to the suffering of the child.

This objection strikes home, and it brings us back to Ivan Karamazov. Ivan refused the harmony that would justify a child's tear; he returned his ticket. Comte-Sponville, like Spinoza and like the Nietzsche of *amor fati*, accepts the whole of reality. But Spinoza asked that evil be understood, not approved, and the *Ethics* is also a treatise on the power of acting, which in no way commands passivity before injustice. Comte-Sponville's spirituality is not, by the criterion, a refoundation: it demands no sacrifice, promises no collective salvation, asks no one to kneel before a Great Being. But it is exposed to another weakness: by saying that the universe is enough, it risks forgetting what is not enough, and what religion, at least, named. What this spirituality lacks is Marx's *Protestation*. The oceanic experience consoles; it does not protest.

> **En littérature.** Arthur Rimbaud, “L'Éternité” (1872) ; Albert Camus, *Noces* (1939) ; Jean Giono, *Que ma joie demeure* (1935)
>
> “Elle est retrouvée. / Quoi ? L'Éternité. / C'est la mer allée / Avec le soleil.”, it is found again. / What? Eternity. / It is the sea gone off / With the sun. The poem of May 1872 says what Comte-Sponville will put into prose: eternity is not another world, it is now, in the sea and the sun, and there one frees oneself “Des humains suffrages, / Des communs élans”, from human approval, from common impulses; then, without transition: “Là pas d'espérance, / Nul orietur. / Science avec patience, / Le supplice est sûr”, there, no hope, / no orietur. / Knowledge with patience, / the torment is certain.
>
> Why here: “Le Royaume, c'est ici et maintenant” is a line of Rimbaud before it is a thesis; but the poem also contains the objection the essay addresses to Comte-Sponville. No hope, no *orietur*, no “he shall rise”: eternity found again is without promise and without consolation, but “le supplice est sûr”, the torment is certain, and Rimbaud does not say yes to everything that is. The oceanic experience consoles, the essay writes, it does not protest; the poem feels eternity and keeps the torment, and in this it is more complete than the wisdom that claims it.
>
> Camus, in “Noces à Tipasa”, wrote the page Comte-Sponville readily quotes: the Roman ruins overgrown with wormwood, the sea, the sun, and a young man who says that there is nothing to hide and nothing to hope for, that the world is beautiful and that this is enough, that the gods speak in the sun and the scent of wormwood. It is the same immanence, in a man who, twelve years later, would write *The Rebel*: the kingdom of Tipasa was not enough, revolt was needed, and this is precisely the path the conclusion of the essay proposes to Comte-Sponville. Giono, finally, in *Que ma joie demeure*, brings down onto the Grémone plateau an acrobat, Bobi, who teaches the peasants to sow flowers, to listen to the stag and to keep joy without possessing it; joy does not remain, and Bobi dies struck by lightning. The title is a prayer from Bach turned into a question: immanent joy, here and now, and the storm that awaits it.

### Luc Ferry (b. 1951): the man-God and horizontal transcendence

Luc Ferry published *L'Homme-Dieu ou le Sens de la vie* in 1996, then, with André Comte-Sponville, *La Sagesse des Modernes* (1998), and, with Marcel Gauchet, *Le Religieux après la religion* (2004). Of all the authors in this part, he is the one who has engaged most directly in dialogue with the others. His thesis describes a double movement, which he calls on one side the humanization of the divine and on the other the divinization of the human.

The humanization of the divine is secularization in Gauchet's sense: Christian contents become secular, love of neighbour becomes solidarity, charity becomes humanitarian action, salvation becomes happiness. The divinization of the human is the inverse and complementary movement: the values that modern man places above his life, the true, the just, the beautiful, love, impose themselves on his conscience as if they came from elsewhere. He does not invent them at will; he discovers them in himself as a demand that exceeds him. He no longer believes in God, but lives by values that bear all the marks of the sacred: one can die for them, and sometimes one does. Ferry calls this structure a transcendence within immanence, or a horizontal transcendence. The sacred is no longer above, in heaven; it is ahead, in other human beings and in the horizon of meaning they open.

Ferry describes this shift from the standpoint of tradition. The bond that unites human beings, he writes, “n'est plus situé dans une tradition, dans un héritage imposé de l'extérieur, dans un amont de leur conscience, mais c'est en aval qu'il nous faut désormais penser ce qui pourrait être l'analogue moderne des traditions perdues: une identité post-traditionnelle”, is no longer located in a tradition, in a heritage imposed from outside, upstream of their consciousness, but it is downstream that we must henceforth think what could be the modern analogue of the lost traditions: a post-traditional identity. Transcendence no longer comes from upstream, from what has been received; it comes from downstream, from what is to be done. Later, Ferry would propose defining philosophy itself as “une doctrine du salut sans Dieu, une spiritualité laïque”, a doctrine of salvation without God, a secular spirituality.

Ferry thus poses a problem for our essay, with a frankness that does him credit. He does not claim that modern man has left religion without remainder; he says that the sacred has shifted from heaven to man, and that this shift is the truth of modern humanism. Now this is what Stirner reproached Feuerbach with: Man has become the supreme being, and one has freed oneself from God only by worshipping humanity. The very title *L'Homme-Dieu* would have made Stirner smile. Ferry takes on the divinization of the human knowingly, which distinguishes him from Feuerbach but does not answer the objection.

The objections are attested. Agnès Antoine, in *Esprit* in December 1996, wrote: “Il y a[…] quelque naïveté ou perversité à choisir comme titre du livre l'"homme-Dieu"”, there is [...] some naivety or perversity in choosing “man-God” as the title of the book. Cantin himself, following Fernand Dumont, wonders whether a transcendence lodged in the immanence of consciousness still deserves to be called transcendence. The objection is just, but it opens an alternative. Either horizontal transcendence is only a metaphor for the moderns' attachment to values they judge superior to their lives: there is then neither refoundation nor spirituality, only a morality. Or it is a true transcendence, and Ferry consecrates, after Feuerbach and Comte, a religion of Humanity, more discreet and more liberal.

I believe that Ferry oscillates between the two, and that this oscillation is instructive. He makes the disposition to die for a value, liberty, justice or those one loves, the sign of its sacredness. This disposition is not enough to place him among the refounders in the strict sense: the criterion concerns the absolute in whose name the sacrifice of the individual can be demanded, not freely consented self-offering, and nothing in Ferry demands of anyone that he die. But Ferry does not confine himself to noting these devotions; he calls them sacred, and that is the need to consecrate that Engels reproached Feuerbach with. He therefore belongs to soft refoundation: a religion of the human that has renounced clergy, dogma and constraint, and contents itself with consecrating that for which some human beings agree to die. It is the most liberal form of refoundation, and perhaps the only one compatible with a democratic society. It nonetheless remains a consecration, and one can apply to it the question Stirner put to Feuerbach: in the name of what does this ideal command me?

> **En littérature.** Victor Hugo, “Le Satyre” (1859) ; Émile Zola, *Travail* (1901)
>
> “Le Satyre”, at the centre of *La Légende des siècles*, brings up onto Olympus a shaggy faun whom the gods want to humiliate; he sings, and his song grows with him: the earth, man, the future, until, having become greater than heaven, he summons the gods: “Place à Tout ! Je suis Pan ; Jupiter ! à genoux”, make way for the All! I am Pan; Jupiter, on your knees!
>
> Why here: this is the divinization of the human and the humanization of the divine in a single line, eighteen years after *The Essence of Christianity* and more than a century before *L'Homme-Dieu*. The satyr does not deny the gods; he makes them kneel before the All of which man is the voice; and Hugo, who believed in God, writes here the horizontal transcendence of *L'Homme-Dieu* in its Romantic version, that is to say without Ferry's liberal restraint. The poem shows where soft consecration leads when it raises its voice: it demands that one kneel.
>
> Zola, in *Travail*, has an engineer, Luc Froment, found a workers' city on Fourier's model, the Crêcherie, which gradually absorbs the factory, the town and the Church; Abbé Marle says his last Mass in an empty church that collapses on him, and the novel ends, sixty years later, with a festival of humanity reconciled by work and love. It is Ferry's religion of humanity written by a novelist who wanted it and who, without knowing it, made a liturgy of it: the happy city has its festivals, its founders and its old Church that must be seen to fall. The essay's soft refoundation finds in Zola its closing chapter.
>
> Sources: Victor Hugo, “Le Satyre”, *La Légende des siècles*, first series, VIII (1859) ; Émile Zola, *Travail*, book III (1901).

### Comte-Sponville and Ferry: the quarrel of the two wisdoms

*La Sagesse des Modernes*, their book of dialogue on the great questions of ethics, attests that their disagreement was public. I have not read its text and I quote nothing from it; all that is known is that Ferry there judges Comte-Sponville's wisdom to be close to Buddhism. This disagreement bears directly on our question, and it can be reconstructed from their other books without attributing to them sentences they did not write.

Comte-Sponville is a materialist. His avowed genealogy runs from Epicurus and Lucretius to Spinoza, with borrowings from the Eastern wisdoms: there is nothing other than nature, of which man is a part, and freedom is only necessity understood and accepted. Wisdom consists in ceasing to hope, in inhabiting the present, in loving what is; transcendence, in all its forms, is an illusion of a mind that believes itself greater than the world. Ferry is a Kantian: man is distinguished from nature by his freedom, his capacity to tear himself away from every natural or historical code, which grounds his dignity. Values are not deduced from reality, they impose themselves on it as an ought, and horizontal transcendence is the truth of human freedom: man is the being who never coincides with what he is.

By the criterion, the two positions are not of equal worth. Comte-Sponville's wisdom refounds no sacrificial absolute, but it risks accepting too much and disarming revolt. Ferry's humanism keeps the capacity to protest, but pays for it with a consecration. Here we meet again the alternative between Spinoza and Kant, which Parts Three and Four brought out without settling it: acceptance without transcendence, or transcendence without God. Perhaps neither is a complete exit; but the first is closer to an exit without refoundation, and the second more capable of hearing Marx's *Protestation*. The conclusion will have to say whether one can have both.

> **En littérature.** Johann Wolfgang von Goethe, “Grenzen der Menschheit” (1781)
>
> The poem is Goethe's answer to his own “Prometheus”, seven years later. “Denn mit Göttern / Soll sich nicht messen / Irgend ein Mensch. / Hebt er sich aufwärts, / Und berührt / Mit dem Scheitel die Sterne, / Nirgends haften dann / Die unsichern Sohlen, / Und mit ihm spielen / Wolken und Winde”, for with gods no man shall measure himself; if he lifts himself upwards and touches the stars with the crown of his head, then his unsteady soles find hold nowhere, and clouds and winds make sport of him. “Was unterscheidet / Götter von Menschen? / Daß viele Wellen / Vor jenen wandeln, / Ein ewiger Strom: / Uns hebt die Welle, / Verschlingt die Welle, / Und wir versinken”, what distinguishes gods from men? That many waves pass before them, an eternal stream: the wave lifts us, the wave swallows us, and we sink.
>
> Why here: the section sets Comte-Sponville's acceptance without transcendence against Ferry's transcendence without God, and Goethe wrote the two poles in two poems. “Prometheus”, quoted in connection with Blumenberg, is Hugo's Satyr, the man who forms men in his own image and honours no one; “Grenzen der Menschheit” is Goethe's Spinozist consent, the one he recounts in book XIV of *Poetry and Truth*: a man who kisses the hem of the old holy Father's garment, not out of fear, but because he knows which wave carries him. The conclusion of the essay will want to hold both; Goethe held them seven years apart, and it took Spinoza in between.
>
> Sources: Johann Wolfgang von Goethe, “Grenzen der Menschheit” (written c. 1781, published 1789).
