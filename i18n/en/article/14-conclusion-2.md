### The sigh without a heaven

One question remains that neither the wisdoms nor the critiques have settled. Religion, Marx said, is also “die Protestation gegen das wirkliche Elend”, the sigh of the oppressed creature. If it were only an error, its disappearance would be pure gain. If it is also a lament, what becomes of that lament when heaven empties?

The spiritualities of immanence do not answer it well, and this is their gravest weakness. Spinoza asks us to understand necessity, and Ivan answered in advance that he did not want to understand the child's tear; Comte-Sponville proposes saying yes to everything that happens, and his critic, H. Hausemer, was right to remark that in this acceptance evil disappears. A spirituality that consoles us for injustice by making it understood refounds nothing, but it does not answer the lament: it keeps religion's function as opium, without its function as protest. Consolation is therefore not a second criterion of refoundation; it is the sign of what an exit leaves unanswered. This is where Marx remains right against the sages. Murdoch herself escapes it only halfway, as we saw with regard to her morality of vision, and corporate meditation offers its caricature.

The theologians of the twentieth century gave this question an answer that unbelievers would be wrong to ignore. Bonhoeffer conceives a God who lets himself be edged out of the world and who helps only through his weakness, that is to say a God who no longer guarantees anything and who shares the lament instead of soothing it. Simone Weil writes: “La religion en tant que source de consolation est un obstacle à la véritable foi : en ce sens l'athéisme est une purification”, religion as a source of consolation is an obstacle to true faith: in this sense atheism is a purification. These two answers take away religion's function as opium without taking away its function as protest. But they remain the answers of believers, and the question of this essay is what becomes of the lament for someone who has no God, not even a weak one.

The most just answer seems to me to be Camus's, and that is why I keep him among the exceptions despite the instability of his position. “Je me révolte, donc nous sommes”, I rebel, therefore we are. The lament that no longer has a heaven does not disappear; it changes its address. It is no longer addressed to God, it is addressed to other human beings, and it founds a community that is not a Church, because it rests on no dogma, but on the recognition of a shared suffering. Here we meet again, by an unexpected detour, the sentence of Markel that Zosima preserved: “всякий пред всеми за всех и за всё виноват”, each of us is guilty before all, for all and for everything. Dostoevsky utters it as a Christian, Camus rediscovers it as an unbeliever, and Levinas, between the two, draws from it a responsibility for the other without reciprocity. Between them there is the difference of God, and there is also the same conviction: the answer to suffering is not an explanation but a solidarity.

This answer has a price, and it must be stated in closing. Camus's solidarity is an exit from religion only if it remains horizontal, that is to say if the “we” it founds never becomes an absolute to which individuals are sacrificed. That is what the twentieth century saw fail, when the “we” of revolt became History, the Party or the Nation, and the secular religions designated their victims; it is what Camus spent his life denouncing, and what his adversaries of 1952 did not forgive him. Girard gives this demand its sharpest form: a “we” that remains a solidarity and does not become a crowd is a “we” that refuses the scapegoat. The sigh of the oppressed creature can find an addressee without a heaven: the others. But on condition that these others are never made into a god, and that no victim is ever designated for them. The question that the quarrel of the two wisdoms left open thus receives its answer: one can hold together Spinoza's acceptance and Marx's protest, if one consents to the necessity of the world without consenting to the injustice of men, and if the solidarity that carries the lament remains horizontal. The critique of religion has not abolished the need to which religion answered; at best, it has found for it an address that demands no adoration. It is little, and it is perhaps all that can be asked of an exit from religion that does not wish to found another.

> **En littérature.** Primo Levi, *Se questo è un uomo* (1947) ; John Steinbeck, *The Grapes of Wrath* (1939) ; Alfred de Vigny, “Le Mont des Oliviers” (1843)
>
> On the evening of the selection of October 1944, at Auschwitz, old Kuhn, who has not been chosen, prays aloud on his bunk, his cap on his head, rocking back and forth, and thanks God; beside him, young Beppo, who is twenty and will leave for the gas chamber the day after next, stares fixedly at the light. Levi concludes that, if he were God, he would spit Kuhn's prayer onto the ground. Why here: it is the end of this section in a single page. Kuhn's prayer is the consolation that Weil forbids and the opium that Marx denounces, and it has a scapegoat, Beppo, whose death is Kuhn's grace; Levi, an unbeliever, takes away the consolation without taking away the protest, and the protest is addressed to God for the last time, in the form of spittle.
>
> Tom Joad, at the end of *The Grapes of Wrath*, leaves his mother in the night after killing the man who killed Casy; he tells her, in substance, that he will be wherever a man is hungry, wherever a cop is beating someone, in the shouts of those who rebel and in the laughter of children who have enough to eat, because Casy taught him that a man has only a piece of a great soul that belongs to everyone. It is “I rebel, therefore we are”, spoken by an Oklahoma farmer in the words of a defrocked preacher: Camus's horizontal “we”, without God, without Party and without victim, which is the essay's answer to the sigh without a heaven.
>
> Vigny wrote, a century earlier, the lament at the moment it no longer has an addressee. On the Mount of Olives, Christ prays and the Father does not answer; the poem ends, in the lines added in 1862 under the title “Le Silence”, with this: “Le juste opposera le dédain à l'absence / Et ne répondra plus que par un froid silence / Au silence éternel de la Divinité”, the just man will meet absence with disdain, and will answer only with a cold silence the eternal silence of the Godhead. It is the sigh without a heaven at its zero point: the lament no longer has an address, and the just man chooses silence. Levi spits, Tom Joad leaves, Vigny falls silent; the essay holds that the lament must change its address, and the three texts show what each of the three answers costs.
