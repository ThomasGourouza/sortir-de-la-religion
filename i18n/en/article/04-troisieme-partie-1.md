## Part Three: the classical age, the God of the philosophers

Between the Wars of Religion and the Enlightenment, the question changes shape. The Reformation shattered the unity of Christendom, and Renaissance scepticism, nourished on Sextus Empiricus, shook confidence in reason. Montaigne uses this double shock to humble reason before received faith, and he leaves it to his readers to decide whether he is defending faith or reducing it to a custom. Descartes builds against doubt a metaphysics with God as its keystone, and Pascal rejects it in the name of faith. Spinoza thinks God as nature itself. Bayle draws from the quarrel a conclusion that will scandalize: morality does not depend on belief. Leibniz, finally, answers him by making God the sufficient reason of everything that exists, called upon to account for evil. This is the age in which the God of the philosophers takes shape, and in which, by the same stroke, the weapons of its critique are forged.

### Montaigne (1533-1592): received religion as a custom

The longest chapter of the *Essays* is a filial commission that went wrong. Pierre Bunel, a humanist passing through the château, had left Montaigne's father a fifteenth-century book, the *Theologia naturalis* of the Catalan Raymond Sebond, a professor at Toulouse, who claimed to establish all the truths of the faith by reason alone, by reading the “book of creatures”. The father asked the son to translate it; Montaigne did, and the translation appeared in 1569. Then, to defend the book against its critics, he wrote the *Apology for Raymond Sebond* (*Essays* II, 12), published with the first two books in 1580 and enlarged up to the posthumous edition of 1595. The story of its origins is in the chapter itself: Bunel made his father a present “d'un livre qui s'intitule Theologia naturalis ; sive, Liber creaturarum magistri Raimondi de Sebonde”, of a book entitled Theologia naturalis, or Book of Creatures, by Master Raymond Sebond.

The paradox of the *Apology* fits into one sentence: Montaigne defends a rational theology by ruining reason. To the critics who reproach Sebond for the weakness of his arguments, he does not reply that they are strong; he replies that the contrary arguments are no better, and that human reason, looked at without indulgence, is worth nothing at all. The two hundred pages that follow are the most systematic inventory of human impotence the Renaissance produced. The thesis is laid down at once: “La presomption est nostre maladie naturelle et originelle. La plus calamiteuse et fragile de toutes les creatures c'est l'homme, et quant et quant, la plus orgueilleuse”, presumption is our natural and original disease; the most wretched and frail of all creatures is man, and at the same time the proudest. Man believes himself master of the world and does not even know what his cat thinks: “Quand je me jouë à ma chatte, qui sçait, si elle passe son temps de moy plus que je ne fay d'elle ?”, when I play with my cat, who knows whether she is not passing her time with me more than I am with her? The privilege theology granted to man, created in the image of God and king of creation, is dismantled piece by piece through a comparison with the beasts that always turns to the beasts' advantage.

Then comes reason, and Pyrrhonian scepticism, which Montaigne had read in Sextus Empiricus, serves as the instrument of demolition. The philosophers contradict one another on everything, the senses deceive us, judgment varies with mood and health. Montaigne draws a motto from it, which he says he bears with the emblem of a pair of scales: “Cette fantasie est plus seurement conceuë par interrogation : Que sçay-je ? comme je la porte à la devise d'une balance”, this idea is more surely conceived in the form of a question: what do I know? as I bear it as a motto with a pair of scales. The interrogative form matters: it avoids the negative dogmatism of the sceptic who asserts that nothing is known, and who, in so doing, still knows something.

The weapon then turns around. If reason can do nothing, it can do nothing for religion either, and the only thing that distinguishes one religion from another is the place where one was born. The passage, the most famous in the chapter, is of an audacity that is poorly measured because it is quoted out of context. Montaigne observes that Christians live no better than others, and he concludes: “Tout cela c'est un signe tres-evident que nous ne recevons nostre religion qu'à nostre façon et par nos mains, et non autrement que comme les autres religions se reçoivent”, all this is a very evident sign that we receive our religion only in our own fashion and by our own hands, and in no other way than other religions are received. Then, after listing the human reasons that bind us to ours (antiquity, authority, fear of threats), he adds: “Une autre region, d'autres tesmoings, pareilles promesses et menasses, nous pourroyent imprimer par mesme voye une creance contraire. Nous sommes Chrestiens à mesme tiltre que nous sommes ou Perigordins ou Alemans”, another region, other witnesses, similar promises and threats could imprint on us by the same path a contrary belief. We are Christians by the same title as we are Perigordians or Germans. What makes us Christians is not the truth of Christianity but custom, just as what makes us Perigordians is geography.

The same logic extends to the gods themselves. Montaigne, who had read Lucretius and Cicero, is ironic about the human manufacture of divinities: “L'homme est bien insensé : Il ne sçauroit forger un ciron, et forge des Dieux à douzaines”, man is truly mad: he could not make a mite, and he makes gods by the dozen. The sentence has the air of a maxim of Epicurus or a fragment of Xenophanes, and one understands why the seventeenth-century libertines made the *Apology* their breviary.

Must we then read Montaigne as an atheist in disguise, defending Sebond the better to undermine faith? That is the libertine reading, which has had great champions. I do not think it right, and the conclusion of the chapter says why. Montaigne there quotes a saying of Seneca, whom he calls a pagan “tesmoing”, a witness: “O la vile chose, dict-il, et abjecte, que l'homme, s'il ne s'esleve au dessus de l'humanité !”, oh what a vile and abject thing, he says, is man, if he does not raise himself above humanity! And he replies: the wish is good, but absurd, for man can see only with his own eyes and grasp only with his own hold. “Il s'eslevera si Dieu lui preste extraordinairement la main ; il s'eslevera, abandonnant et renonçant à ses propres moyens, et se laissant hausser et soubslever par les moyens purement celestes. C'est à nostre foy Chrestienne, non à sa vertu Stoique, de pretendre à cette divine et miraculeuse metamorphose”, he will rise if God extraordinarily lends him a hand; he will rise by abandoning and renouncing his own means, and letting himself be raised and lifted up by purely heavenly means. It is for our Christian faith, not for his Stoic virtue, to aspire to that divine and miraculous metamorphosis. The structure is that of fideism: reason is humbled so that grace alone can raise. The humbling of reason is not the negation of faith; it is, in Montaigne as later in Pascal and in Bayle, its condition.

This structure nonetheless has an effect its author does not control, and that is what matters here. By placing faith entirely outside reason, Montaigne leaves reason a single explanation of religion as it exists in the world: custom. He has no need to deny grace; it is enough for him to observe that it cannot be seen, and that people believe what they have been taught to believe. The sincerity of the Catholic Montaigne, who died, according to the account given by Étienne Pasquier, while mass was being said in his room, is beyond question and in any case undecidable; what is not undecidable is that his book already contains, two centuries before Hume, the thesis of *The Natural History of Religion*: people's religion is explained by their nature and their history, not by its truth. Without being a critic of religion in the sense that d'Holbach is, Montaigne brackets religion: without denying it, he suspends judgment on its truth and describes its reality. This is a new form, descriptive rather than legislative, of what I have called the regime of social function, and it is born from the pen of a believer.

Pascal's response is attested, and it is twofold. In the *Conversation with M. de Sacy*, dated to 1655 and known to us only through the account left by Nicolas Fontaine, Sacy's secretary, Pascal sets Epictetus and Montaigne against each other as the two philosophers who saw most clearly, the one the greatness of man, the other his wretchedness; each is right against the other, and only Christianity reconciles the two truths. Montaigne thus serves Pascal as an instrument: he brings down the pride of reason and prepares the place of faith. But the *Pensées* also show what Pascal does not forgive him, and it is not his scepticism, it is his way of dying. Fragment 62 of the Brunschvicg edition attacks the “sot projet qu'il a de se peindre”, the foolish project he has of portraying himself, and fragment 63 something graver: “on ne peut excuser ses sentiments tout païens sur la mort ; car il faut renoncer à toute piété, si on ne veut au moins mourir chrétiennement ; or, il ne pense qu'à mourir lâchement et mollement par tout son livre”, one cannot excuse his wholly pagan feelings about death; for one must renounce all piety if one does not at least wish to die as a Christian; yet throughout his book he thinks only of dying cowardly and softly. And the next fragment says in a word what Montaigne was for him: “Ce n'est pas dans Montaigne, mais dans moi, que je trouve tout ce que j'y vois”, it is not in Montaigne but in myself that I find everything I see there (fr. 64).

Pascal's reproach hits the mark, and it opens the second side of Montaigne, the one that concerns spirituality. The first book contained a chapter with a Ciceronian title, “Que philosopher, c'est apprendre à mourir”, that to philosophize is to learn to die (I, 20), where Montaigne takes up the Stoic meditation on death: “La premeditation de la mort est premeditation de la liberté. Qui a apris à mourir, il a desapris à servir”, the premeditation of death is the premeditation of freedom. Whoever has learned to die has unlearned how to serve. But he already slips in a wish that announces the book's evolution: “Je veux qu'on agisse, et qu'on allonge les offices de la vie tant qu'on peut, et que la mort me treuve plantant mes chous, mais nonchalant d'elle, et encore plus de mon jardin imparfait”, I want us to act, and to prolong the tasks of life as long as we can, and I want death to find me planting my cabbages, but unconcerned about it, and even more so about my unfinished garden. It is this nonchalance that Pascal calls dying softly. And the last chapter of the *Essays*, “De l'expérience”, “Of experience” (III, 13), completes the movement by explicitly refusing any elevation above the human. There Montaigne says of those who want to get outside themselves: “C'est folie : au lieu de se transformer en anges, ils se transforment en bestes ; au lieu de se hausser, ils s'abattent. Ces humeurs transcendentes m'effrayent, comme les lieux hautains et inaccessibles”, it is folly: instead of transforming themselves into angels, they transform themselves into beasts; instead of raising themselves, they bring themselves down. These transcendent humours frighten me, like lofty and inaccessible places. Wisdom lies elsewhere: “C'est une absolue perfection, et comme divine, de scavoyr jouyr loiallement de son estre”, it is an absolute perfection, and as it were divine, to know how to enjoy one's being loyally. And the book almost closes on a sentence that could serve as an epigraph to the whole of Part Twelve of this essay: “Les plus belles vies sont, à mon gré, celles qui se rangent au modelle commun et humain, avec ordre, mais sans miracle et sans extravagance”, the finest lives are, to my mind, those that conform to the common human pattern, with order, but without miracle and without extravagance.

Between the conclusion of the *Apology* and that of the *Essays*, something has tipped over. In the *Apology*, man can rise only if God lends him a hand. In “Of experience”, he no longer has to rise at all: elevation has become a danger, and perfection “as it were divine” consists in inhabiting one's condition loyally. Montaigne does not deny salvation; he stops talking about it. This silence is a spirituality, in the sense in which Hadot and Foucault will take the word, a way of transforming oneself through the exercise of writing and judgment; and it is a spirituality without salvation, which promises nothing after death. Long before modern wisdoms argued over whether they should remain immanent or keep a transcendence (Part Twelve), Montaigne chose immanence, and he did so without polemic, as a practising Catholic. That is why he enters none of the refoundations this essay records: he proposes no absolute, he asks no one to sacrifice themselves to it, and his motto is a question.

### Descartes (1596-1650): the idea of the infinite and the guarantor God

The *Meditationes de prima philosophia* (*Meditations on First Philosophy*) appeared in Latin in Paris in 1641, followed by the objections of several learned men and the author's replies, then in Amsterdam in 1642; the French translation by the duc de Luynes, revised by Descartes, appeared in 1647. The book opens with a dedicatory letter to the Faculty of Theology of Paris, the Sorbonne, and this letter states the project in so many words: “Semper existimavi duas quaestiones, de Deo & de Animâ, praecipuas esse ex iis quae Philosophiae potius quàm Theologiae ope sunt demonstrandae”, I have always thought that these two questions, of God and of the soul, were the chief among those that ought to be demonstrated by the aid of philosophy rather than of theology.

The rest of the sentence gives the reason, political as much as philosophical. For the faithful, faith suffices; but infidels can be persuaded of no religion, and of hardly any moral virtue, unless one first proves these two things to them by natural reason, for, says Descartes, “pauci rectum utili praeferrent, si nec Deum timerent, nec aliam vitam expectarent”, few would prefer the right to the useful if they neither feared God nor expected another life. Descartes takes it for granted that human morality hangs on the fear of God and the hope of the hereafter, a thesis that Bayle, forty years later, will set about demolishing in the *Various Thoughts on the Occasion of a Comet*. The comparison is constructed, since Bayle is not aiming at Descartes here; the fact remains that the philosopher who founds modern science shares, on religion, the oldest prejudice of all, that of Polybius and Critias. Descartes even adds, in the same letter, that proving God by Scripture and Scripture by God would seem to infidels a circle. The irony is that his readers, beginning with Antoine Arnauld in the Fourth Objections, will accuse him of committing another circle, by guaranteeing through God the clear and distinct ideas he uses to prove God.

Descartes's gesture is understood by contrast with the tradition he rejects. Thomas Aquinas, in the *Summa theologiae* (I, q. 2, a. 3), had given five ways of proving God, all *a posteriori*, all starting from the world: motion, causality, contingency, degrees of perfection, order; and he had rejected, in the first article of the same question, Anselm's argument, which claimed to conclude from the idea of God to his existence. Descartes reverses the order. He does not start from the world, which he has just doubted to the point of supposing that an evil genius deceives him about everything; he starts from the only point that resists doubt, the *cogito*, “Ego sum, ego existo”, and he looks for God in thought.

The Third Meditation finds in me an idea that cannot come from me: that of the infinite. I do not perceive the infinite by negating the finite, as I perceive rest by negating motion; on the contrary, “manifeste intelligo plus realitatis esse in substantiâ infinitâ quàm in finitâ, ac proinde priorem quodammodo in me esse perceptionem infiniti quàm finiti, hoc est Dei quàm meî ipsius”, I clearly understand that there is more reality in an infinite substance than in a finite one, and hence that my perception of the infinite is in some way prior to my perception of the finite, that is, my perception of God prior to that of myself. How would I know that I doubt, that I desire, that I lack something, if I had no idea of a being more perfect than myself? This idea cannot have been produced by a finite being; it must therefore have been placed in me by God himself, “tanquam nota artificis operi suo impressa”, like the mark of the craftsman stamped on his work. The Fifth Meditation adds the proof known as ontological, a name Kant will give it: existence belongs to the essence of the supremely perfect being, so that it is no less contradictory to think of God without existence “quàm cogitare montem cui desit vallis”, than to think of a mountain without a valley. This is the argument of Anselm of Canterbury, who in chapter 2 of the *Proslogion* defined God as “aliquid quo nihil maius cogitari possit”, something than which nothing greater can be thought, recast in the language of clear and distinct ideas.

But Descartes's God is not only proved; he works. His function in the system is to guarantee truth. God being perfect, he cannot be a deceiver, for “proculdubio velle fallere, vel malitiam vel imbecillitatem testatur”, the will to deceive undoubtedly bears witness either to malice or to weakness. He therefore guarantees that what I conceive clearly and distinctly is true, and the Fifth Meditation draws the conclusion that makes him the keystone of science: “Atque ita plane video omnis scientiae certitudinem & veritatem ab unâ veri Dei cognitione pendere”, and so I see plainly that the certainty and truth of all knowledge depend on the knowledge of the true God alone.

It is this God that Pascal does not forgive Descartes. Fragment 77, which opens with “Je ne puis pardonner à Descartes”, I cannot forgive Descartes, goes on: Descartes “n'a pu s'empêcher de lui faire donner une chiquenaude, pour mettre le monde en mouvement ; après cela, il n'a plus que faire de Dieu”, could not help making him give a flick of the finger to set the world in motion; after that, he has no further use for God. The Cartesian God is indispensable, but he is so as a foundation: he guarantees mathematics and physics, then withdraws and leaves science to work on its own. He has no history, no covenant, no people; he has spoken to no one. Heidegger, who will make Descartes the moment when the being of beings becomes the certain representation of a subject, will see in this God the accomplished figure of onto-theology, and his reproach joins Pascal's: a God who serves to found is not a God before whom one prays.

Two features nonetheless complicate this picture, and they bear on our question. The first is the doctrine of the creation of the eternal truths. In a letter to Mersenne of 15 April 1630, Descartes maintains that mathematical truths, which are called eternal, were established by God and depend on him like the rest of his creatures, so that God “a été aussi libre de faire qu'il ne fût pas vrai que toutes lignes tirées du centre à la circonférence fussent égales, comme de ne pas créer le monde”, was as free to make it untrue that all lines drawn from the centre to the circumference are equal as he was free not to create the world. The God who guarantees reason is thus also a God above reason, with a freedom that exceeds anything we can understand. Leibniz will see a danger in this. For our purpose, the doctrine shows that the God of the philosophers, in the very thinker who constructed him best, is not wholly reducible to a function: there remains in him an abyss of will that Duns Scotus and Ockham would recognize.

The second feature is the very form of the book. The *Meditationes* are not a treatise. They are six meditations, spread out like six days, written in the first person, which ask the reader to retrace with the author the path from doubt to certainty, and which warn him that he must sometimes dwell for a long time on a single thought to make it familiar. And the Third Meditation, at the moment when God has just been found, breaks off for a contemplation: Descartes says he now wishes “in ipsius Dei contemplatione immorari, ejus attributa apud me expendere, & immensi hujus luminis pulchritudinem, quantum caligantis ingenii mei acies ferre poterit, intueri, admirari, adorare”, to dwell for a while in the contemplation of God himself, to weigh his attributes within myself, and to gaze upon, admire and adore the beauty of this immense light, so far as the eye of my darkened mind can bear it. He adds that, just as faith teaches us that the supreme happiness of the next life consists in this contemplation, so we experience even now that a similar contemplation, though less perfect, gives us the greatest contentment of which we are capable in this life.

This passage creates a difficulty for Foucault's thesis, examined in Part Twelve, of a “Cartesian moment” in which philosophy supposedly separated itself from spirituality: the book that performs this separation keeps the form of the exercise and culminates in adoration. This is my reading, and I offer it as such: the Cartesian moment is not a clean break, it is a conversion of spirituality into metaphysics, in which the contemplation of God remains the summit, but a summit reached by demonstration and not by purification. Descartes is the last great philosopher for whom the knowledge of God is at the same time a joy, and the first for whom that joy is no longer necessary to science. Spinoza, his reader, will take up both ends, the geometrical method and the intellectual love of God, and will try to weld them together.

Descartes therefore does not leave religion. He does more and less: he takes away from theology the task of proving God and entrusts it to philosophy, and he makes God a component of reason. This is at once the triumph of the God of the philosophers and the beginning of his fragility, for a God who is a component of reason can be dismantled by reason. Kant will do so, by showing that existence is not a predicate that can be drawn from a concept, and the ontological proof, after him, will hardly be defended except as an exercise in logic.

### Pascal (1623-1662): the God of Abraham against the God of the philosophers

On the night of Monday 23 November 1654, from about half past ten in the evening until about half past midnight, Blaise Pascal lived through an experience that he recorded on a sheet of paper, copied onto a piece of parchment, and sewed into the lining of his coat, where it was found after his death. This text, known as the *Memorial*, bears after the date a single isolated word, “Feu”, fire, and continues thus: “Dieu d'Abraham, Dieu d'Isaac, Dieu de Jacob, / non des philosophes et des savants. / Certitude. Certitude. Sentiment. Joie. Paix.”, God of Abraham, God of Isaac, God of Jacob, / not of the philosophers and the scholars. / Certainty. Certainty. Feeling. Joy. Peace.

These two lines contain a critique of religion that is never ranked among the critiques of religion, because it is made in the name of faith. The God “of the philosophers and the scholars” is the God of Descartes, whom Pascal knew, and whom he did not forgive Descartes for having wanted to do without (fr. 77, quoted above): a demonstrated God, guarantor of the eternal truths, first mover, cause of himself. This God Pascal does not deny; he declares him useless for salvation. The true God is the God of a history, that of Abraham, Isaac and Jacob, the God who reveals himself to persons and not to reasonings. Heidegger will say the same thing of the *causa sui* (Part Ten); Pascal had said it, without the concept of onto-theology, on that night in 1654. And Jean-Luc Marion, a reader of both Pascal and Heidegger, will make this opposition the centre of *God Without Being*.

The *Pensées*, published in 1670 by Port-Royal from the bundles of papers Pascal left (the order of the fragments is a reconstruction by the editors, and the Léon Brunschvicg edition I quote proposes one among others), develop this critique in three directions.

The first is the primacy of the heart. “Le cœur a ses raisons, que la raison ne connaît point”, the heart has its reasons, which reason does not know (fr. 277); and the next fragment: “C'est le cœur qui sent Dieu, et non la raison : voilà ce que c'est que la foi : Dieu sensible au cœur, non à la raison”, it is the heart that feels God, and not reason: that is what faith is: God perceptible to the heart, not to reason (fr. 278). The Pascalian “heart” is not feeling in the Romantic sense; it is the faculty that grasps first principles, immediate intuition. But the formula opens a path that Schleiermacher will take, and that leads to religion as experience.

The second is the hidden God. Pascal takes up the verse of Isaiah, “Vere tu es Deus absconditus” (Is 45, 15), and makes it a thesis: Scripture “dit au contraire que Dieu est un Dieu caché”, says on the contrary that God is a hidden God (fr. 242). One must therefore not seek to prove God from nature, as the apologists do; nature proves nothing, or rather it shows both the presence and the absence of God. Modern man, in the infinite universe, first experiences the void: “Le silence éternel de ces espaces infinis m'effraie”, the eternal silence of these infinite spaces frightens me (fr. 206). Pascal is one of the first Christian thinkers to take the experience of God's absence seriously as a religious experience. Nietzsche, who said he did not read him but loved him, will see in him “das lehrreichste Opfer des Christentums”, the most instructive victim of Christianity (*Ecce homo*, “Warum ich so klug bin”, § 3); Simone Weil will take up the idea of a God who withdraws.

The third is the wager, fragment 233, the most famous and the most misread. It is presented as a calculation of interest, and it is one; but the essential lies elsewhere. “Dieu est, ou il n'est pas”, God is, or he is not: reason cannot decide. Yet one cannot not choose: “il faut parier ; cela n'est pas volontaire, vous êtes embarqué”, you must wager; it is not optional, you are embarked. And when the interlocutor objects that he is so made that he cannot believe, Pascal gives him a method: do as those who believe do, “en prenant de l'eau bénite, en faisant dire des messes, etc. Naturellement même cela vous fera croire et vous abêtira”, taking holy water, having masses said, and so on. Quite naturally this will make you believe and will make you like the beasts.

This “vous abêtira” does not mean “will make you stupid” but “will make you like the beasts”, that is, will submit the machine, the automaton, habit, in place of proud reason. But it says something that all of modern critique will rediscover: belief is acquired through practice, and practice precedes belief. Durkheim will show that rite makes belief and not the reverse; Pascal, for his part, had prescribed it. One sees here how thin the line is between the apologist and the sociologist: the same proposition, that belief is born of habit, serves Pascal to convert and Hume to explain.

Pascal is therefore not a critic of religion in the sense that Spinoza is, but he radically separates the God of faith from the God of reason, and it is this separation that will make possible, on the one hand, the rational critique of theology (if the God of the philosophers is not the true God, he can be demolished without touching faith), and on the other, a religion of the heart that no longer owes anything to proofs. Kierkegaard, two centuries later, will take up his fight against an overly reasonable Christendom.

> **En littérature.** Charles Baudelaire, “Le Gouffre” (1862) ; Jean Racine, *Phèdre* (1677)
>
> “Pascal avait son gouffre, avec lui se mouvant”, Pascal had his abyss, moving with him: the first line of Baudelaire's “Le Gouffre” turns the anecdote of the abyss that Pascal is said to have seen open at his side into the very name of modern anguish, which now finds in the sky only a window open onto nothingness.
>
> Why here: Baudelaire keeps from Pascal the eternal silence of the infinite spaces and loses the God of Abraham who filled it. The abyss without the wager is what fragment 206 becomes when the heart no longer feels anything: the dread remains, the addressee is missing. The poem measures what the essay calls an exit from above, and what happens when the above empties.
>
> *Phèdre* is the tragedy of the hidden God as Lucien Goldmann read it in 1955, in the book that bears that title and that places Racine among the writers of Port-Royal: the dominant reading in its day, now disputed, and which I give for what it is. Phèdre, granddaughter of the Sun, lives under the gaze of a heaven that sees everything and never answers: “Soleil, je te viens voir pour la dernière fois !”, Sun, I come to see you for the last time! (I, 3); then, when the sin is consummated, “J'ai pour aïeul le père et le maître des dieux ; / Le ciel, tout l'univers est plein de mes aïeux : / Où me cacher ? Fuyons dans la nuit infernale”, my forebear is the father and master of the gods; / heaven, the whole universe is full of my forebears: / where can I hide? Let us flee into the infernal night (IV, 6). Grace does not come; the heroine is judged by a God who keeps silent. This is the world of the *Memorial* seen from the side of those who did not have the night of 23 November.
>
> Sources: Charles Baudelaire, “Le Gouffre”, *Les Fleurs du mal*, 1868 ed. (poem of 1862); Jean Racine, *Phèdre*, I, 3 and IV, 6 (1677); Lucien Goldmann, *The Hidden God* (*Le Dieu caché*, 1955), for the reading.
