### Plotinus (205-270): a mysticism without a Church

Plotinus is one of the few Ancients in this story who neither fought nor founded any religion, and that is precisely why he must appear here. Born in Egypt, a pupil of Ammonius Saccas in Alexandria, he taught in Rome from 244 onwards and did not begin to write until he was fifty. After his death his fifty-four treatises were arranged by his disciple Porphyry into six groups of nine, the *Enneads* (*Ἐννεάδες*, the “nines”), in a thematic order that is not the order of composition; Porphyry added a *Life of Plotinus*, which is our main source. The Plotinus of this *Life* “paraissait honteux d'avoir un corps”, seemed ashamed of having a body, refused to let anyone paint his portrait, and never spoke of his family or his homeland (*Life*, 1, trans. Bouillet). He was a teacher, not a founder; and yet he described with precision an experience of the absolute that owes nothing to a revelation, a Church or a cult.

The starting point is Platonic. Of the Good of the *Republic*, beyond essence, Plotinus makes the principle of everything, the One (τὸ ἕν, *to hen*), and he draws every consequence from the formula. If the One is beyond essence, it is also beyond thought: “ὅτι ἐπέκεινα οὐσίας, ἐπέκεινα καὶ ἐνεργείας καὶ ἐπέκεινα νοῦ καὶ νοήσεως”, because it is beyond essence, it is also beyond activity, beyond intellect and thought (I 7, 1). The distance from Aristotle is great: his first principle was a thought thinking itself; that of Plotinus is above thought, and does not even think itself: one treatise goes so far as to say that it is not absurd that it should not know itself (V 6, 6). Between the One and us come, in descending order, the Intellect (νοῦς, *nous*), which is Aristotelian thought demoted to second place, then the World Soul; everything proceeds from the One by superabundance, without decision or creation, as light proceeds from the sun.

But Neoplatonism is less a cosmology than a path of return. The soul that has scattered itself among bodies must climb back to its source, and the method of this ascent is a stripping away. At the end of treatise V 3, having shown that the One can be neither spoken nor thought, Plotinus asks how it can be seen, and answers with two words, perhaps the most famous in the whole of Western mysticism: “Πῶς ἂν οὖν τοῦτο γένοιτο; Ἄφελε πάντα”, how then could this come about? Take away everything (V 3, 17). One is not united with the absolute by adding a belief, a rite or a body of knowledge, but by removing everything that is not it.

The experience itself is described at the end of treatise VI 9, *On the Good or the One*, which Porphyry placed at the end of the whole work as its crowning. The one who has seen, says Plotinus, cannot say that he has seen, for there were no longer two, the seer and the seen: “ἐπεὶ τοίνυν δύο οὐκ ἦν, ἀλλʼ ἓν ἦν αὐτὸς ὁ ἰδὼν πρὸς τὸ ἑωραμένον, ὡς ἂν μὴ ἑωραμένον, ἀλλʼ ἡνωμένον”, since there were not two, but the one who saw was one with what was seen, as though he had not seen it but been united with it (VI 9, 11). Plotinus compares it to the mysteries, whose secret may not be divulged to the uninitiated: not because that would be sacrilege, but because the thing cannot be divulged. And the treatise, and so the *Enneads*, ends on the formula that sums everything up: the life of the gods and of godlike men is “ἀπαλλαγὴ τῶν ἄλλων τῶν τῆιδε, βίος ἀνήδονος τῶν τῆιδε, φυγὴ μόνου πρὸς μόνον”, release from the other things of this world, a life that takes no pleasure in the things of this world, flight of the alone to the alone (VI 9, 11). Bouillet's translation adds “Dieu” and “l'âme”, God and the soul, which the Greek does not name: a small example of the way the Christian tradition read Plotinus.

What is striking is the complete absence of religious apparatus in this experience. No revelation: the One does not speak, does not manifest itself in history, chooses no people. No grace: it is the soul that strips itself bare, through philosophy and virtue. No Church: the flight is “of the alone to the alone”. No cult, or rather a sovereign contempt for cult: Porphyry reports that Amelius, a disciple punctilious in sacrificing, wanted to take Plotinus to the festival of the new moon, and that Plotinus replied: “C'est à ces dieux de venir me chercher, et non pas à moi d'aller les trouver”, it is for those gods to come to me, not for me to go to them (*Life*, 10, trans. Bouillet); Porphyry admits that the disciples, not understanding this pride, did not dare to question him. All of this is nonetheless religious in James's sense: a solitary experience of what the subject holds to be divine. Porphyry gives the count with an archivist's precision: during the six years he lived with him, Plotinus “eut quatre fois le bonheur de toucher à ce but, non par simple puissance, mais par un acte réel et ineffable”, four times had the happiness of reaching this goal, not merely in potency but by a real and ineffable act, and Porphyry himself only once, at the age of sixty-eight (*Life*, 23, trans. Bouillet). The last words of the dying Plotinus, according to the same *Life*, were: “je m'efforce de réunir ce qu'il y a de divin en nous à ce qu'il y a de divin dans l'univers”, I am striving to unite what is divine in us with what is divine in the universe (*Life*, 2, trans. Bouillet).

It will be objected that this mysticism did not leave religion, since it had never entered it in the Christian sense; and that Plotinus is a pious pagan, who believed in the gods, in daemons, in providence. This is true, and it is what makes him precious: he shows that an experience of the absolute can exist without any of the structures that modern critique will attack. The sacrifice criterion does not apply to him. The One demands nothing: it is what the soul turns towards of its own accord when it ceases to scatter itself. It is he, far more than Epicurus, who is the distant ancestor of the spiritualities without God of the last part. And it is no accident that Pierre Hadot, before writing on the spiritual exercises of antiquity, devoted his first book of his own to Plotinus, *Plotin ou la simplicité du regard* (1963): he saw in him the model of an inner transformation that dispenses with every institution.

Plotinus had two posterities, which fought each other. The first is pagan and polemical. Porphyry, his editor, wrote a treatise *Against the Christians* in fifteen books, condemned to the flames by the Christian emperors and known to us only in fragments; and Plotinus himself composed a treatise against the Gnostics (II 9), which Porphyry entitles *Against the Gnostics*, in which he reproaches these sects of Christian origin for despising the world and its creator. Neoplatonism was thus, in the third century, the last great philosophy to oppose rising Christianity, and Julian the Apostate would try, in the following century, to make it the theology of a restored paganism: a refoundation, this time, in the strict sense.

The second, Christian, proved the more lasting. Augustine relates in the *Confessions* how, in Milan, he was supplied by a man “immanissimo typho turgidum”, swollen with monstrous pride, with “quosdam platonicorum libros ex graeca lingua in latinam versos”, certain books of the Platonists translated from Greek into Latin, and that he read in them, not in those words but in substance, that “in the beginning was the Word”; but that the Word was made flesh and dwelt among us, “non ibi legi”, I did not read that there (*Confessions*, VII, 9, 13-14). Augustine's sentence draws the frontier between Plotinian spirituality and the Christian religion: everything is there, except the incarnation, that is, except history, the Church and the salvation of a God made man. The “books of the Platonists” had been translated by Marius Victorinus, as Augustine himself says (*Confessions*, VIII, 2, 3); that they were treatises of Plotinus, of Porphyry or of both is the common opinion of historians, and the detail remains disputed. Through Augustine, then through Pseudo-Dionysius, Neoplatonic negative theology would pass into Christianity, and it is this that we shall find again, transformed, in Maimonides and in Eckhart, whose God beyond God owes as much to Plotinus as to the Gospels.

I therefore hold, against a widespread opinion, that Plotinus is not a precursor of Christianity, but the accomplished example of a spirituality that might never have become a religion. Christianity absorbed him by adding to him what Augustine “did not read there”; late paganism wanted to make him a state religion. Plotinus wanted neither. Take away everything: the formula holds for Churches too.

Before the Rhineland mystic, a detour by way of Córdoba and Cairo is necessary, and it is not an ecumenical courtesy. Our question presupposes an older one: can one think God as a philosopher within a religion of revealed Law, without betraying either the Law or philosophy? It was first posed, and more radically than in Latin Christianity, in Islam and in the Arabic-speaking Judaism of the twelfth century, by judges and court physicians who read Aristotle in the text handed down by the translators of Baghdad and had to account for their philosophy before jurists. It is largely through Averroes's commentaries that scholasticism read Aristotle. Averroes and Maimonides, born twelve years apart in the same city, do not leave religion, but they invent the two instruments that every later exit will need: the interpretation that separates the apparent meaning from the true meaning, and the division of human beings into those who can hear the second and those who must keep to the first.

### Averroes (1126-1198): the Law commands philosophy

Abū l-Walīd Muḥammad ibn Aḥmad ibn Rushd, whom the Latins would call Averroes and, more simply, “the Commentator”, was a judge before he was a philosopher, or rather he never ceased to be both. Grandson of a chief qadi of Córdoba, himself qadi in Seville and then in Córdoba, physician to the Almohad caliph in Marrakesh, he commented on almost the whole of Aristotle at the sovereign's request, before falling into disgrace in 1195, being exiled to Lucena and seeing his books burned, then being recalled shortly before his death. The text that concerns us, a short one, is entitled *Faṣl al-maqāl*, the “decisive discourse”, or more exactly the examination that settles the question of the relation between the religious Law and philosophy, and it dates from around 1179. Léon Gauthier translated it under the title *Accord de la religion et de la philosophie* (Algiers, 1905; the title page of the digitized copy bears 1906).

The form of the text is already a thesis: Averroes poses the question not as a philosopher but as a jurist, in the categories of Islamic law, which classify every action as obligatory, recommended, permitted, reprehensible or forbidden. The task, he writes, is to “examiner, au point de vue de la spéculation religieuse, si l'étude de la philosophie et des sciences logiques est permise ou défendue par la Loi religieuse, ou bien prescrite par elle soit à titre méritoire soit à titre obligatoire”, to examine, from the standpoint of religious inquiry, whether the study of philosophy and the logical sciences is permitted or forbidden by the religious Law, or else prescribed by it, either as meritorious or as obligatory. The answer is the strongest possible: philosophy is a duty. If philosophy is nothing but the study of the universe insofar as it makes its Maker known, and if the Law invites us to consider the universe, then philosophy is “de par la Loi religieuse, ou bien obligatoire ou bien méritoire”, by virtue of the religious Law, either obligatory or meritorious. Averroes quotes the Qur'an: “Draw a lesson [from this], O you who are endowed with understanding!” (sura LIX, 2), and reads in it a formal injunction to reason. Since the most perfect form of reasoning is demonstration (*burhān*) in the sense of the *Posterior Analytics*, the Law itself commands the study of Aristotle's logic.

The gesture is striking: philosophy has no need to leave religion, since religion orders it to exist. But it comes at a price, stated in the most famous sentence of the treatise: “la vérité ne saurait être contraire à la vérité : elle s'accorde avec elle et témoigne en sa faveur”, truth cannot be contrary to truth: it agrees with it and bears witness in its favour. If demonstration arrives at a knowledge that the apparent meaning (*ẓāhir*) of the revealed text contradicts, the text must be interpreted, that is, led back to its inner meaning (*bāṭin*) by *taʾwīl*, the interpretation that brings the word back to what it truly signifies. Demonstration never bends; the text does.

Then arises the question of the addressee: if the Qur'an has an apparent meaning and a true meaning, to whom should the second be told? Averroes answers with an anthropology of assent, which human beings do not give in the same way: some through demonstration, others through dialectical arguments, most through oratorical, that is, rhetorical, arguments. Hence the division: “Les gens [se divisent] donc, au point de vue de la Loi religieuse, en trois classes”, people are therefore divided, from the standpoint of the religious Law, into three classes: those who are “hommes de démonstration en aucune façon”, in no way people of demonstration, who “constituent la grande masse”, make up the great mass; the dialecticians; and “les hommes de démonstration par nature et par art, je veux dire l'art de la philosophie”, the people of demonstration by nature and by art, I mean the art of philosophy. The consequence is a rule of silence: the duty of the common people is to take the symbols “dans leur sens extérieur”, in their outward sense, and “il n'est pas permis qu'ils connaissent, en aucune façon, cette interprétation”, it is not permitted that they should know this interpretation in any way; more than that, “les interprétations vraies ne doivent pas être traitées dans les livres destinés au vulgaire, à plus forte raison les fausses”, true interpretations must not be treated in books intended for the common people, still less false ones. Averroes traces the sects of Islam back to this fault: the community split because interpretations were set out openly before the people.

This rule is aimed at a named adversary, al-Ghazālī (1058-1111), who, in the *Tahāfut al-falāsifa*, which Gauthier proposes to render as *L'Effondrement des falāsifa* (The Collapse of the falāsifa) rather than *La Destruction des philosophes* (The Destruction of the Philosophers), had declared al-Fārābī and Avicenna unbelievers on three points: the eternity of the world, the claim that God does not know particular things, and the interpretation of the resurrection of bodies. Averroes recalls this in the *Faṣl* and answers as a jurist: the charge of unbelief for having broken the consensus is only “hypothétique”, hypothetical, since no unanimous consensus can be established on questions that the ancients themselves reserved for “hommes d'une science profonde”, people of profound learning. The philosophical answer would come in the *Tahāfut al-tahāfut*, the “Collapse of the Collapse”, which turns the title of Ghazālī's book against him. This is an attested response: the first debate on the right to philosophize within a revealed religion sets two Muslims against each other, one of whom had been dead for nearly seventy years when the other replied to him.

Let us correct here a stubborn legend, which distorts everything that follows: Averroes never taught the “double truth”, the idea that a proposition could be true in philosophy and false in religion. He teaches the opposite: a single truth, two modes of access, a single arbiter, demonstration. The formula of the “double truth” comes from elsewhere: in 1277 the bishop of Paris, Étienne Tempier, condemning two hundred and nineteen propositions taught in the faculty of arts, reproaches certain masters in his prologue for saying things that are true according to philosophy but not according to the Catholic faith, as though there were two contrary truths. Historians still debate whether a single one of the “Latin Averroists” targeted, Siger of Brabant or Boethius of Dacia, ever upheld the thesis in that form; what is certain is that Averroes did not uphold it and that the label is polemical. Renan devoted his first major book, *Averroès et l'averroïsme* (1852), to this posterity, and it was he who made Ibn Rushd the patron saint of freethinkers. The Commentator deserved both better and less: he was not a freethinker, but a judge who thought freely.

Where should he be placed among our eight regimes? Not in the regime of error: religion is not false, it tells the truth in symbols. Nor outside critique, for the doctrine of the three classes is already, in theological form, a theory of the **social function** of religion. The Law is a medicine for the many: Averroes compares the lawgiver to the skilful physician who gives everyone precepts of health to which each can assent, because “il ne lui est pas possible de les rendre tous médecins”, it is not possible for him to make them all physicians; and whoever divulges the interpretations to the people resembles the man who would ruin the patients' confidence in the physician's prescriptions without being able to give them an understanding of them. Plato had wanted the city to impose a salutary theology; Aristotle had acknowledged, as we have seen, that the myths had been added to persuade the multitude. Averroes gives this tradition a more elegant form: for him, the religion of the people is not a useful lie, but the truth itself, clothed for those who cannot see it naked. He ends on an image that says everything: philosophy is “la compagne de la religion et sa sœur de lait”, the companion of religion and its foster sister, and harm that comes from a friend is more painful than harm that comes from an enemy.

The confrontation that follows is constructed. Spinoza did not, to my knowledge, discuss the *Faṣl*, which did not circulate in Latin. But when he attacks the method of Maimonides, which on this point is the same, he is aiming at the Averroist arrangement: if Scripture can be understood only in the light of demonstration, then the common people, who do not know what a demonstration is, “ne pourrait connaître l'Écriture sainte que sur l'autorité et le témoignage des philosophes”, could know Holy Scripture only on the authority and testimony of the philosophers, and one would have invented “une autorité fort nouvelle dans l'Église, une nouvelle espèce de prêtres et de pontifes”, a quite new authority in the Church, a new kind of priests and pontiffs (*Theological-Political Treatise*, ch. VII, trans. Saisset). The objection goes deep: the Averroist division does not lead out of religion, it installs at its summit a caste that knows what the text means and hides it. Averroes, at least, believes that the Law is true; but the structure will outlive the faith that justified it.

My judgement is therefore twofold. Averroes does not leave religion and founds nothing: he divides. He demands no sacrifice of the philosopher, who enjoys the truth without guilt since the Law itself commands it of him. But he demands of the people a sacrifice he does not name, that of access to the truth. By the criterion, he installs no new absolute; he ranks access to the old one. It is the most stable solution the question has ever received, and so it will return under every regime, in Spinoza himself, in Comte, in Renan, and even in the Straussian theory of esoteric writing.

> In Borges's story, collected in *El Aleph* (*The Aleph*), Averroes, in Córdoba, is commenting on Aristotle's *Poetics* and stumbles over two words whose meaning he cannot find in any library of Islam, “tragedy” and “comedy”.
