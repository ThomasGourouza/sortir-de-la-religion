### Bataille (1897-1962): experience without God, and atheology

Georges Bataille was a librarian at the Bibliothèque nationale; he converted to Catholicism in 1914 and spent a year at the seminary of Saint-Flour before entering the École des chartes in 1918, then became an atheist, an adversary and later a fellow traveller of surrealism, a founder of reviews and secret societies, and a passionate reader of Nietzsche at a time when Nietzsche was suspected in France of Nazism. During the war he undertook a *Summa Atheologica*: the title parodies the *Summa theologiae* of Thomas Aquinas and announces an enterprise of the same scope, but in the opposite direction. The project, left unfinished, comprises *Inner Experience* (1943), *Guilty* (1944) and *On Nietzsche* (1945).

The word “atheology” is thus Bataille's, and it matters for our story, since Michel Onfray would take it up in 2005 for his *Traité d'athéologie*. Onfray declares that he borrows it from Bataille's project. A theologian who reviewed the *Traité*, Emilio Brito, observes that Onfray borrows the word but does not use it in the same sense. The observation seems to me correct. For Onfray, atheology is a rational critique of the monotheisms, a dismantling of their lies. For Bataille, it is the search for an experience that has the form of mystical ecstasy but refers to no God.

The first volume of the *Summa* gives, a few pages after its opening, a definition: “J'appelle expérience un voyage au bout du possible de l'homme”, I call experience a journey to the end of what is possible for man. And Bataille had fixed its meaning from the first page: he has in mind “moins à l'expérience confessionnelle, à laquelle on a dû se tenir jusqu'ici, qu'à une expérience nue, libre d'attaches, même d'origine, à quelque confession que ce soit”, less the confessional experience to which one has had to keep until now than a naked experience, free of ties, even of origin, to any confession whatever. Inner experience is thus that of the mystics, the wrenching away, the ecstasy, the loss of self, but delivered from its confessional framework. It leads to no God, to no salvation, to no knowledge, but to non-knowledge, to night, to what Bataille calls the impossible.

The kinship with Weil has a history: the two met in the early 1930s, in Boris Souvarine's Cercle communiste démocratique and around his review *La Critique sociale*, and Bataille drew a barely veiled portrait of Weil in the figure of Lazare in *Blue of Noon*, finished in 1935 and published in 1957. Like Weil, Bataille wants an experience without any representation of God, and the loss of the self. But where Weil consents not to be so as to let God pass through, Bataille loses himself for the sake of losing himself, letting nothing pass through; where she seeks decreation, he seeks expenditure, transgression, excess. His sacred is not that of a love which withdraws, but that of the festival, of sacrifice, of eroticism and of death. It comes from Durkheim and Mauss, whom Bataille read closely, more than from Saint John of the Cross.

The response that follows is attested. In 1943, Jean-Paul Sartre published in the *Cahiers du Sud* a review of *Inner Experience* under an ironic title, “Un nouveau mystique” (“A New Mystic”). In it he reproaches Bataille for remaining a shamefaced Christian, a mystic who has lost God but keeps the posture of the believer. He writes in particular: “Dieu est mort : n'entendons pas par là qu'il n'existe pas, ni même qu'il n'existe plus. Il est mort : il nous parlait et il se tait, nous ne touchons plus que son cadavre”, God is dead: let us not take this to mean that he does not exist, nor even that he no longer exists. He is dead: he used to speak to us and he is silent, we now touch only his corpse. The sentence describes Bataille as Sartre sees him: a man who lives in mourning for God, who touches the corpse, and who turns this mourning into an experience. For Sartre, this is an imposture: one does not leave religion by prolonging indefinitely one's mourning for God. Bataille would reply in an appendix to *On Nietzsche* in 1945.

I believe that Sartre is right on one point and wrong on the essential one. He is right when he notes that Bataille keeps the whole structure of Christian mysticism: asceticism, the dark night, ecstasy, the language of the impossible. But Sartre is wrong when he concludes that it is an imposture. Bataille does not claim to have overcome religion through reason; he claims that the experience religion used to frame can be lived outside it, and that it is truer there. This is William James's thesis, turned towards night rather than towards light, and Sartre, for whom consciousness is always transparent to itself, could not hear it.

Bataille gave this experience a theory, in a book written around 1948 and published after his death, *Theory of Religion* (1973). I have read no extract from it; according to the publishers' presentations, religion is understood there as the search for a lost intimacy, the intimacy of the animal with the world, which labour, the tool and discursive thought have broken. If that is indeed the thesis, it places Bataille in a singular position: religion is neither an error, nor a symptom, nor an illusion of desire, but the memory of a continuity that man lost in becoming man. And the exit from religion is not progress; it is the completion of the rupture.

I hold Bataille to be a witness of capital importance, but not an exception. He does not refound a religion in the sense of Comte or the late Heidegger: he promises nothing, awaits nothing, organizes nothing. But he refounds a *sacred*, that of transgression, and this sacred has its rites, the festival, eroticism, sacrifice, and its officiants, the members of the secret society Acéphale which he founded in 1936 and which lasted until 1939. Bataille's atheology is not an exit from the religious; it is its radicalization outside God. It confirms, unexpectedly, the thesis of Durkheim set out in Part Eight: one can do without God, one cannot do without the sacred.

### Sartre (1905-1980): atheism as a starting point

Sartre is not a thinker of religion. He wrote no book on it, and his atheism is not the outcome of a critique but a premise. That is what makes him valuable here: he marks the moment when atheism ceases to be a thesis to be demonstrated and becomes a situation to be assumed. He is the hurried heir of the whole series, the one who takes for granted what Feuerbach, Marx and Nietzsche had conquered, and who asks what it costs.

The programmatic formula is found in the lecture given in Paris on 29 October 1945 and published the following year, *Existentialism Is a Humanism*: “L'existentialisme n'est pas autre chose qu'un effort pour tirer toutes les conséquences d'une position athée cohérente”, existentialism is nothing other than an effort to draw all the consequences of a coherent atheist position. The important word is “cohérente”, coherent. Sartre reproaches the atheists of the nineteenth century, and he expressly targets the “professeurs français” (French professors) who, “vers 1880” (around 1880), wanted to “constituer une morale laïque” (establish a secular morality), in short “tout ce qu'on appelle en France le radicalisme” (everything that in France is called radicalism), for having suppressed God while keeping everything else: a human nature, an a priori morality, values inscribed in an intelligible heaven. This is, in Sartrean terms, Stirner's objection to Feuerbach and Nietzsche's to the freethinkers, but formulated in the name of a consequence rather than a genealogy: if God does not exist, there is no human nature, since there is no God to conceive it; existence precedes essence; man is nothing other than what he makes of himself; he is condemned to be free, and without excuse.

It is in this context that Sartre invokes Dostoevsky, and one must be exact, for it was established above that the formula “if God does not exist, everything is permitted” does not appear in this proverbial form in the *Karamazovs*. Sartre writes, in *Existentialism Is a Humanism*: “Dostoïevsky avait écrit : ‘Si Dieu n'existait pas, tout serait permis.’ C'est là le point de départ de l'existentialisme”, Dostoevsky had written: “If God did not exist, everything would be permitted.” That is the starting point of existentialism. Sartre thus attributes to Dostoevsky a formula manufactured by the reception, and makes it the starting point of a philosophy. It is an exemplary case of what tradition makes a text say: Ivan puts forward a conditional thesis, which Smerdyakov takes seriously and which the novel puts to the test; Sartre makes it an axiom.

Yet the use Sartre makes of the formula matters more than the misattribution. For Ivan, and for the adversaries of atheism, “everything is permitted” is a threat: without God, no more morality. For Sartre, it is a liberation that is at the same time a burden: everything is permitted, so nothing is guaranteed, so I am responsible for everything. The absence of God does not abolish morality; it abolishes its guarantees, and lays on each choice the weight of a universal legislation. Sartre turns Ivan around as Bonhoeffer turned Feuerbach around: what was an objection becomes a task.

There is, finally, in *The Words* (1964), the autobiography of his childhood, a sentence saying that atheism “est une entreprise cruelle et de longue haleine”, is a cruel and long-drawn-out undertaking. The context is that of the writer who realizes that literature had been for him a salvation, a substitute religion, and who must rid himself of it. The admission carries weight: the most declared atheist of his time acknowledges that he refounded a religion, that of writing, and that it took him decades to notice.

Sartre is thus a witness for the prosecution of our thesis, and a lucid witness. He shows that the exit from religion is never secured, that it is an “entreprise”, an undertaking, and that it remains unfinished even in the man who proclaimed it finished. But I do not count him among the exceptions. His atheism is all of a piece, without spiritual remainder, and that is its limit: Sartre sees in religion only an escape from freedom, bad faith, and grants it nothing of what Marx still granted it, protest, nor of what James or Bergson granted it, experience. Ph. Cabestan observes that Sartre remains indifferent to the death of God as an *event*, unlike Heidegger. One can go further: for Sartre, the death of God is not an event, because God never lived. This is the position of the man of the marketplace in § 125 of *The Gay Science*, that of the unbelievers who laugh at the madman.

Heidegger, moreover, replied to the 1945 lecture in the *Letter on Humanism* (*Brief über den Humanismus*, written in 1946, published in 1947), rejecting the existentialism and the humanism attributed to him. The response is attested: Heidegger reproaches Sartre for remaining a metaphysician by simply inverting the order of essence and existence, the very reproach he had made to Nietzsche. Sartre's coherent atheism remains, in his eyes, within onto-theology: it has replaced God with human freedom as foundation.

>
> Sartre put his lecture on the stage, before and after delivering it. In *The Flies*, Orestes returns to Argos, where Jupiter reigns through remorse and Aegisthus governs through fear; he kills Aegisthus and Clytemnestra, then refuses Jupiter his repentance: the men of Argos are his men, he must open their eyes, and he leaves taking the flies, the Erinyes, far from the city. In *The Devil and the Good Lord*, Goetz, a warlord, wagers first on absolute evil, then on absolute good, founds a City of the Sun which is massacred, and discovers that God has never answered him: there are only men, he concludes, and he takes command of the rebel peasants.
>
> Why here: the two plays are the very movement of *Existentialism Is a Humanism*, and they show the remainder the essay points out. Orestes and Goetz leave God through an act, not through a demonstration, and they keep from religion the form of sacrifice: Orestes carries off the flies like a voluntary scapegoat, Goetz takes upon himself the evil of all. Sartre wanted to draw all the consequences of a coherent atheist position; his heroes draw them by taking up the role of the redeemer, and this is what the essay calls the religion of writing, avowed in *The Words*: an atheist who can think of freedom only as a passion.

### Camus (1913-1960): revolt and measure

Camus is the only author in this part who himself composed, before this essay, a genealogy of the exit from religion covering almost all our authors. *The Rebel* (1951) devotes chapters to Stirner, to Ivan Karamazov, to Nietzsche, to Marx, to Hegel, to Sade, to the regicides and deicides of the Revolution. Camus is therefore not only an author to be placed, but a competitor, whose answer to the same question must be discussed.

*The Myth of Sisyphus* (1942) set out from the absurd: the confrontation between the human call for meaning and the unreasonable silence of the world. Religion there is one of the temptations to be refused: that of the “saut”, the leap, for which Camus reproaches Kierkegaard and Shestov, and which consists in making the absurd itself a path towards God. Camus asks that one live in the absurd without leaving it either through suicide or through faith, and he ends on the image of Sisyphus going back down towards his rock: “La lutte elle-même vers les sommets suffit à remplir un cœur d'homme. Il faut imaginer Sisyphe heureux”, the struggle itself towards the heights is enough to fill a man's heart. One must imagine Sisyphus happy. The happiness of Sisyphus promises nothing, justifies nothing: it is, avant la lettre, a spirituality without consolation, in the sense in which Weil spoke of it in the very year Camus published this book.

*The Rebel* shifts the question from the absurd to revolt. The central formula is a cogito: “Je me révolte, donc nous sommes”, I rebel, therefore we are. Revolt is not a solitary act: it discovers, in the very refusal, a value common to all men, something in me that must not be violated and that is also in the other. And it has a metaphysical form: “La révolte métaphysique est le mouvement par lequel un homme se dresse contre sa condition et la création tout entière”, metaphysical revolt is the movement by which a man rises up against his condition and the whole of creation.

It is here that Camus meets Ivan Karamazov, and his reading joins that of Part Seven. Ivan, Camus writes, “inaugure l'entreprise essentielle de la révolte qui est de substituer au royaume de la grâce celui de la justice”, inaugurates the essential undertaking of revolt, which is to substitute for the kingdom of grace that of justice. Ivan does not deny God; he judges him, in the name of justice, and returns his ticket. But this revolt contains in germ its own perversion: if justice replaces grace, and if man is the sole judge, man may permit himself anything in the name of justice. Camus says it bluntly: “A ce ‘tout est permis’ commence vraiment l'histoire du nihilisme contemporain”, with this “everything is permitted” the history of contemporary nihilism truly begins.

The thesis of the book is therefore this. Metaphysical revolt, just in its principle, becomes criminal when it forgets its limit. It begins by contesting God in the name of man; it ends by divinizing man, then history, and by killing in the name of this new divinity. Camus describes the outcome of this drift through an enumeration: “Le cynisme, la divinisation de l'histoire et de la matière, la terreur individuelle ou le crime d'Etat”, cynicism, the divinization of history and of matter, individual terror or state crime. The divinization of history: that, in Camus, is the name of what this essay calls the refoundation of a religion. Marxism, for him, has not left religion; it has replaced the kingdom of God with the end of history, and reproduces, with the Inquisition removed and the camps added, the structure of collective salvation.

The encounters between Camus and our authors are attested by the book's very table of contents: one chapter is entitled “L'Unique” (“The Unique One”), on Stirner (p. 84), another “Nietzsche et le nihilisme” (“Nietzsche and Nihilism”, p. 88), others “Les régicides” (“The Regicides”, p. 143) and “Les déicides” (“The Deicides”, p. 168). Camus read Stirner, Nietzsche, Hegel, Marx, and Dostoevsky with a fervour that would lead him, in 1959, to adapt for the stage *Les Possédés*, the title under which *Demons* was then known in France. With them he builds a genealogy of nihilism that resembles, on many points, the one this essay has proposed: Stirner pushes revolt to absolute egoism; Nietzsche sees nihilism and wants to overcome it through the yes to life, but his doctrine would be misappropriated; Marx turns revolt into a religion of history.

Against this drift, Camus proposes a thought of the limit, which he calls “pensée de midi”, the thought of noon, in reference to the Mediterranean, to Greek light, to measure. The last part of the book closes on a sentence that condenses everything: “Au midi de la pensée, le révolté refuse ainsi la divinité pour partager les luttes et le destin communs”, at the noon of thought, the rebel thus refuses divinity in order to share the common struggles and destiny. To refuse divinity: not only that of God, but that which the rebel is tempted to attribute to himself. Measured revolt contests God without making itself God, refuses grace, but also history as supreme tribunal. It remains on a human scale, in solidarity with other men, without promise.

The book provoked one of the most violent quarrels of the postwar period. Francis Jeanson, in *Les Temps modernes*, published in 1952 a severe article, “Albert Camus ou l'âme révoltée” (“Albert Camus, or the Rebel Soul”); Camus replied with a letter to the review's director dated 30 June 1952, and Sartre in turn replied to him, which consummated the break between the two men. For Jeanson and Sartre, Camus, by refusing revolutionary violence in the name of a morality of measure, places himself outside history, in the purity of a beautiful soul, and his thought of noon is a disguised religious position: eternal morality against concrete history. For Camus, on the contrary, it is Sartre's thought, which accepts violence in the name of the end of history, that is religious: it sacrifices present men to future men, as the inquisitors sacrificed bodies to the salvation of souls.

I believe that Camus is right on the substance and Sartre on a point of method. On the substance, Camus saw, two years after *Meaning in History* and without depending on Löwith, that the philosophies of history refound an eschatology, and that this eschatology authorizes murder. He saw that the exit from religion, when it claims to found a collective salvation on history, is more murderous than the religion it leaves, because it no longer has a beyond to limit its demands here below. On method, Sartre is right to reproach him for judging history from above: the thought of noon has no institution, no politics, no grip on reality. It is an individual wisdom presenting itself as a collective answer.

For our question, Camus is a new serious candidate for exception, after Spinoza, Hume, Nietzsche and Wittgenstein, and alongside Weil. He does not refound a religion: he expressly refuses divinity, of God as of man, and he refuses consolation, since Sisyphus is happy without hope. Yet he keeps what resembles a spirituality: the beauty of the Mediterranean world, solidarity, Greek measure, lucid acceptance. Is that enough to say that he has left religion without founding another? I believe so, with one reservation. The “pensée de midi” is a myth, in the sense in which Camus himself likes the word, and this myth has its sacred geography, the Mediterranean, and its golden age, Greece. Camus divinizes neither man nor history; but he aestheticizes a nature and a culture, and this aestheticization is perhaps the most discreet form of refoundation. I leave the question open for the conclusion.
