### Spinoza (1632-1677): God is not someone

The *Tractatus theologico-politicus* (*Theological-Political Treatise*) appeared in 1670, anonymously, under a false publisher's imprint; the *Ethica* (*Ethics*) appeared only after Spinoza's death, in the *Opera posthuma* of 1677. This gap between what Spinoza published in his lifetime and what he held back matters: the TTP is a book of political combat, the *Ethics* a system. The critique of religion does not have the same function in the one and in the other, and confusing them produces the most widespread misreading of Spinoza, the one that makes him a masked atheist.

His fundamental thought is contained in a formula from the preface to the fourth part of the *Ethics*: “Deus seu Natura”, God, that is, Nature. The full sentence reads: “Ratio igitur, seu causa, cur Deus seu Natura agit, et cur existit, una eademque est”, the reason, or cause, why God, that is, Nature, acts and why he exists is one and the same. The formula does not abolish God; it displaces him. God is not a being separate from the world who wants something for himself; he is the single substance of which all things are modes, and he acts by the sole necessity of his nature. He therefore has neither design, nor anger, nor favour. What is false in religion is not God; it is the image of a God who resembles a king. One recognizes the gesture of Epicurus, but radicalized: the gods of Epicurus were indifferent, the God of Spinoza is impersonal.

The appendix to the first part dismantles the mechanism of this image. Men are born ignorant of the causes of things and conscious of their desires; they believe themselves free because they do not know what determines them, and they imagine that nature, like them, acts for ends. Hence finalism, and from finalism a God who supposedly arranged everything for man. When such a mind is pressed with questions about the cause of an accident, it goes back from cause to cause “donec ad Dei voluntatem, hoc est ignorantiæ asylum, confugeris”, until you take refuge in the will of God, that is, the asylum of ignorance. The will of God, in vulgar theology, is not an explanation: it is the name that explanation takes when it comes to a halt.

The TTP adds to this analysis an affective genesis and a political thesis. The preface opens on superstition: if men could manage all their affairs by a sure plan, or if fortune were always favourable to them, they would not be superstitious; but they waver between hope and fear. And Spinoza concludes: “The true cause of superstition, what preserves and sustains it, is therefore fear”. This is the thesis of Lucretius, transposed from physics to the psychology of the passions, and Hume will take it up. As for the political thesis, it lies in the radical separation of faith and philosophy. Philosophy aims at truth; faith “requires not so much truth in its doctrines as piety, that is, what moves the mind to obedience” (ch. XIV). Between the two, “there is no commerce and no affinity” (ch. XIV).

Here lies Spinoza's originality: he does not ask for religion to disappear. He asks that it cease to lay claim to truth and content itself with what it actually does: securing obedience to a few precepts of justice and charity. Religion becomes a social practice without theoretical content, which the State can regulate and which the philosopher has no need to combat. Spinoza therefore does not leave religion: he *puts it in its place*. Religion so placed closely resembles Cicero's *religio*, civic observance without metaphysics, and what Durkheim will call the social function of the sacred. Spinoza thinks of religion, after Polybius and Machiavelli but with another rigour, as a useful institution whose truth matters little.

For Spinoza keeps, for himself, the name of God, charged with a meaning the vulgar do not suspect, and here the question of spirituality changes the reading. The fifth part of the *Ethics* culminates in a notion that hasty readers treat as a rhetorical concession and that is the summit of the system: the intellectual love of God. “Mentis Amor intellectualis erga Deum est ipse Dei Amor, quo Deus se ipsum amat”, the mind's intellectual love of God is the very love with which God loves himself (V, prop. 36). This love expects nothing in return, since God loves no one in the human sense; it is the joy that accompanies the knowledge of things in their necessity, *sub specie aeternitatis*. It affords an experience that Spinoza does not hesitate to describe in terms of eternity: “At nihilominus sentimus experimurque, nos æternos esse”, and nevertheless we feel and experience that we are eternal (V, prop. 23, scholium). And it is not the reward of a virtuous life: “Beatitudo non est virtutis præmium, sed ipsa virtus”, blessedness is not the reward of virtue, but virtue itself (V, prop. 42).

This last proposition is Spinoza's deepest answer to religion, deeper than the critique of finalism. Every religion of salvation promises a reward: a paradise, an eternal life, a retribution. Spinoza abolishes the reward by interiorizing it: there is no other salvation than the present joy of understanding. This is what Foucault will call a spirituality: a transformation of the subject that gives it access to the true. And this is why the *Ethics* can end on a sentence that sounds like a word of wisdom rather than a theorem: “Sed omnia præclara tam difficilia quam rara sunt”, but all things excellent are as difficult as they are rare. The free man, the fourth part says again, “de nulla re minus quam de morte cogitat; et ejus sapientia non mortis, sed vitæ meditatio est”, thinks of nothing less than of death, and his wisdom is a meditation not on death but on life (IV, prop. 67).

The libertine tradition, and later part of Marxist criticism, read the retention of the name of God as prudence. I believe this reading is wrong. The God of the *Ethics* is not a pseudonym for Nature meant to deceive the censors; he is what allows Spinoza to think Nature as infinite, necessary and perfect, and to ground a blessedness upon it. What is preserved out of prudence is not the name of God; what is preserved, out of conviction, is the structure of the blessed life. Spinoza is a philosopher of the exit from superstition, not of the exit from the divine. Novalis, in a fragment published after his death by Friedrich Schlegel and Ludwig Tieck, called him “ein Gott-trunkener Mensch”, a God-intoxicated man (*Novalis Schriften*, vol. II, Berlin, 1802, p. 487).

This is precisely what his successors will hold against him, and their objections already form a debate. Hegel, in his lectures on the history of philosophy, will say that Spinozism is not an atheism but an acosmism, “Der Spinozismus ist also Akosmismus”, and that there is in him not too little but “zu viel Gott”, too much God (*Vorlesungen über die Geschichte der Philosophie*, Suhrkamp ed., vol. 20, p. 162-163): far from denying God, he denies the world, which is now no more than a set of modes without consistency. Feuerbach and the Hegelian left, conversely, will read Deus seu Natura as an elegant way of saying that only Nature exists. The real objection, the one that will tell, is a different one: Spinoza explains the illusion by ignorance and fear; he does not explain why man clings to it even when it is shown to him. He describes the error; he does not account for the *need* for the error. That need will be the object of Feuerbach, Marx and Freud. Nietzsche, finally, who will recognize in Spinoza a precursor, will also mock the “Hocuspocus von mathematischer Form” with which he armours his philosophy (*Jenseits von Gut und Böse*, § 5): the geometrical form itself will seem to him a mask of the will.

### Bayle (1647-1706): a society of atheists is possible

Pierre Bayle, a French Protestant who took refuge in Rotterdam in 1681 after the suppression of the Protestant Academy of Sedan, published in 1682 a *Lettre sur la comète*, which became the following year the *Pensées diverses sur la comète*. The pretext was the passage of the comet of 1680, which popular belief held to be an omen. Bayle first demonstrates that a comet portends nothing; then he arrives at an unexpected argument. If comets were omens sent by God to warn men, they could only strengthen the idolatry of the pagans, who interpreted them as signs from their gods. God would thus have worked a miracle to fortify idolatry. Now is idolatry, Bayle asks, less grave than atheism? And he answers, in the very title of a section: “Que l'Athéisme n'est pas un plus grand mal que l'Idolâtrie”, that atheism is not a greater evil than idolatry (§ CXIV).

The scandal was immediate, and it provoked an immediate attested response. The pastor Pierre Jurieu, a former friend turned enemy, attacked him precisely on this point; the modern editor quotes his complaint: “On lit le dangereux paradoxe, que l'Athéisme n'est pas un plus grand mal que l'idolâtrie”, one reads the dangerous paradox that atheism is not a greater evil than idolatry. What troubles Jurieu is not so much the theological proposition as its practical consequence: if the atheist is no worse than the idolater, why punish him?

Bayle goes further, and this is the heart of the matter: he maintains that men act not according to their principles but according to their passions, their temperament and their habits; that belief in God therefore does not prevent Christians from being as vicious as anyone else; and that, conversely, the absence of belief does not prevent one from being virtuous. Hence the conjecture, in § CLXI of the 1749 edition: as regards morals and civil actions, a society of atheists “seroit toute semblable à une société de Paiens. Il y faudroit à la vérité des loix fort severes, et fort bien exécutées pour la punition des criminels. Mais n'en faut-il pas par tout ?”, would be quite like a society of pagans; it would indeed need very severe laws, very well enforced, for the punishment of criminals; but are they not needed everywhere? And Bayle concludes with a sentence that would delight a sociologist: “la Justice humaine fait la vertu de la plus grande partie du monde”, human justice makes the virtue of the greater part of the world.

Bayle's argument is therefore not an apology for the atheist; it is a sociology before the term existed: men's virtue does not depend on their belief in immortality, it depends on laws, honour, opinion, temperament. Durkheim will draw the opposite consequence: if morality depends on society, and if religion is society worshipping itself, then religion did not die with God. The confrontation with the thesis that posterity attributed to Ivan Karamazov will be conducted in Part Seven.

Bayle was not an atheist but a sceptical Calvinist, convinced that reason is powerless to establish the truths of faith and that faith must therefore do without reason, which brings him close, by an entirely different path, to Pascal. His defence of the virtuous atheist is a defence of tolerance: if the atheist can be an honest man, the State has no reason to persecute him, and all the less reason to persecute the Protestant or the Jew. The exit from religion he sketches is not an exit from faith; it is an exit from religion as *political cement*. In this sense he is one of the first to think the exit from religion as the end of a structure of society, not of a belief held by individuals.

### Leibniz (1646-1716): why something rather than nothing

Leibniz wrote about God all his life, but the two texts in which his thought is summed up are two brief expositions from 1714, written in French during a stay in Vienna: the *Principes de la nature et de la grâce fondés en raison*, published in 1718 in *L'Europe savante*, and the *Monadology*, first published in German translation in 1720, whose French text appeared only in 1840. The only major philosophical work he published in his lifetime is of another kind: the *Theodicy: Essays on the Goodness of God, the Freedom of Man and the Origin of Evil*, published in Amsterdam in 1710 and born of a controversy with a dead man, Pierre Bayle, who had died four years earlier.

Leibniz's thought about God comes down to a question, the most famous in modern metaphysics. It is posed in § 7 of the *Principes de la nature et de la grâce*: “maintenant il faut s'élever à la métaphysique, en nous servant du grand principe, peu employé communément, qui porte que rien ne se fait sans raison suffisante ; c'est-à-dire que rien n'arrive sans qu'il soit possible à celui qui connaîtrait assez les choses de rendre une raison qui suffise pour déterminer pourquoi il en est ainsi, et non pas autrement. Ce principe posé, la première question qu'on a droit de faire sera : Pourquoi il y a plutôt quelque chose que rien ? Car le rien est plus simple et plus facile que quelque chose”, now we must rise to metaphysics, making use of the great principle, little employed as a rule, which holds that nothing happens without a sufficient reason; that is, that nothing happens without its being possible for one who knew things well enough to give a reason sufficient to determine why it is so and not otherwise. This principle laid down, the first question we have the right to ask will be: Why is there something rather than nothing? For nothing is simpler and easier than something.

Everything hinges on the “for” of the last sentence: nothingness needs no explanation, since it is the simplest state; it is existence that demands one. And this explanation cannot be found in the sequence of contingent things, for each refers to another, to infinity, without any of them accounting for the whole series. The *Monadology* formulates the principle (“aucun fait ne saurait se trouver vrai ou existant, aucune énonciation véritable, sans qu'il y ait une raison suffisante, pourquoi il en soit ainsi et non pas autrement”, no fact can be true or existent, no statement true, unless there is a sufficient reason why it is so and not otherwise, § 32) and draws the conclusion: “la dernière raison des choses doit être dans une substance nécessaire, dans laquelle le détail des changements ne soit qu'éminemment, comme dans la source, et c'est ce que nous appelons Dieu”, the ultimate reason of things must lie in a necessary substance, in which the detail of changes exists only eminently, as in its source, and this is what we call God (§ 38). It also takes up Descartes' argument and completes it: the ontological proof is valid only if one first shows that the idea of God is possible, that is, non-contradictory, and Leibniz concludes that “Dieu seul ou l'Être nécessaire a ce privilège, qu'il faut qu'il existe, s'il est possible”, God alone, or the necessary Being, has this privilege, that he must exist if he is possible (§ 45).

The next paragraph contains an attested response to Descartes, and it illuminates the whole difference between the two theologies. “Cependant il ne faut point s'imaginer avec quelques-uns, que les vérités éternelles, étant dépendantes de Dieu, sont arbitraires et dépendent de sa volonté, comme Descartes paraît l'avoir pris et puis M. Poiret”, yet one must not imagine, with some, that the eternal truths, being dependent on God, are arbitrary and depend on his will, as Descartes seems to have taken it, and after him M. Poiret (§ 46). Only contingent truths depend on God's choice; necessary truths depend on his understanding. Leibniz's God does not have the abyss of freedom of the Cartesian God: he is bound by logic, and he chooses according to reasons. He is reason itself become a person. One cannot go further in the rationalization of the divine, and that is why Heidegger, who in 1955-1956 devoted an entire lecture course to the principle of reason, published in 1957 under the title *Der Satz vom Grund* (*The Principle of Reason*), will see in Leibniz the thinker in whom onto-theology speaks its name most clearly: in it God is the sufficient reason of beings, the supreme being that grounds all the others.

This God-as-reason must have reasons for creating this world rather than another, and they can lie only in his goodness. Hence the thesis that made Leibniz famous and ridiculous. Among all possible worlds, God chose the best, for “s'il n'y avait pas le meilleur (optimum) parmi tous les mondes possibles, Dieu n'en aurait produit aucun”, if there were not a best (optimum) among all possible worlds, God would have produced none (*Theodicy*, I, § 8). Evil is not denied: it is classified, and its presence is justified by the whole. “On peut prendre le mal métaphysiquement, physiquement et moralement. Le mal métaphysique consiste dans la simple imperfection, le mal physique dans la souffrance, et le mal moral dans le péché”, evil may be taken metaphysically, physically and morally; metaphysical evil consists in mere imperfection, physical evil in suffering, and moral evil in sin (I, § 21). Metaphysical evil is inevitable, since every creature is finite; physical evil and moral evil are not necessary, but they are possible, and God permits them because the world that contains them is better, taken as a whole, than any world that would not contain them.

The *Theodicy* is an attested response, and its target is named from the preface onwards. Bayle, in his *Historical and Critical Dictionary*, had maintained that the Manichaeans' objections against the goodness of God were rationally unanswerable, and that one must therefore believe against reason. Leibniz replies that faith and reason cannot contradict each other, since two truths do not contradict each other, and he judges his adversary's position severely: “M. Bayle paraît l'avoir pris tout autrement : il se déclare contre la raison, lorsqu'il se pouvait contenter d'en blâmer l'abus”, M. Bayle seems to have taken it quite otherwise: he declares himself against reason, when he could have contented himself with censuring its abuse (“Discourse on the Conformity of Faith with Reason”, § 46). The debate on religion, at the dawn of the Enlightenment, thus does not run between believers and unbelievers: it runs between two believers, one who separates faith from reason to save faith, the other who unites them to save both. The same preliminary discourse counts among the adversaries of this union an Italian “secte de philosophes”, sect of philosophers, who were called “Averroïstes”, Averroists (§ 7), and we saw in Part One what was really the case with Averroes.

The meeting with Spinoza belongs to the same picture, and it calls for caution, for Leibniz himself recounted it in a way that minimized its significance. In November 1676, returning from Paris by way of London, Leibniz stopped at The Hague and visited Spinoza, who would die three months later. The *Theodicy*, thirty-four years afterwards, speaks of it in one offhand sentence: “Je vis M. de la Court, aussi bien que Spinosa, à mon retour de France par l'Angleterre et par la Hollande, et j'appris d'eux quelques bonnes anecdotes sur les affaires de ce temps-là”, I saw M. de la Court, as well as Spinoza, on my return from France by way of England and Holland, and I learned from them some good anecdotes about the affairs of that time (III, § 376). Political anecdotes, then; Leibniz's papers show something else: a note from the same period records that he presented Spinoza with his demonstration of the possibility of the supremely perfect being, and that Spinoza read it. What Leibniz thought at bottom of Spinozism, the preface to the *Theodicy* states bluntly: Spinoza holds that everything came from the first cause “par une nécessité aveugle et toute géométrique, sans que ce premier principe des choses soit capable de choix, de bonté et d'entendement”, by a blind and wholly geometrical necessity, without this first principle of things being capable of choice, goodness and understanding. The whole of Leibnizian theology can be read as the effort to save the God of reason from the God of necessity: to keep Spinoza's rigour, but to give back to God an understanding, a will and a goodness, that is, everything Spinoza had taken from him.

This God is not only a principle; he is also the object of a love, and Leibniz has his spirituality, which is that of the optimist. The *Principes de la nature et de la grâce* end with it: “On peut même dire que dès à présent l'amour de Dieu nous fait jouir d'un avant-goût de la félicité future”, one may even say that even now the love of God gives us a foretaste of future happiness; and this love, though disinterested, produces “une véritable tranquillité de l'esprit ; non pas comme les stoïciens résolus à une patience par force, mais par un contentement présent”, a true tranquillity of mind, not like the Stoics resolved upon a forced patience, but through a present contentment (§ 18). The difference from Stoicism is carefully marked: the Stoic resigns himself to the order, the Leibnizian approves it, because he knows the order is the best. Minds, § 15 says further, are members of the “cité de Dieu”, the city of God, governed by the best of monarchs, “où il n'y a point de crime sans châtiment, point de bonnes actions sans récompense proportionnée”, where there is no crime without punishment, no good action without proportionate reward. The same man spent years, in the last decade of the century, negotiating by letter with Bossuet a project for reuniting the Catholic and Protestant Churches: Leibniz's religion is a religion of reason and concord, which would have the confessions reconciled as truths agree.

The most famous response to Leibniz is not by a professional philosopher, and it is attested. After the Lisbon earthquake of 1 November 1755, Voltaire wrote a poem on the disaster, then, in 1759, *Candide, or Optimism*, whose tutor Pangloss teaches “la métaphysico-théologo-cosmolonigologie”, metaphysico-theologo-cosmolonigology, and proves “que, dans ce meilleur des mondes possibles, le château de monseigneur le baron était le plus beau des châteaux”, that in this best of all possible worlds, my lord the baron's castle was the finest of castles (ch. I). After the earthquake and the auto-da-fé that follows it, Candide, flogged and bleeding, wonders: “Si c'est ici le meilleur des mondes possibles, que sont donc les autres ?”, if this is the best of all possible worlds, what then are the others? (ch. VI). And the tale ends on the answer that dismisses metaphysics: “il faut cultiver notre jardin”, we must cultivate our garden (ch. XXX). Rousseau, in a letter to Voltaire of 1756 that we shall meet again, will on the contrary take up the defence of Providence.

Voltaire caricatures, but he touches a point that the caricature must not make us forget. Theodicy justifies each particular evil by the good of the whole. The criterion asks whether one installs an absolute in whose name the sacrifice of the individual can be demanded; now theodicy is, in the strict sense, a thought that accepts the sacrifice of the individual to the harmony of the whole, even if it is God, and not man, who decides it. Hegel will transpose theodicy into history, making world history the tribunal where individuals are judged and crushed so that Spirit may advance; and Ivan Karamazov, in Part Seven, will refuse the final harmony if it must be paid for with the tears of a single tortured child. This refusal, a constructed comparison with Leibniz, is the moral response to the *Theodicy*, as *Candide* is its ironic response. I believe it strikes truer: what is refused is not the best of worlds, it is the idea that an individual could be counted as a part of the best.

It would be a mistake to abandon the question of § 7 along with theodicy. Leibniz answered it with God; but the question can outlive the answer. Schelling will take it up in his lectures on the philosophy of revelation, Heidegger will make it the first question of his *Introduction to Metaphysics*, Bergson will hold it to be a false problem, and Wittgenstein will shift the wonder it expresses towards the very fact that the world is. The last part will gather them around this single question, perhaps the purest form of what may be called a spirituality without religion: wonder before existence, which asks for neither cult, nor dogma, nor sacrifice, and which Leibniz, the most rationalist of philosophers, formulated with the greatest clarity.

### Transition: from fear to morality

On the threshold of the Enlightenment, then, the critique of religion has its principal tools at its disposal, and they come from opposed traditions. From the Epicureans and from Spinoza it inherits the idea that religion is born of fear and of ignorance of causes, and that a knowledge of nature, lived as an exercise, affords a peace that religion promised without giving. From the mystics and from Pascal it inherits the idea that the God of representations and proofs is not the true God, and that the critique of the idol can be an act of faith. From Bayle it inherits the idea that morality does not depend on belief. From Leibniz it receives the barest question, why something rather than nothing, and the obligation to justify God in the face of evil, which Voltaire is about to turn against him. The eighteenth century will combine them in incompatible ways, and Kant, at the century's end, will keep only what is rational in them, that is, morality.
