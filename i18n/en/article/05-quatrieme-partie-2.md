### d'Holbach (1723-1789): nature against the gods

Paul Henri Thiry, baron d'Holbach, a German naturalized as French, host of a salon frequented by Diderot, Helvétius, Grimm and many others, published the *Système de la nature* in 1770 under the name of Mirabaud, a perpetual secretary of the Académie française who had died ten years earlier. The first great openly atheist book of European literature, it was condemned to be burned. Voltaire was alarmed by it and answered it in the article “Dieu, dieux” of the *Questions sur l'Encyclopédie* (fourth part, 1771), in the section “Du Système de la nature”, which opens with an admission: “L'auteur du Système de la nature a eu l'avantage de se faire lire des savants, des ignorants, des femmes”, the author of the System of Nature has had the advantage of being read by the learned, by the ignorant, by women.

The thesis is a thoroughgoing materialism. Nothing exists but matter in motion, governed by necessary laws; the soul is a function of the body; there is neither freedom, nor immortality, nor God. And religion is explained by ignorance of this nature: “c'est uniquement l'ignorance où l'on fut des causes naturelles et des forces de la nature qui donna la naissance aux dieux”, it was solely ignorance of natural causes and of the forces of nature that gave birth to the gods. Hence the formula that sums up the whole programme: “Si l'ignorance de la nature donna la naissance aux dieux, la connoissance de la nature est faite pour les détruire”, if ignorance of nature gave birth to the gods, knowledge of nature is made to destroy them (II, 1). This is Lucretius without Epicurean piety: the gods are no longer misrepresented, they are phantoms of ignorance.

On one point d'Holbach directly announces Feuerbach: he describes the mechanism by which man manufactures a god for himself. If he misunderstands nature and needs a god to move it, he is forced to make that god a man of whom he himself is the model; “il croit en faire un dieu en lui donnant ses propres qualités, il croit les rendre plus dignes du souverain monde, en les exagérant”, he believes he makes a god of him by giving him his own qualities, he believes he makes them worthier of the sovereign of the world by exaggerating them, whereas by dint of abstractions and exaggerations he makes them unintelligible (II, 7). Here, in 1770, is the theory of projection that Feuerbach will develop in 1841: God is man raised to infinity. The difference is that d'Holbach sees in it an absurdity, whereas Feuerbach will see in it an unrecognized truth.

And d'Holbach answers in advance the objection of Jurieu and of Ivan, as Bayle had done, but in terms of utility. The atheist is not a monster: he is “un homme qui connoit la nature et ses loix, qui connoit sa propre nature, qui sçait ce qu'elle lui impose”, a man who knows nature and its laws, who knows his own nature, who knows what it imposes on him, and his experience proves to him that vice harms him and that society is useful to his happiness (II, 12). Morality is founded on enlightened self-interest, not on the fear of hell.

The book then does something its militant readers pass over in silence. It ends with an *Abrégé du code de la nature* in which Nature herself speaks to exhort man; and the author, in a burst of fervour, exclaims: “O nature ! Souveraine de tous les êtres ! Et vous ses filles adorables, vertu, raison, vérité ! soyez à jamais nos seules Divinités ; c'est à vous que sont dus l'encens et les hommages de la terre”, O nature! Sovereign of all beings! And you, her adorable daughters, virtue, reason, truth! be for ever our only Divinities; to you are due the incense and the homage of the earth (II, 14).

So the book ends with a prayer. Nature is “sovereign”, her daughters are “adorable”, they are our “Divinities”, we owe them “the incense and the homage”. This is not a careless metaphor, it is the very structure of worship, transferred from God to Nature. D'Holbach leaves religion by founding a religion of Nature, just as the French Revolution would found, twenty years later, a cult of Reason and then of the Supreme Being, and as Comte would found a religion of Humanity. Here we have, before Stirner, the proof of what he would say of Feuerbach: our atheists are pious people (Part Six). The comparison is constructed, since Stirner was aiming at his contemporaries, but the formula applies to the baron even better than to them.

### Kant (1724-1804): religion within the boundaries of mere reason

*Die Religion innerhalb der Grenzen der bloßen Vernunft* (*Religion within the Boundaries of Mere Reason*) appeared in 1793, and an enlarged second edition in 1794. The book earned its author a rescript from the King of Prussia, Frederick William II, dated 1 October 1794 and signed by the minister Wöllner; Kant answered by promising, “als Ew. Königl. Maj. getreuester Unterthan”, as Your Royal Majesty's most faithful subject, to abstain entirely from any public discourse on religion, and he later explained that he had chosen this formula deliberately, so as to renounce his freedom of judgement only during the king's lifetime (preface to the *Streit der Fakultäten*, 1798). Kant's dealings with the censorship, from the moment the first chapters appeared in a journal, are recounted in the introduction to the Kehrbach edition. Kant is the culmination of the German Enlightenment and will be the main target of the following century.

Kant's principle is laid down at the very outset, in the preface to the first edition: morality does not need religion to found itself, since it rests on the autonomy of practical reason; but it leads necessarily to religion. “Moral also führt unumgänglich zur Religion, wodurch sie sich zur Idee eines machthabenden moralischen Gesetzgebers außer dem Menschen erweitert”, morality thus leads inevitably to religion, through which it extends itself to the idea of a powerful moral lawgiver outside the human being. For reason demands that virtue be crowned by happiness, and only a God can guarantee this agreement that nature does not guarantee. This is the doctrine of the postulates, set out in the *Critique of Practical Reason* (1788): God, freedom and immortality are not known, they are postulated as conditions of possibility of the highest good. Kant had written in the preface to the second edition of the *Critique of Pure Reason* (1787) that he had had to abolish knowledge in order to make room for faith: “Ich mußte also das Wissen aufheben, um zum Glauben Platz zu bekommen” (B XXX).

Hence the definition of religion, which is a complete reversal of the traditional order: “Religion ist (subjectiv betrachtet) das Erkenntniß aller unserer Pflichten als göttlicher Gebote”, religion is, subjectively considered, the recognition of all our duties as divine commands (fourth part, first section). The traditional Christian fulfils his duties because God commands them; the Kantian recognizes as divine duties he already knows through his reason. It is not religion that founds morality, it is morality that adorns itself with a divine name.

The book then takes up the great Christian dogmas one by one to give them a moral interpretation, and this translation is the model for all those that will follow, from Hegel to Feuerbach and on to Habermas. Original sin becomes “radical evil”, a propensity to evil inscribed in freedom itself, which Kant had first set out in an article of 1792 submitted to the Berlin censorship, as he recounts in a note on his “Censurleiden” published by his biographer Borowski and reproduced by the editor Kehrbach. This evil is not a corrupted nature transmitted by generation, in the manner of Augustine, but a timeless choice of freedom, which subordinates the moral law to self-love. Kant thus keeps from Christianity its darkest anthropology, the one the French Enlightenment had sought to dismiss: man is not naturally good.

Christ becomes an ideal of reason. “Das Ideal der Gott wohlgefälligen Menschheit”, the ideal of humanity well-pleasing to God, we can represent to ourselves only “unter der Idee eines Menschen”, under the idea of a human being who would not only fulfil all duties but spread good around him and accept every suffering, up to the most ignominious death, for the good of the world (second part). It matters little, Kant says in substance, whether this man existed: the ideal is in our reason, and it is the ideal that we recognize in the Gospel narrative, not the other way round. The whole nineteenth-century quarrel over the life of Jesus is here in germ. Strauss will make the Gospels myths produced by the collective consciousness; Bauer will go so far as to doubt his historicity; Onfray, at the end of this story, will make Jesus a pure concept. Kant had already made historicity indifferent, but for the opposite reason: not to discredit Christianity, but to save its moral core from the hazards of historical criticism.

Finally the Church becomes an ethical community. Kant calls “ein ethisches gemeines Wesen”, an ethical community, the society of human beings united under laws of virtue, as opposed to the juridical society united under laws of coercion; and he specifies that this concept “ist der Begriff von einem Volke Gottes unter ethischen Gesetzen”, is the concept of a people of God under ethical laws (third part). The invisible Church, that of all people of good will, is the truth of the visible Churches, which are merely historical approximations of it.

This point is almost always overlooked. Kant does not merely reduce religion to individual morality; he keeps its *communal* dimension, the one Durkheim will judge essential. The people of God under ethical laws is already, in the terms of 1793, the religion of Humanity that Comte will organize in 1852, with fewer rites and more rigour. It is also what Habermas, in 2001, will call the translation of religious contents into a secular language, and he will explicitly pay tribute to Kant as the first great example of a deconstruction of the truths of faith that secularizes them while saving them. Kant did not abolish the Church; he made it invisible, which is a way of making it universal.

This reduction has a critical consequence, which Kant draws with a severity that is forgotten: everything in historical religion that exceeds morality falls under suspicion. Kant distinguishes the *reiner Religionsglaube*, pure religious faith, common to all reasonable human beings, and the *Kirchenglaube*, ecclesiastical faith, founded on a historical revelation and on Scriptures, which is merely a vehicle. And ecclesiastical faith must be interpreted by pure faith: “Der Kirchenglaube hat zu seinem höchsten Ausleger den reinen Religionsglauben”, ecclesiastical faith has pure religious faith as its highest interpreter (third part, title of section VI). The history of religion is the history of a gradual passage from the first to the second, which is “die Annäherung des Reichs Gottes”, the approach of the kingdom of God (title of section VII).

The fourth part, the most violent, is entitled “Vom Dienst und Afterdienst unter der Herrschaft des guten Princips oder von Religion und Pfaffenthum”, on service and counterfeit service under the dominion of the good principle, or on religion and priestcraft. There Kant lays down a principle he declares evident: “Alles, was außer dem guten Lebenswandel, der Mensch noch thun zu können vermeint, um Gott wohlgefällig zu werden, ist bloßer Religionswahn und Afterdienst Gottes”, whatever, apart from a good way of life, the human being supposes he can still do to become well-pleasing to God is mere religious delusion and counterfeit service of God. Prayers, pilgrimages, fasts, rites, observances, if they are held to be meritorious in themselves, belong to *Fetischmachen*, the making of fetishes; and *Pfaffenthum*, clericalism, is the constitution of a Church “sofern in ihr ein Fetischdienst regiert”, insofar as a fetish service reigns in it.

Let us measure the critique contained in this text by a man brought up as a Pietist Lutheran: Kant turns almost all actual religious practice into fetishism. He applies to Christian worship the vocabulary of the fetish, which Marx, a reader of Charles de Brosses (*Du culte des dieux fétiches*, 1760), will rediscover to speak of the commodity. He anticipates Bergson's distinction between static and dynamic religion, and Barth's between religion and faith. And yet he does not leave religion: he keeps its core, God as moral lawgiver, immortality as hope, Christ as the ideal of humanity well-pleasing to God.

This is where the three objections of the following century strike. Schleiermacher, in 1799, will answer that religion is neither a knowledge nor a morality, but an intuition and a feeling, and that by reducing it to morality one has missed it entirely; the reply targets Kant without naming him, and it opens the regime of experience. Hegel will answer that the postulated God is an empty God, a *Sollen*, an ought-to-be that never is, and that true religion is the one in which the infinite is reconciled with the finite. And Nietzsche, much later, will formulate the hardest objection: Kantian morality has not left religion, it *is* the Christian religion, become invisible because it has disguised itself as reason. In *The Antichrist* (§ 10), he will say that German philosophy is “eine hinterlistige Theologie”, an underhanded theology, and that “Der Erfolg Kants ist bloß ein Theologen-Erfolg”, Kant's success is merely a theologian's success. The response is attested, though a century late. Kant believed he had reduced religion to morality; Nietzsche replies that he sacralized morality.

### A constructed debate: can morality do without God?

It remains to set face to face the answers of the Enlightenment to the question that will haunt Ivan Karamazov and that Bayle had already asked: if God does not exist, does morality still hold? The confrontation is constructed; each of these authors took a position, and their positions map out all the possible solutions.

Bayle answers that morality holds through society: laws, honour and opinion make the virtue of the greatest number, and a society of atheists would be like a society of pagans, provided the laws were severe. Hume answers, across the whole of his moral work, that morality holds through sentiment: sympathy, utility, the approval that agreeable and useful characters arouse in us, have no need of a divine lawgiver; religion, far from supporting morality, often corrupts it by substituting frivolous observances for it. D'Holbach answers that morality holds through enlightened self-interest: the atheist knows nature and knows that vice harms him. Voltaire answers that morality does not hold without God, at least for the people and for kings: there must be a judge, otherwise the scoundrel and the tyrant have no curb; and if this judge did not exist, it would be necessary to invent him. Kant, finally, answers that morality holds without God, since it rests on the autonomy of reason, but that it cannot *understand itself* without God, since it demands an agreement of virtue and happiness that only a God guarantees.

Ivan's question thus receives, as early as the eighteenth century, five answers, only one of which proves him right: Voltaire's. The other four maintain that morality survives God, because it rests on society (Bayle), on sentiment (Hume), on self-interest (d'Holbach) or on reason (Kant). But what does each put in God's place? Bayle puts human Justice, that is, the State; d'Holbach Nature, which he ends by invoking as a divinity; Kant Reason, which postulates God; Voltaire an invented God. Only Hume puts something that does not have the form of an absolute: a human sentiment, variable, fragile, and which promises nothing. That is why I take Hume, and not d'Holbach, to be the one who truly leaves religion in the Enlightenment: he is the only one who replaces God with no capital letter.

Dostoevsky, a century later, will know or retain only Voltaire's answer, turned against him: if God is necessary to morality, and if God does not exist, then everything is permitted. The other four will seem to him the tricks of philosophers.

### Transition: from reason to experience

The Enlightenment thus ran through every way of leaving religion by reason. Only Hume, having escaped into the obscure regions of philosophy, refounds nothing, at the price of a suspension that is hard to sustain. Voltaire kept God for his function; d'Holbach destroyed the gods only to end with a prayer to Nature; Kant reduced religion to morality and made morality a religion. The generation of Romanticism and German idealism draws a radical conclusion from this: if reason can neither found nor destroy religion, it is because religion is not a matter of reason. A matter of feeling, Schleiermacher will say; of the concept, Hegel will reply, but of a concept that contains feeling and goes beyond it. Between these two answers the modern critique of religion will be born.
