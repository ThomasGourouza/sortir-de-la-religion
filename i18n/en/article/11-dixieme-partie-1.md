## Part Ten: the twentieth century, God after metaphysics

Part Nine showed that explaining belief does not dissolve it; Part Eight had already rehabilitated, with Bergson, James and Otto, the mystic whom nineteenth-century critique regarded as a sick man, a dreamer or an exploiter. The question of this essay has thus shifted ground. We no longer ask only whether dogmas are true, what Churches are for, which desire beliefs satisfy. We ask what becomes of an *experience*, and whether it survives the ruin of the representations that long framed it: can one keep spirituality while losing religion, or religion while losing the God of the philosophers?

The twentieth century answers in a way that no critic of the previous century had foreseen. It does not merely produce atheists more consistent than Feuerbach. It produces, and this is the major fact, a *theological* critique of religion, and a *religious* critique of God. The atheists, for their part, take atheism as a starting point rather than a conclusion, and ask what it costs; others seek the divine outside being, or the mystical outside language.

This shift is decisive. Until now, the exit from religion was made *against* religion, and one could ask whether it did not refound another. From now on, it is also made *from within* religion, and one can ask the reverse question: is the critique of religion not, at least sometimes, the work of faith itself? If so, the simple opposition between believers and unbelievers, from which we were still starting, no longer holds. The dividing line runs elsewhere: between those who want an absolute that is available, possessed, usable, and those who renounce it, whether they pray or not.

### Heidegger (1889-1976): God and metaphysics die together

Heidegger's texts are protected by copyright, and I have been unable to check any of them against a printed edition: the quotations that follow are given from concordant secondary sources, with their provenance, or from an online reproduction of the German text; they will have to be verified against the *Gesamtausgabe* before any publication.

I begin with Heidegger, not because he is the most important, but because almost all the other authors in this part situate themselves in relation to the configuration he named *onto-theology*. The word does not designate an error of this or that philosopher, but the very structure of Western metaphysics since Plato and Aristotle, which comes down to a double question: what are beings as beings, what is common to everything that is, and what is the supreme being, the one that grounds all the others? The first question is ontological, the second theological, and metaphysics is both at once: it thinks being as ground, and the ultimate ground as a supreme being. What philosophers have called God is the name of this ground.

The essay “Nietzsches Wort "Gott ist tot"” (*Holzwege*, 1950), the substance of which, according to Heidegger's own note, was read several times in 1943 before small circles, drawing on the lectures on Nietzsche given at Freiburg between 1936 and 1940, turns Nietzsche against himself. The word “God”, in Nietzsche, does not designate only the Christian God, but the supersensible world in general, Ideas, ends, values. Hence: “Das Wort ›Gott ist tot‹ bedeutet: die übersinnliche Welt ist ohne wirkende Kraft. Sie spendet kein Leben. Die Metaphysik, d.h. für Nietzsche die abendländische Philosophie als Platonismus verstanden, ist zu Ende.” The death of God is not an episode in the history of religions; it is the end of Western metaphysics, which Nietzsche pronounces without seeing that he still belongs to it. To think beings as will to power and the world as values to be posited is to complete metaphysics, not to leave it. The reversal of Platonism remains Platonic: it puts life where Plato put the Idea, but still thinks in terms of hierarchy and value, and “value” is for Heidegger the last avatar of the Platonic Good, become a mere condition posited by the will for its own growth. The essay ends on a sentence that strikes the reason of the Enlightenment as much as faith: “Das Denken beginnt erst dann, wenn wir erfahren haben, daß die seit Jahrhunderten verherrlichte Vernunft die hartnäckigste Widersacherin des Denkens ist”, thinking begins only when we have experienced that reason, glorified for centuries, is the most stubborn adversary of thinking.

This reading strikes the whole series of Parts Six, Seven and Eight. If Heidegger is right, Feuerbach, Marx, Nietzsche and Freud did not leave religion, not because they kept a residue of piety, as Stirner maintained, but because they remained metaphysicians. Feuerbach replaces God with the human essence: he changes the ground, he does not renounce grounding. Marx replaces providence with the laws of history, Nietzsche the Idea with the will to power: the same structure. Freud replaces God with the Logos, and says so, as we have seen, with disarming frankness. Stirner's objection bore on the content of the absolute; Heidegger's bears on its form. One can empty the absolute of all theological content and remain within onto-theology, as long as one thinks the real on the basis of a first principle that accounts for it. The critique of religion by reason is, in this respect, the continuation of philosophical theology by other means.

The second text is the lecture “Die onto-theo-logische Verfassung der Metaphysik”, collected in *Identität und Differenz* (1957). The God of the philosophers is there the ultimate ground, and its proper name is *causa sui*. But such a god has nothing divine about it: “Zu diesem Gott kann der Mensch weder beten, noch kann er ihm opfern. Vor der Causa sui kann der Mensch weder aus Scheu ins Knie fallen, noch kann er vor diesem Gott musizieren und tanzen”, to this god man can neither pray nor offer sacrifice; before the *causa sui*, man can neither fall to his knees in awe, nor play music and dance before this god. And Heidegger adds that god-less thinking, “das gott-lose Denken, das den Gott der Philosophie, den Gott als Causa sui preisgeben muß”, which must give up the god of philosophy, is “dem göttlichen Gott vielleicht näher”, perhaps closer to the divine god.

The sentence says that philosophical atheism, the critique of the God-as-ground, is not opposed to religion: it perhaps clears the place for a god who would no longer be that of the philosophers. The critique of religion becomes, in Heidegger, a critique of *philosophical theology* in favour of waiting. As early as 1927, in “Phänomenologie und Theologie”, he separated the two domains radically: theology is “eine positive Wissenschaft”, a positive science, and “als solche daher von der Philosophie absolut verschieden”; faith is the mortal enemy, “Todfeind”, of philosophy, and a Christian philosophy is a “hölzernes Eisen”, a wooden iron, a square circle. The gesture recalls Spinoza, but reverses its meaning: Spinoza separated in order to free philosophy from theology, Heidegger separates in order to free the divine from philosophy.

Heidegger thus gives, three centuries later, a history and a reason to the opposition that Pascal's *Memorial* drew between the God one encounters and the God one demonstrates (Part Three); a constructed comparison, for I have not verified that Heidegger cites Pascal in this text.

A text from 1951, less well known and sharper, confirms this meaning. At a seminar held in Zurich on 6 November 1951, Heidegger answers students who question him on the relation between being and God. His answers, recorded in volume 15 of the *Gesamtausgabe*, settle a persistent misunderstanding: “Sein und Gott sind nicht identisch, und ich würde niemals versuchen, das Wesen Gottes durch das Sein zu denken”, being and God are not identical, and I would never attempt to think the essence of God by way of being. Then: “Wenn ich noch eine Theologie schreiben würde, wozu es mich manchmal reizt, dann dürfte in ihr das Wort »Sein« nicht vorkommen”, if I were still to write a theology, as I am sometimes tempted to do, the word “being” would not be allowed to appear in it. And finally: “Der Glaube hat das Denken des Seins nicht nötig”, faith has no need of the thinking of being.

These three sentences undo the lazy reading that makes “being” a substitute for God, an impersonal divinity for philosophers disappointed with Christianity. Being is not God, and faith has no need of it. The critique of onto-theology therefore does not aim to replace the God of the philosophers with being, which would be to refound a metaphysics; it aims to restore to faith its own source, which is not reason, and to thinking its own task, which is not to ground God. The young Heidegger had been a seminarian and a student of Catholic theology at Freiburg before writing to Engelbert Krebs, in January 1919, that epistemological insights had made “das System des Katholizismus problematisch und unannehmbar”, the system of Catholicism problematic and unacceptable to him, “nicht aber das Christentum und die Metaphysik”, but not Christianity and metaphysics; it is not insignificant that his most radical critique of metaphysics is formulated as a service rendered to the divine rather than as its liquidation.

Hence Heidegger's last public word on the question, the interview given to *Der Spiegel* in September 1966 to Rudolf Augstein and Georg Wolff, published at his wish after his death, in issue 23 of 31 May 1976, under the title “Nur noch ein Gott kann uns retten”, only a god can still save us. In W. J. Richardson's English translation, he declares that philosophy cannot bring about any immediate change in the state of the world, that the only possibility left to us is to prepare, through thinking and poetry, a readiness for the appearance of the god or for his absence in the decline.

This formula is made to say too much or too little. Too much, when one sees in it a late conversion, a return of the old thinker to the faith of his childhood: nothing in the interview names the Christian God, and the indefinite article, “ein Gott”, is deliberate. Too little, when one sees in it a quip: Heidegger says in earnest that neither technology, nor politics, nor philosophy can answer the distress of the age, and that thinking can only *prepare*. The structure is that of waiting. It is not unrelated to the *Gelassenheit* of the small volume bearing that title (1959), which brings together the Messkirch address of 1955 and a conversation written in 1944-1945: a letting-be, a serenity that renounces willing, and a word that Heidegger deliberately takes over from Meister Eckhart (*gelâzenheit*) while shifting its meaning, since it is no longer a matter of surrendering one's own will to the divine will but of leaving willing behind.

The objection is obvious, and I believe it is right. Heidegger saw that the critique of religion by reason remains caught in the metaphysics it believes it has overcome. But he draws from this a waiting without content, a piety without object, a religion without dogma or Church whose priest is the thinker and whose sacred text is Hölderlin. One does not leave religion by announcing a god to come: one founds one, a vague one, and perhaps a dangerous one, since it does not say what it is waiting for. The objection still needs to be made precise, otherwise it would fall under the reproach Heidegger levels at his critics, that of judging thinking by the yardstick of calculating reason. I do not reproach him for waiting, but for not saying *who* waits and in whose name. Pascal waited too, but he knew what he was waiting for, and his waiting was governed by a Scripture, a Church, a prayer. Spinoza waited for nothing, and his beatitude was actual. Heidegger waits without object or rule, and it is this indeterminacy that could, in 1933, allow itself to be filled by a historical object: one cannot pretend that the commitment of 1933 and the *Black Notebooks* do not exist, since they show what a thinking that waits for the saviour was able to recognize, once, in real history. A spirituality without content is not for all that without an absolute: it is an empty absolute, available to whoever wishes to occupy it. This is the lesson that Part Eleven will find again in Blumenberg, under the name of the reoccupation of positions.

Heidegger nevertheless established what no one in this part will call into question: the critique of the God of the philosophers is not the critique of God, and can be its opposite. The Protestant theologians of his time had understood this before him, and often better than he did.

### Wittgenstein (1889-1951): that the world is

Ludwig Wittgenstein never wrote a book on religion, nor even an article: a few pages of the *Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus*, a lecture of 1929 published after his death, notebooks, remarks on a book of anthropology, conversations reported by his students. Yet he occupies in our debate a place that no one else can hold: that of a man who closed, with unprecedented rigour, the possibility of saying anything whatever about God, and who left open, with the same rigour, the place of what cannot be said.

The *Tractatus*, written during the war, published in German in 1921 and in a bilingual edition in London in 1922, ends with a series of propositions whose sequence is the argument. First, the sense of the world cannot lie in the world: “Der Sinn der Welt muss ausserhalb ihrer liegen. In der Welt ist alles wie es ist und geschieht alles wie es geschieht; es gibt in ihr keinen Wert”, the sense of the world must lie outside it; in the world everything is as it is and everything happens as it happens; in it there is no value (6.41). Next, God does not show himself in the facts: “Wie die Welt ist, ist für das Höhere vollkommen gleichgültig. Gott offenbart sich nicht in der Welt”, how the world is, is completely indifferent for what is higher; God does not reveal himself in the world (6.432). Then the central proposition: “Nicht wie die Welt ist, ist das Mystische, sondern dass sie ist”, it is not how the world is that is the mystical, but that it is (6.44). And the conclusion: “Es gibt allerdings Unaussprechliches. Dies zeigt sich, es ist das Mystische”, there is indeed the inexpressible; it shows itself, it is the mystical (6.522).

It would be wrong to see in this a concession made to religion by a positivist. Wittgenstein is not Carnap, and the Vienna Circle was mistaken in taking him for an ally. The *Tractatus* does not say that what cannot be said does not exist; it says that what matters most cannot be said. “Wir fühlen, dass selbst, wenn alle möglichen wissenschaftlichen Fragen beantwortet sind, unsere Lebensprobleme noch gar nicht berührt sind”, we feel that even when all possible scientific questions have been answered, the problems of our life have not been touched at all (6.52). And the solution of the problem of life, adds 6.521, is seen in the vanishing of this problem: that is why, Wittgenstein asks, those to whom the sense of life became clear after long doubt could not say what this sense consisted in. The last proposition, “Wovon man nicht sprechen kann, darüber muss man schweigen”, what one cannot speak about, one must keep silent about (7), is not contempt, but a form of respect.

Two less cited propositions bear more directly on our question. The first concerns the modern view of the world: “Der ganzen modernen Weltanschauung liegt die Täuschung zugrunde, dass die sogenannten Naturgesetze die Erklärungen der Naturerscheinungen seien”, the whole modern view of the world rests on the illusion that the so-called laws of nature are the explanations of natural phenomena (6.371); “So bleiben sie bei den Naturgesetzen als bei etwas Unantastbarem stehen, wie die älteren bei Gott und dem Schicksal”, so the moderns stop at the laws of nature as at something inviolable, as the ancients did at God and fate (6.372). The thesis of this essay is here applied to science: the exit from religion through science installed a new inviolable in place of the old one. Freud, a few years later, would call it “our god Logos”. The comparison is constructed: Wittgenstein denies science the status of ultimate explanation that Freud granted it, and that is why he cannot put it in God's place.

The second concerns eternity: “Wenn man unter Ewigkeit nicht unendliche Zeitdauer, sondern Unzeitlichkeit versteht, dann lebt der ewig, der in der Gegenwart lebt”, if by eternity one understands not infinite temporal duration but timelessness, then he lives eternally who lives in the present (6.4311). The preceding sentence says that death is not an event of life, and the following one that the immortality of the soul would solve no riddle. One hears Epicurus, for whom death is nothing to us, and Spinoza, for whom we feel and experience that we are eternal: Wittgenstein rediscovers ancient wisdom and the fifth part of the *Ethics* by the most improbable path, that of logic.

The lecture on ethics, delivered at Cambridge in 1929 and published in 1965 in the *Philosophical Review*, gives this experience a content. To convey what he calls absolute value, Wittgenstein describes the experience that is for him the experience par excellence: “I believe the best way of describing it is to say that when I have it I wonder at the existence of the world”; and he adds that he is then inclined to use such phrases as “how extraordinary that anything should exist”. Further on, he redescribes it as seeing the world as a miracle, distinguishing the relative miracle, what science has not yet explained, from the absolute miracle; when we look at a fact scientifically, he says, everything miraculous about it disappears. But he adds at once that the expression of this wonder is nonsense, since one cannot wonder that a thing is without being able to imagine its not being, and one cannot imagine the world not being. The wonder is real, and its formulation is a misuse of language: it is this double affirmation, held to the end, that makes Wittgenstein singular.

Here appears the question that Part Twelve will take up: why is there something rather than nothing? Leibniz had posed it as the first question we have the right to ask; Schelling and Heidegger would take it up as the fundamental question of metaphysics. Wittgenstein receives it as a wonder, not as a question, and refuses to answer it, because there is nothing there to answer.

The war notebooks of 1914-1916 show that this silence was not indifference. In them Wittgenstein identifies God with the meaning of life, and prayer with thinking about this meaning; to believe in God is to see that the facts of the world are not everything. We also know, from the concordant testimony of his friends, that during the war he carried Tolstoy's *The Gospel in Brief* and recommended it to those around him. His student Maurice Drury reports that he said he was not a religious man, but could not help seeing every problem from a religious point of view. These are only recollections, but they agree with the *Tractatus*.

One last text concerns the anthropology of religion of Part Nine. In 1931, Wittgenstein read James Frazer's *The Golden Bough* and set down remarks on it that were published in 1967. He reproaches Frazer for explaining magical rites as errors, false hypotheses about causality, and turns the accusation around: it is Frazer who is primitive, more savage than most of his savages, since he cannot imagine a practice that is not a failed technique. The man who burns an effigy does not believe he is acting on the enemy as the doctor acts on a fever; he is expressing something, and the rite satisfies by expressing. This is a radical critique of the regime of error, from Lucretius to d'Holbach and Frazer: religion is not bad physics. Durkheim had seen this in his own way, making the rite the act by which a society represents its own strength to itself; Wittgenstein goes further, since he refuses even the functional explanation, which remains an explanation. Freud, who explained religion by infantile desire, would fall under the same reproach. The confrontation with Durkheim and Freud is constructed; the one with Frazer is attested.

Where should Wittgenstein be placed with regard to the criterion? He refounds nothing, and could refound nothing, since any doctrine about what is higher falls into nonsense: no cult, no morality, no community. But neither does he leave religion in the sense in which d'Holbach or Freud wanted to leave it, since he keeps intact, and even protects against science, the place of wonder, of meaning and of what shows itself. I regard him as a mystic without God and without doctrine, in the sense in which he uses the word: someone who experiences the world as a limited whole, wonders that it is, and falls silent. His position is stricter than that of Spinoza, who still spoke of God, and than that of Camus, who spoke of the absurd: Wittgenstein denies himself even the name. His silence is not an answer to the question of religion; it is the refusal to let an answer occupy the place of the question.
