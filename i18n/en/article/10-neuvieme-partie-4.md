### Why do people believe? A constructed debate

The answers gathered so far do not overlap, and they must now be brought to meet. None of the authors called upon had read all the others: most of these encounters are constructed confrontations, and I point out those that are attested. The question is simple to state and hard to settle: why have nearly all known human societies had gods, spirits, rites and sacred stories? I distinguish four families of answers, which appeared in a roughly chronological order: fear and desire, meaning, society, cognition.

The oldest answer is fear, from Lucretius and Hume to Freud, and then to Becker, who traces it back to its root: the fear of death, which man alone knows. Among these four authors, two lines of descent are attested: Hume discusses Lucretius in the *Natural History of Religion* (sections 5 and 12), and Becker starts explicitly from Freud; for the others, the kinship is my own doing. What unites them is a strong thesis: religion answers a threat, and would disappear with it.

Feuerbach gave this first answer a deeper version, because he speaks not only of fear but of desire. In his Heidelberg lectures, he grants that the thesis may seem paradoxical, the thesis that derives religion from human desires, and even holds the deity to be “als eins mit dem Wunsche”, one and the same thing as desire. He defines desire precisely: “Der Wunsch ist das Verlangen, daß Etwas sei, was nicht ist”, desire is the demand that something be which is not. And he draws from it the consequence that matters most to this essay. If the god owes his existence solely to the human drive for happiness, the *Glückseligkeitstrieb*, and if religion satisfies this drive only in imagination, then, Feuerbach writes, it necessarily follows “daß der Mensch auf andere Weise als religiöse, durch andere Mittel als religiöse diesen Trieb zu befriedigen sucht”, that man seeks to satisfy this drive in a way other than religious, by means other than religious. The sentence holds in germ the whole question of this essay: the need survives religion, and it looks for other objects. Feuerbach believed these objects would be nature, politics, the love of human beings; we know they can also be History, the Nation or the Race.

Marx shifted this first answer without leaving it. Religion, for him, is not only a fear or a desire, it is the lament of a real condition, the sigh of the oppressed creature (Part Six). The response to Feuerbach is attested: if religion is born of real distress, one does not dissolve it by explaining its psychological mechanism, one dissolves it by abolishing the distress. Norris and Inglehart, a hundred and sixty years later, gave this intuition a confirmation Marx would not have disowned, but which changes its meaning: it was not revolution that secularized Northern Europe, it was the welfare state.

The second family no longer speaks of fear but of meaning. Nietzsche formulated it at the end of the third essay of *On the Genealogy of Morality*. Man, he writes, does not deny suffering, he even seeks it out, provided he is shown its meaning: “Die Sinnlosigkeit des Leidens, nicht das Leiden, war der Fluch, der bisher über der Menschheit ausgebreitet lag”, the meaninglessness of suffering, not suffering itself, was the curse that until now lay spread over humanity. The ascetic ideal was accepted because it gave a meaning, any meaning at all: “irgendein Sinn ist besser als gar kein Sinn”, some meaning or other is better than no meaning at all; and man, Nietzsche concludes, would rather will nothingness than not will (Part Seven). Berger rediscovers this thesis as a sociologist in his theory of theodicy, Becker as a psychologist when he makes culture a hero system. The difference from the first family lies in the consequences. If religion answered only to fear, it would recede with security; if it answers the need for meaning, security is not enough, and a protected society can be a desperate one. According to Nietzsche, European nihilism was born not of poverty but of comfort.

The third family is sociological, and its chief representative is Durkheim: religion is neither a fear nor an individual search for meaning, it is the figurative form in which a society represents its own strength to itself. Bergson gave this thesis a biological version in *The Two Sources*: static religion was born of a “fonction fabulatrice”, a fabulating function, which nature placed in intelligence to keep it from dissolving social cohesion. Bergson's response to Durkheim is attested: he accepts the existence of collective representations, but refuses to see in them the essential, which he locates in the dynamic religion of the mystics. Berger, Luckmann and Hervieu-Léger extend this family into the twentieth century: religion provides a world, an identity, a lineage. Girard, finally, gives it its darkest version: society does not merely represent its strength to itself in the sacred, it protects itself there from its own violence by designating a victim; the question of whether one can leave religion without founding another then becomes the question of whether people can live together without a scapegoat.

The fourth family is the most recent, and it partly dissolves the question. For Boyer and the cognitive sciences, there is no religious need, only mental mechanisms shaped by evolution for other tasks. People do not believe because they are afraid, nor because they seek a meaning, nor because society demands it; they believe because gods are concepts that suit the way our mind works, and the other reasons, fear, meaning, society, merely select, among these easy concepts, the ones that will survive. This answer has two great merits: it explains the universality of religion without assuming some mysterious faculty in man, and it explains why arguments have almost no hold on it: one does not refute an inclination. Its limit, as I have said, is that it explains beliefs, not exercises; gods, not mystics.

These families did not merely follow one another, they clashed, and one attested exchange shows what is at stake better than any classification. In 1927, Romain Rolland responded to *The Future of an Illusion* by reproaching Freud for having explained the religion of the crowds without saying anything about its true source, a feeling of eternity, of the unbounded, of union with the whole, which he called oceanic; Freud answered him in 1930, in the first chapter of *Civilization and Its Discontents*, that he could not find this feeling in himself, and traced it back to the narcissism of the infant, while maintaining that the source of the religious need is infantile helplessness. The exchange sets the first family, that of fear and desire, against what the second would become in James and Bergson: a positive experience, which answers no threat and which no security makes disappear. Freud won the debate on instituted religions, which existential security makes recede as he predicted. Rolland perhaps won it on experience, since the most secure societies are also those in which the spiritual quests without a Church that Luckmann and Hervieu-Léger describe are multiplying. Both are right, because they are not talking about the same thing.

We must then return to the word need. A need, in the strict sense, is that whose deprivation causes harm: we need to eat, to sleep, to be loved. If religion were a need in this sense, the societies that have detached themselves from it ought to suffer for it in some visible way. Yet the Scandinavian societies, which Norris and Inglehart rank among the most secularized in the world and which Phil Zuckerman has observed at close quarters, show none of the symptoms one would expect of a lack: they are among the most trusting, the least violent and the most satisfied with their lot. The argument is not decisive: one could reply with Durkheim that they live on a secular sacred, equality, trust, the welfare state, which takes the place of religion; or with Nietzsche that their comfort is merely a nihilism unaware of itself. It is enough, however, to establish what seems to me the most solid conclusion of this debate: religion is not a need, in the sense of being irreplaceable; it is an answer, long the most readily available, to needs that can receive other answers.

One voice is still missing, that of a believer who had formulated as early as the seventeenth century the thesis this debate puts to the test. Pascal does not deny the mechanism that Lucretius, Hume and Becker describe; he described it himself. Diversion, he writes, comes from the fact that “tout le malheur des hommes vient d'une seule chose, qui est de ne savoir pas demeurer en repos, dans une chambre”, all the unhappiness of men comes from a single thing, which is not knowing how to stay quietly in a room; and the reason for this unhappiness is “le malheur naturel de notre condition faible et mortelle”, the natural unhappiness of our weak and mortal condition. But he draws from it the opposite conclusion to that of the explainers. Human dissatisfaction is for him not the cause of religion but its sign: it cries out, he says, that there was once in man a happiness of which there remains only “la marque et la trace toute vide”, the mark and the wholly empty trace, which he vainly tries to fill with everything around him, “parce que ce gouffre infini ne peut être rempli que par un objet infini et immuable, c'est-à-dire que par Dieu même”, because this infinite abyss can be filled only by an infinite and immutable object, that is to say, only by God himself. And he adds an observation that this essay confirms without sharing his conclusion: since man lost his true good, there is nothing in nature “qui n'ait été capable de lui en tenir la place”, that has not been capable of taking its place for him. The confrontation is constructed. Pascal and the refounders say the same thing, that the place of God does not remain empty; they interpret it in opposite directions. For Pascal, it proves that the place has the shape of God; for Feuerbach, it proves that God had the shape of human desire. Boyer, who denies that there is a place shaped like God, and Norris and Inglehart, who show that it shrinks with security, prove Pascal wrong on the interpretation. But they prove him right on the fact, which the exits from religion of the nineteenth and twentieth centuries never ceased to illustrate: what comes to occupy the place can be anything at all, and sometimes the worst.

What is to be done with these four families? Each discipline chooses its own: the psychoanalyst fear, the philosopher meaning, the sociologist society, the cognitive scientist the mind. On the contrary, I believe, they must be held together, for they do not answer the same question. The cognitive sciences say why religious beliefs are possible and easy. Fear, desire and the need for meaning say why they are desirable. Sociology says why they are transmitted and imposed. And the data of Norris and Inglehart say when they grow stronger and when they weaken. The disposition to believe is therefore universal in its springs, which are cognitive, existential and social, and variable in its intensity, which depends on existential security and on the solidity of lineages. It is real; but it is not a need for God. It is a need for meaning, for bonds and for protection, to which gods were long the easiest answer, because they were also the easiest to think.

This conclusion changes the question of this essay, but not in the expected direction. One might think that the natural explanation of belief settles the problem: if we know why we believe, we can stop believing, and the exit from religion becomes a matter of lucidity. That was the hope of Lucretius, Hume, Feuerbach and Freud. The history of our series shows that it was naive, and the analysis of the four families shows why. If the need is not a need for God, it survives God and looks for other objects. These may be substitute absolutes, History, the Nation, the Race, Civilization, which offer meaning, bonds and protection at the price of sacrificing those who do not belong to them: these are the refoundations. They may also be spiritualities without God, exercises, friendships, protective institutions, which offer the same thing without demanding any sacrifice: these are the successful exits, or at least the candidates. The natural explanation of belief therefore does not settle the essay's question, it sharpens it: the place religion occupied does not remain empty, and the whole problem is to know what will come to occupy it.

There remains what this debate contributes to the sacrifice criterion, and this is perhaps its main result. Becker and Girard, as we have seen, make sacrifice not an accident of certain religions but a spring of belief itself. If this is true, the refoundations are not perversions of the exit from religion, they are its natural drift, and the exits that do not refound are costly exceptions, which must be willed, built and maintained. Norris and Inglehart add a material condition: these exceptions are possible, on the scale of a society, only where existence is secure enough for fear no longer to look for a victim. Leaving religion without refoundation is therefore not only a matter of philosophy: it presupposes a wisdom, which the spiritualities of exercise offer, and a security, which only institutions can guarantee.

> Why here: Flaubert wrote the debate of this section in a single page. Emma believes out of lack, exactly as Feuerbach and Freud say, “à la place du bonheur”, in place of happiness; her devotion has the shape of her love affairs, with objects to kiss; it ends when Léon returns. But Father Bournisien is right to worry: this need, which is not a need for God, produces a religion that is not his, and he will later keep vigil over Emma's body with Homais, arguing. The essay's four families of explanation can all be found in the character, and none exhausts her, because Flaubert does not judge: he describes a need for meaning, for bonds and for protection that has found a reliquary.
