### Did Jesus exist? The Jesus of history and the mythicist thesis

The preceding sections followed the construction of a figure. Was there, behind it, a man? The question is a recent one. The Ancients argued over the nature of Christ, over his divinity, over the worth of his miracles; they did not doubt that a Jesus had been crucified under Pontius Pilate, and Celsus himself takes him for a magician, not for an invented character. Doubt about his existence arises in the eighteenth century, becomes radical in the nineteenth with Bruno Bauer, turns into a public movement at the beginning of the twentieth with Arthur Drews, and returns today, in France, through Michel Onfray.

I state my position at the outset, without false symmetry, as the state of knowledge requires. The historical existence of Jesus of Nazareth, a Jewish preacher from Galilee crucified in Jerusalem under Pontius Pilate, is the established consensus of historians of antiquity, believers, agnostics and atheists alike. The mythicist thesis, according to which Jesus never existed and was a god humanized after the fact, is a marginal thesis, held by almost no specialist in Christian origins. This is not a matter of opinion or of academic caution, but the result of an examination of the sources, which are few and convergent. The mythicist thesis nonetheless deserves a constructed debate rather than a summary dismissal, for it reveals, in its main defenders, something unexpected: the desire to found another religion.

**What the sources say.** The earliest witness is Paul, who did not know Jesus, and whose authentic letters, written in the 50s, tell almost nothing of his life, as the mythicists have always stressed. They nonetheless contain a few data that historians regard as decisive. Paul reports that in Jerusalem, three years after his conversion, he saw no other apostle but Peter, “εἰ μὴ Ἰάκωβον τὸν ἀδελφὸν τοῦ κυρίου”, except James, the brother of the Lord (Ga 1, 19). He says of Jesus that he was born “γενόμενον ἐκ γυναικός, γενόμενον ὑπὸ νόμον”, born of a woman, born under the Law (Ga 4, 4). And he passes on to the Corinthians a formula he himself “received”, whose origin critics agree in dating to the first years after the death of Jesus: “ὅτι Χριστὸς ἀπέθανεν ὑπὲρ τῶν ἁμαρτιῶν ἡμῶν κατὰ τὰς γραφάς, καὶ ὅτι ἐτάφη”, that Christ died for our sins according to the Scriptures, and that he was buried (1 Co 15, 3-4). A brother, a mother, the Law, a death, a burial: it is little, but it is a man.

The gospels come next, from Mark, c. 70, to John, at the end of the first century. They are texts of faith, composed for communities, and Strauss showed as early as 1835 how far legend shapes history in them. But their theological character no more proves the nonexistence of their hero than the legends of Alexander prove that of Alexander. And they contain elements that embarrassed the first Christians, which for the historian is a sign of early tradition: the baptism of Jesus by John, which implies subordination; his Galilean origin, from Nazareth, an obscure village that later had to be linked to Bethlehem; above all the crucifixion, an infamous punishment reserved for slaves and rebels, which no community would have invented for its Messiah.

The non-Christian sources are rare and disputed, but they exist. Flavius Josephus, a Jewish historian who wrote the *Jewish Antiquities* c. 93-94, mentions Jesus twice. The first passage, the *Testimonium Flavianum*, says in the form transmitted by the manuscripts: “Γίνεται δὲ κατὰ τοῦτον τὸν χρόνον Ἰησοῦς σοφὸς ἀνήρ, εἴγε ἄνδρα αὐτὸν λέγειν χρή”, there was about this time Jesus, a wise man, if indeed one ought to call him a man; then: “ὁ χριστὸς οὗτος ἦν”, this man was the Christ; and further that he appeared alive on the third day to those who had loved him (*AJ* XVIII, 63-64). No non-Christian Jew could have written “this man was the Christ”. The passage was therefore retouched by a Christian hand, and an external clue confirms it: Origen, in the third century, writes that Josephus did not believe that Jesus was the Christ, “not believing in Jesus as the Christ” (*Against Celsus* I, 47), which he could not have said had he read the text we read. The dominant interpretation sees in it a partial interpolation: an authentic core, which mentioned a wise man, a doer of astonishing works, crucified by Pilate, and Christian additions that turn it into a confession of faith. A minority of scholars regard the whole passage as interpolated; almost no one regards it as entirely authentic. The second passage is rarely contested. Josephus relates there that the high priest Ananus brought before the Sanhedrin, in 62, “τὸν ἀδελφὸν Ἰησοῦ τοῦ λεγομένου Χριστοῦ, Ἰάκωβος ὄνομα αὐτῷ”, the brother of Jesus who was called Christ, whose name was James, and had him stoned (*AJ* XX, 200). The “who was called Christ” is a nonbeliever's qualification, and the James of Josephus is the James of Paul.

Tacitus, finally, a Roman senator and a hostile historian, relates c. 116 how Nero, accused of the fire of Rome in 64, shifted the blame onto those the crowd called Christians, and he specifies the origin of the name: “auctor nominis eius Christus[…] per procuratorem Pontium Pilatum supplicio adfectus erat”, the author of that name, Christus, had been put to death by the procurator Pontius Pilate (*Annals* XV, 44). Tacitus, who despises the Christians, did not get this information from them; he gives it as a fact, with an administrative title, procurator, which is moreover inaccurate, Pilate having been prefect, which suggests that he is not copying an official document but reporting what was known in Rome. None of this makes a biography, but all of it suffices to establish, by the ordinary criteria of ancient history, that a man lived, taught and was executed.

**Reimarus, or the first quest.** The historical criticism of the life of Jesus begins with a book its author did not dare to publish. Hermann Samuel Reimarus, professor of oriental languages in Hamburg, who died in 1768, left a manuscript from which Lessing published extracts from 1774 to 1778 under the title *Fragmente eines Ungenannten* (*Fragments of an Anonymous Author*), the last and most explosive being *Von dem Zwecke Jesu und seiner Jünger* (*On the Aims of Jesus and His Disciples*, 1778). Reimarus does not doubt the existence of Jesus; he doubts his divinity and the good faith of his disciples. Jesus, in his view, was a political Jewish messiah who failed; the disappointed disciples stole the body and invented the resurrection and the return. This is still the Enlightenment thesis, that of imposture, which Strauss would ruin. But Albert Schweitzer, who wrote the history of this whole inquiry, sees in Reimarus the first to have understood the essential point. His work, he writes, is “perhaps the most splendid achievement in the whole course of the historical investigation of the life of Jesus, for he was the first to grasp the fact that the world of thought in which Jesus moved was essentially eschatological”.

Strauss would substitute myth for imposture, and Bruno Bauer, going further, would make the gospels the conscious work of an author and would come to doubt that Jesus had existed (Part Five). Schweitzer, who does not share Bauer's conclusion, pays him a rarely cited tribute: “For us the great men are not those who solved the problems, but those who discovered them. Bauer's "Criticism of the Gospel History" is worth a good dozen Lives of Jesus”. The phrase states the place history must give to radical doubt: it discovers real difficulties, and resolves them badly.

**Schweitzer, or the broken mirror.** Schweitzer's book, *Von Reimarus zu Wrede. Eine Geschichte der Leben-Jesu-Forschung* (*From Reimarus to Wrede: A History of Research on the Life of Jesus*, 1906, recast in 1913 under the title *Geschichte der Leben-Jesu-Forschung*), is one of the great works of criticism of the twentieth century, and it bears on our question more closely than one might think. Its first thesis is that each age has made Jesus into its own portrait. For the rationalists, he was the revealer of a virtue in conformity with reason; for the authors fascinated by the order of the Illuminati, the instrument of a secret society. “So fand jede folgende Epoche der Theologie ihre Gedanken in Jesus, und anders konnte sie ihn nicht beleben”, thus each successive age of theology found its own thoughts in Jesus, and it could bring him to life in no other way. And Schweitzer adds: “Es gibt kein persönlicheres historisches Unternehmen, als ein Leben-Jesu zu schreiben”, there is no historical undertaking more personal than writing a life of Jesus. One gives life to this figure, Schweitzer specifies, only by pouring into it all the hatred or all the love of which one is capable.

The second thesis bears directly on the mythicist debate. At the end of his inquiry, Schweitzer writes: “Es gibt nichts Negativeres als das Ergebnis der Leben-Jesu-Forschung”, there is nothing more negative than the result of research on the life of Jesus. And he specifies what has collapsed: “Der Jesus von Nazareth, der als Messias auftrat, die Sittlichkeit des Gottesreiches verkündete, das Himmelreich auf Erden gründete und starb, um seinem Werke die Weihe zu geben, hat nie existiert”, the Jesus of Nazareth who came forward as Messiah, proclaimed the ethics of the kingdom of God, founded the kingdom of heaven on earth and died to consecrate his work, never existed. “Er ist eine Gestalt, die vom Rationalismus entworfen, vom Liberalismus belebt und von der modernen Theologie mit geschichtlicher Wissenschaft überkleidet wurde”, he is a figure sketched by rationalism, brought to life by liberalism, and clothed by modern theology in historical scholarship.

A misreading must be avoided: what “never existed”, for Schweitzer, is not Jesus; it is the liberal Jesus, the moral teacher of cultivated Protestantism. The Jesus Schweitzer recovers is strange in quite another way: an apocalyptic prophet, who awaits the imminent end of the world and the coming of the kingdom, who believes himself called to hasten that end through his own suffering, and whom the end did not follow. “The historical Jesus will be to our time a stranger and an enigma”. Schweitzer thus did the opposite of what the mythicists would do: he destroyed the image and kept the man, a man all the more real for resembling us less. And his last page, one of the most famous in modern theology, turns the gesture of criticism into a call: “Als ein Unbekannter und Namenloser kommt er zu uns, wie er am Gestade des Sees an jene Männer, die nicht wußten wer er war, herantrat”, he comes to us as one unknown and without a name, as he came, by the shore of the lake, to those men who did not know who he was. Schweitzer would leave shortly afterwards for Lambaréné. His spirituality is no longer a Christology; it is an ethics of reverence for life: a case of faith that has passed through criticism, in the sense Ricoeur would give the phrase.

**The Christ of the cult.** The history of religions school, which dominated German exegesis at the beginning of the twentieth century, shifts the question. Wilhelm Bousset, in *Kyrios Christos* (1913), does not ask who Jesus was, but how he became the Lord. His answer lies in the title. “Kyrios Christos ist Jesus von Nazareth im wesentlichen als der im Kultus verehrte Herr seiner Gemeinde”, the *Kyrios Christos* is Jesus of Nazareth essentially as the Lord venerated in the cult of his community; and the book means to take its starting point everywhere “von der Praxis des Kultus und des Gemeindegottesdienstes”, from the practice of the cult and of communal worship. According to Bousset, the primitive Palestinian community did not yet know the title *Kyrios*; it arose in the Hellenistic communities, where Jesus was invoked as the faithful of the mystery cults invoked their saviour god. Later exegesis has much corrected this scheme, by showing that the Aramaic invocation *maranatha*, “Lord, come”, goes back to the Aramaic-speaking community. But the intuition remains, and it is the one this part has followed since Philo and Justin: the figure of Christ was built in the cult and in Greek thought, at the crossing of Jewish expectation and the Logos of the philosophers. This in no way requires that there was no man at the origin, only that one not confuse the man with the object of the cult.

Rudolf Bultmann draws a radical consequence from this, but not a mythicist one. In his *Jesus* (1926), he maintains that we can know almost nothing any more of the life and personality of Jesus, because the sources are not interested in them, and that this is of no importance for faith, which rests not on a historical portrait but on the *kerygma*, the proclamation of Christ crucified and risen. In 1941, in *Neues Testament und Mythologie*, he proposes to “demythologize” the New Testament: one cannot, he argues, use electric light and the radio, and resort to modern medicine, while believing in the New Testament world of spirits and miracles. The myth must therefore be translated into existential terms, as an address that sets man before his decision. The quarrel this programme provoked with Jaspers will come later (Part Twelve). What matters here is the paradox: one of the greatest Protestant exegetes of the century admits that the Jesus of history is almost unknown to us, and he does not doubt for a moment that he existed. Scepticism about the content does not entail scepticism about the existence.

**Drews, or myth in the service of a religion.** It is against this theology that Arthur Drews (1865-1935), professor of philosophy at Karlsruhe and a disciple of Eduard von Hartmann, published in 1909 *Die Christusmythe* (*The Christ Myth*). The book takes up the arguments of Bauer, of the Englishman John M. Robertson and of the American William Benjamin Smith: it makes Jesus a cult god of a pre-Christian Jewish sect, a mediator after the manner of Philo, a god who suffers and dies like the gods of the mysteries, whom the gospels then supposedly clothed in a story. The arguments were neither new nor sound, and a recent article on the controversy says so plainly. The book nonetheless became a public event. On 31 January and 1 February 1910, the Deutscher Monistenbund organized in Berlin, at the Zoological Garden, a public debate on Drews's thesis, and Drews then toured Germany from town to town.

One rarely reads why Drews was so keen that Jesus should not have existed; the preface says it bluntly. The book is addressed not to theologians but to cultivated people who feel the reduction of religion to a historical belief “als eine bedenkliche Verflachung und Veräußerlichung des religiösen Bewußtseins”, as a worrying flattening and externalization of religious consciousness; and the question of historicity, he adds, must necessarily lead to a discussion “des religiösen Problems der Gegenwart überhaupt”, of the religious problem of the present as a whole. The last chapter in fact bears this title: “Das religiöse Problem der Gegenwart”. And its conclusion reveals the programme. What Drews calls the truth of the Christian doctrine of salvation is a religion of the immanent god: “Das Leben der Welt als Gottes Leben; die leidvolle Entwicklung der Menschheit als göttliche Passionsgeschichte”, the life of the world as the life of God; the painful development of humanity as the story of the divine passion. And the obstacle to this religion is named: “Das Haupthindernis einer monistischen Religion und Weltanschauung aber ist der mit keiner Vernunft und Geschichte vereinbare Glaube an die geschichtliche Wirklichkeit einer "einzigartigen", vorbildlichen und unübertrefflichen Erlöserpersönlichkeit”, but the chief obstacle to a monist religion and worldview is the belief, compatible with no reason and no history, in the historical reality of a “unique”, exemplary and unsurpassable redeemer personality.

This is a textbook case for this essay, and I believe it has not been sufficiently noticed. The most famous of the German mythicists does not deny Jesus in order to leave religion; he denies him in order to found another religion, an idealist monism in which God suffers in every creature, which he wants to substitute for historical Christianity and which he offers to Haeckel's materialist monists to “reconcile” them with piety. The denial of the man is the condition of a new cult. Stirner's law (Part Six) is verified here in its purest form: the author of *The Christ Myth* is a founder of religion who needs Christ to be a myth so that his own god may have the place free. The comparison is constructed; it rests entirely on what Drews himself writes.

**Couchoud, Goguel and the French quarrel.** In France, the thesis was taken up in the 1920s by a physician and man of letters, Paul-Louis Couchoud (1879-1959), a friend of Anatole France, in *Le Mystère de Jésus* (1924). The book has the merit of clarity. “Jésus n'est pas un homme progressivement divinisé, mais un Dieu progressivement humanisé”, Jesus is not a man progressively divinized, but a God progressively humanized. And again: “Il n'est pas un fondateur religieux mais un Dieu nouveau.[…] Il n'est pas Mahomet, il est Allah”, he is not a religious founder but a new God.[…] He is not Muhammad, he is Allah. The conclusion of this chapter is fine prose: Jesus “n'est pas un personnage historique. Il n'a pas de place dans les générations des hommes. Il n'est pas un homme qui a vécu et qui s'est évanoui dans la mort. Il est un grand rêve des hommes, continuellement vivant”, is not a historical figure; he has no place in the generations of men; he is not a man who lived and vanished into death; he is a great dream of mankind, continually alive. Couchoud makes a prediction that shows the measure of his confidence: “Je crois que vers 1940 Jésus tout entier aura passé du plan des faits matériels dans celui des représentations mentales collectives”, I believe that by about 1940 the whole of Jesus will have passed from the plane of material facts to that of collective mental representations; and he calls Durkheim's sociology to his aid, since “L'histoire de Jésus est une représentation collective de nature sacrée”, the story of Jesus is a collective representation of a sacred nature.

The appeal to Durkheim turns against Couchoud. If the story of Jesus is a sacred collective representation, it can be so, in sound sociology, whether or not a man existed: Durkheim never asked whether the totem had lived. Couchoud confuses two questions, that of the nature of the Christ of believers, which is indeed a collective representation, and that of the existence of the Jesus of history, which is a matter for the sources. The answer came from a Protestant exegete, professor at the Faculté libre de théologie protestante in Paris, Maurice Goguel, in *Jésus de Nazareth : mythe ou histoire ?* (1925), and it is an attested response, addressed by name. Goguel begins by quoting a contributor to the very series Couchoud edited, Albert Houtin, for whom “Without Jesus, the history of Christianity would seem as inexplicable as that of Islam without Mahomet”; then he pays tribute to Couchoud's loyalty and sincerity, which does not prevent him from seeing in his book “the dream of a poet rather than the work of an historian”. Goguel then examines the mythicist arguments source by source and refutes them one by one: Paul's silence is not a silence, the “brother of the Lord” is not the title of a confraternity, the parallels with the gods of the mysteries are remote. Prosper Alfaric, a former priest turned militant rationalist, extended Couchoud's thesis in the 1930s; Michel Onfray prefaced in 2005 the reissue of his *Jésus a-t-il existé ?*. Couchoud had got the date wrong: in 1940, and since, Jesus has not left the plane of facts for that of representations; he has remained, for historians, a poorly known fact that produced an immense representation.

**Carrier, Ehrman and the consensus.** The mythicist thesis enjoyed a revival at the beginning of the twenty-first century, first on the internet, then in a scholarly form, with the American historian Richard Carrier. His *On the Historicity of Jesus* (Sheffield Phoenix Press, 2014), published by an academic house, submits the question to an explicit method, Bayesian probability calculus. Carrier argues there that the probability that Jesus existed cannot reasonably exceed one chance in three, and that with less favourable estimates it falls to one chance in twelve thousand. The most detailed review, that of Daniel Gullotta in the *Journal for the Study of the Historical Jesus* (15, 2017, p. 310-346), acknowledges, according to Carrier himself, the rigour of the undertaking, but judges the mythicist reconstruction implausible, and concludes that the reality of a crucified Jesus, “another failed messianic pretender from Palestine”, “is remarkably more likely than a demonic crucifixion in outer space”, which is Carrier's reconstruction. The irony hits the mark: to account for Paul's letters without a historical Jesus, Carrier must suppose that the first Christians believed in a Christ crucified by demons in a celestial sphere, a myth that the gospels then transposed onto earth. The hypothesis is not absurd in itself; it is simply more costly than the one it seeks to replace.

The most widely read response is that of Bart Ehrman, a New Testament scholar and declared agnostic, in *Did Jesus Exist?* (2012). Ehrman states the finding I make my own: “The view that Jesus existed is held by virtually every expert on the planet”. He makes clear that he has no stake in the matter: “I am not a Christian, and I have no interest in promoting a Christian cause or a Christian agenda”; and he concludes: “a dispassionate consideration of the case makes it quite plain: Jesus did exist”. Ehrman goes further, and here he must be read with reservation: those who deny the existence of Jesus, he writes, do so not because they have considered the evidence “with the dispassionate eye of the historian, but because they have some other agenda that this denial serves”. The argument is ad hominem, and it would be worthless if it replaced the examination of the sources, which Ehrman carries out elsewhere. But the case of Drews shows that it is not without historical foundation: mythicism has often been, from Bauer to Onfray, a weapon more than a hypothesis.

E. P. Sanders, in *Jesus and Judaism* (1985), draws up a list of facts he regards as practically indisputable: Jesus was baptized by John, he preached and healed in Galilee, he called disciples, he was involved in a conflict over the Temple, he was crucified in Jerusalem by the Roman authorities, and his disciples continued to form a movement after his death. John P. Meier, in the volumes of *A Marginal Jew* (1991-2016), and Paula Fredriksen, in *Jesus of Nazareth, King of the Jews* (1999), reconstruct an apocalyptic Jewish prophet, very close to Schweitzer's, whose crucifixion as “king of the Jews” is explained by Roman fear of unrest during Passover. This Jesus is neither that of the Churches nor that of the mythicists: he is a man of his time, whom his disciples understood, after his death, through the Scriptures, then through the Greek Logos.

**Onfray in the debate.** It is in this landscape that Michel Onfray's thesis must be placed. The *Traité d'athéologie* (2005) already replaced the question of the existence of Jesus with that of “cette construction nommée Jésus”, this construction named Jesus (p. 158). *Théorie de Jésus. Biographie d'une idée* (Bouquins, 2023) radicalizes this point. The thesis is stated bluntly on the back cover: “Jésus a bel et bien existé, mais comme un mythe qui convoque et cristallise les savoirs de l'époque”, Jesus did indeed exist, but as a myth that summons and crystallizes the knowledge of the age, and: “Jésus a bel et bien existé, mais comme concept. Cette Théorie de Jésus propose la biographie de cette immortelle idée”, Jesus did indeed exist, but as a concept; this Theory of Jesus offers the biography of that immortal idea. In an interview, Onfray is blunter still: “Il n'y a pas eu quelqu'un qui s'appelait Jésus”, there was no one called Jesus. The genealogy is the one we have just followed: Bruno Bauer, Arthur Drews, Paul-Louis Couchoud, Prosper Alfaric. The Dominican Renaud Silly, who has examined it, even judges that to call it, as Onfray does, “ultra-minoritaire chez les historiens”, ultra-minority among historians, “c'est complaisance, courtoisie ou euphémisme”, is indulgence, courtesy or euphemism.

One then sees what is original in Onfray's thesis and what is borrowed. It borrows from Couchoud the idea of a humanized God and the fine formula of the great dream; from Bauer and Drews the argument from Paul's silence; from Strauss, without saying so, the vocabulary of the idea: Strauss already made the human species, and not an individual, the subject of the predicates the Church attributes to Christ (Part Five). “Jesus as concept” is not his formula, but it is indeed his gesture. It owes nothing to the research that, from Schweitzer to Sanders, has established what can be known. And it illustrates precisely Schweitzer's first thesis: each age, each individual creates the Jesus he can love or hate. The liberal Jesus was a professor of morals; Onfray's Jesus is a concept, that is to say the object a philosopher can take apart.

**Why the question matters little, and why it matters.** This debate, which so excites public opinion, nonetheless counts for little in our question. Two of the greatest critics of Christianity did not doubt Jesus. Nietzsche, who makes him the only true Christian (Part Seven), takes him for a real man, and the Church for the betrayal of that man. Engels, who thought the historicity doubtful, held the question to be secondary, and said why. He recalled that Bauer had made Philo of Alexandria “der eigentliche Vater des Christentums” and the Stoic Seneca “sozusagen dessen Onkel”, the true father of Christianity and, so to speak, its uncle; but he at once added that philosophers do not found religions: “Religionen werden gestiftet von Leuten, die selbst ein religiöses Bedürfnis empfinden und Sinn haben für das religiöse Bedürfnis der Massen, und das ist in der Regel nicht der Fall bei Schulphilosophen”, religions are founded by people who themselves feel a religious need and have a sense of the religious need of the masses, and as a rule that is not the case with academic philosophers. And the text of 1894, already cited, saw in early Christianity a movement of the oppressed. What explains Christianity, for Engels, is not the existence or nonexistence of a man; it is the need to which it responded, and which the part devoted to the reasons for belief will examine.

The mythicist thesis here shows its true nature. It believes that Christianity would be refuted by proving that its founder did not exist, and thus remains within the first regime of critique, the regime of error, in its most naive form. Yet Feuerbach had shown, as early as 1841, that the truth of religion lies not in the accuracy of its narratives but in what it says about man; Nietzsche, that its strength lies in the values it installs; Durkheim, that its object is a collective representation, which lives independently of its origin. Whether or not Jesus existed changes nothing in the history followed from Philo to Augustine, that of a figure built by the faith of a community and thought through with the concepts of Greek philosophy: a figure that exceeds on every side what the historian can know of the man. My conclusion is therefore twofold. On historical ground, the debate is settled: Jesus existed, and the mythicist thesis does not have the standing of a competing hypothesis. On the ground of this essay, it is instructive: to deny the existence of Jesus is not to leave religion, and that denial served, in Drews, to found another. What matters for our question is not the man from Galilee, whom historians know poorly but do know; it is the way in which a crucified Jewish prophet became, in four centuries, the incarnate Logos of the philosophers. This transformation Celsus denounced as plagiarism, and Origen celebrated as a fulfilment. It is the true mystery of Jesus, and there is nothing mythical about it.
